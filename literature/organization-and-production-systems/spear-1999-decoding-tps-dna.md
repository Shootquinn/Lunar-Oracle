# Decoding the DNA of the Toyota Production System

## Abstract

Reports findings from a four-year field study of more than 40 manufacturing and service-support
operations in the United States, Europe, and Japan, undertaken to identify the implicit rules
underlying the Toyota Production System (TPS). Argues that TPS's visible tools and practices
(kanban cards, andon cords, quality circles) are surface artifacts rather than the system itself,
and that outside imitators fail because they copy these artifacts without adopting the four
unwritten rules that govern how work, connections, flow, and improvement are designed. Frames
each rule as embedding a testable hypothesis, so that every activity, hand-off, and pathway
functions as a standing scientific-method experiment, taught to workers through Socratic
questioning rather than written procedure. Method is qualitative and observational: plant visits,
interviews, and one author's five-month embedded participation on a Toyota Supplier Support
Center team.

## Summary

### Background and objective

TPS has long been credited as the source of Toyota's manufacturing performance, and its
component practices — kanban cards, quality circles, andon cords — have been widely observed and
copied. General Motors, Ford, and Chrysler each independently built Toyota-style production
system initiatives after benchmarking Toyota, and firms in aerospace, consumer products, metals
processing, and industrial products have attempted similar imitation. The authors note that
despite Toyota's openness — hundreds of thousands of executives have toured its plants in Japan
and the United States — few imitators have matched Toyota's results.

The authors reject the common explanation that Toyota's advantage is rooted in Japanese culture,
pointing out that other Japanese manufacturers (Nissan, Honda) have not matched Toyota's
performance, while Toyota has successfully exported its system to North America, where it was
that year building over a million cars, minivans, and light trucks. Their alternative
explanation: visitors confuse the tools and practices observed on plant tours with the system
itself, which prevents them from resolving TPS's apparent paradox — that activity, connections,
and flow are rigidly scripted, yet the operation as a whole is flexible and constantly
self-improving. The article's stated objective is to make this implicit design logic explicit by
describing four rules — three rules of design and one rule of improvement — that the authors
present as the "DNA" of the system, in contrast to the specific practices and tools observers
typically notice on plant visits.

The reprint packages the article with two auxiliary front-matter sections, "The Idea in Brief" and
"The Idea in Practice," which condense the same four rules and give abbreviated versions of the
seat-installation and changeover-time examples that the body text develops at greater length; a
"Further Reading" section lists three related HBR articles by other authors (on managerial
training at Toyota, on Toyota's product-development system, and on applying lean principles to a
financial-services back office).

### Methods and scope

The findings are drawn from a four-year study examining the internal operations of more than 40
plants in the United States, Europe, and Japan, some run according to TPS and some not, used as a
comparison set. The study covered both process and discrete manufacturers, with products ranging
from prefabricated housing, auto parts, and final auto assembly to cell phones, computer
printers, injection-molded plastics, and aluminum extrusions. Scope extended beyond routine
production work to service functions: equipment maintenance, worker training and supervision,
logistics and materials handling, and process design and redesign.

The research method was observational and interview-based rather than statistical: watching
people perform jobs, and participating in the design of production processes, alongside
interviews with workers, supervisors, and managers. A substantial portion of the material is
attributed to one author's five-month tenure as a member of a Toyota Supplier Support Center
(TSSC) team, embedded at a plant that supplied Toyota and two other auto assemblers while
promoting TPS on-site. The article does not report a sampling frame, selection criteria for the
40-plus sites, or any quantitative comparison across the full sample; illustrative examples
(Georgetown, Kentucky; Aisin Seiki; Kamigo) are used to substantiate each rule rather than
aggregate statistics across the sample.

### Key findings

**The four rules.** The article's central claim is that TPS rests on four unwritten rules,
summarized in the sidebar "The Four Rules":

- Rule 1 (how people work): all work shall be highly specified as to content, sequence, timing,
  and outcome.
- Rule 2 (how people connect): every customer-supplier connection must be direct, with an
  unambiguous yes-or-no way to send requests and receive responses.
- Rule 3 (how the line is built): the pathway for every product and service must be simple and
  direct.
- Rule 4 (how to improve): any improvement must be made in accordance with the scientific method,
  under the guidance of a teacher, at the lowest possible level in the organization.

All four rules are described as building in automatic tests that signal problems, and it is the
continual response to those signals — not the rules' rigidity as such — that the authors credit
with making the system adaptable rather than a command-and-control bureaucracy.

**Rule 1 examples.** At Toyota's Georgetown, Kentucky plant, installing the right-front seat into
a Camry is designed as seven tasks completed within 55 seconds while the car moves through a
worker's zone at fixed speed; the work-area floor is marked in tenths so that, for example, a
worker still on task 4 at the sixth of ten marks (33 seconds elapsed) is immediately known to be
behind schedule. At a Toyota supplier in Japan, moving equipment to create a new production line
was broken into 14 separate activities, each subdivided into tasks with a specific person
assigned to each task in a specified sequence, so discrepancies between planned and actual
execution were signaled immediately during the move.

**Rule 2 mechanism.** Requests for parts are typically made via a kanban — a laminated card
specifying the part's identification number, the quantity per container, and the locations of
both supplier and customer. A worker requiring help must ask for it at once, and the designated
assistant is expected to resolve the problem within the requester's own cycle time (for example,
under 55 seconds for the seat installer); failure to do so is itself treated as a signal that a
hypothesis embedded in the connection — about request clarity, assistant workload, or assistant
capability — has been refuted.

The authors describe this immediate-request norm as counterintuitive to managers accustomed to
encouraging workers to resolve problems on their own before calling for help. Under that more
common approach, they argue, problems stay hidden and unshared companywide, and workers who
decide for themselves when a problem is "big enough" to escalate cause information about root
causes to be lost by the time help finally arrives; direct, standardized connections are presented
as the corrective to that pattern.

**Rule 3 example.** At an unnamed auto parts supplier, parts had previously been routed to
whichever press or welding station was next available. Under Toyota's guidance, the plant was
redesigned so each part type followed exactly one specified production path; if workers found
themselves wanting to divert to another machine or seek help outside their designated helper,
that mismatch between expected and actual demand or capacity triggered a redesign of the flow
path rather than an ad hoc reroute.

**Rule 4 / Aisin Seiki mattress case.** Aisin Seiki, a Toyota Group company making auto power
trains, opened a mattress line in 1986 to absorb excess plant capacity. Per the exhibit
"On-Demand Production at the Aisin Mattress Factory," between 1986 and 1997 the number of
mattress styles grew from 200 to 850, daily unit volume from 160 to 550, units produced per
person from 8 to 26, a productivity index from 100 to 208, finished-goods inventory fell from 30
days to 1.5 days, and the number of assembly lines moved from 2 up to 3 (to cope with the growth
in product types) and back down to 2. A mattress assembly team, after being assigned a leader who
taught them to frame problems and formulate and test hypotheses, redesigned how edging tape was
attached to mattresses and cut the associated defect rate by 90%.

**Rule 4 / Ohba changeover case.** Hajime Ohba, general manager of the Toyota Supplier Support
Center, evaluated a group that had reduced a machine changeover time from 15 minutes toward a
stated goal of 5 minutes, achieving 7.5 minutes (a 50% reduction). Ohba's follow-up questions —
including whether four bolts were truly necessary or the job could be done with two — were meant
to show that the 5-minute figure had been an unexamined goal rather than a hypothesis derived
from a specific proposed change, so the team had not run a genuine experiment ("if we make the
following specific changes, we expect to achieve this specific outcome") and could not test what
had caused the shortfall.

**The experiments framework.** The exhibit "The Experiments of the Toyota Production System"
tabulates, for each rule, the embedded hypotheses, the signs that a hypothesis has been refuted,
and the prescribed response. For Rule 1, the hypotheses are that a person or machine can perform
the activity as specified and that doing so yields a defect-free result; problem signs are the
activity not being done as specified or the outcome being defective; the response is to determine
the person's true skill or the machine's true capability and train, modify, or redesign
accordingly. Analogous hypothesis/signal/response triads are given for Rules 2 through 4,
covering demand-capacity mismatches, unnecessary or missing suppliers within a flow path, and
improvement changes whose actual effect diverges from the predicted one.

**Teaching method.** The rules are described as never having been formally written down inside
Toyota; they are transmitted through a Socratic, iterative process rather than documented
procedure. The sidebar "How Toyota's Workers Learn the Rules" gives the standard supervisor
questions used at the work site: "How do you do this work?" "How do you know you are doing this
work correctly?" "How do you know that the outcome is free of defects?" "What do you do if you
have a problem?" The authors state that successful transfer of TPS outside Toyota has so far
occurred only when receiving managers engage in this same questioning process themselves.

**Institutional infrastructure.** The sidebar "Toyota's Commitment to Learning" describes the
Operations Management Consulting Division (OMCD), which grew out of Taiichi Ohno's efforts to
diffuse TPS through Toyota and its suppliers, and through which many of Toyota's senior officers
— including then-incoming Toyota Motor president Fujio Cho — rotated while relieved of line
responsibilities. The Toyota Supplier Support Center (TSSC), founded in the United States in 1992
and modeled on OMCD, had by the time of writing given workshops to more than 140 companies and
direct hands-on assistance to 80, including firms outside the auto industry and non-Toyota
suppliers.

**Countermeasures and inventory.** The sidebar "Countermeasures in the Toyota Production System"
states that Toyota treats kanban, andon cords, and similar tools as temporary "countermeasures"
rather than permanent solutions, to be replaced as better approaches are found. Contrary to the
popular association of TPS with zero inventory, the authors report observing Toyota deliberately
holding inventory under three circumstances: unpredictable machine downtime or yield loss (safety
stock, owned by whoever is responsible for machine/process reliability), time-consuming
changeovers (batch inventory, owned by the operator and team leader responsible for reducing
changeover time), and volatile customer demand mix or volume (buffer stock near the shipping
point). Toyota does not pool these inventory types even though pooling would reduce short-term
holdings, because pooling would obscure which specific problem and which specific owner each
stock of inventory is tied to.

**The ideal.** The article reports a shared, concretely defined notion of an "ideal" output
across the plants studied: defect-free, deliverable in a batch size of one, suppliable on demand
in the version requested, deliverable immediately, produced without wasting materials, labor,
energy, or other resources, and produced in a work environment safe physically, emotionally, and
professionally. Supporting examples include an unnamed electromechanical-products plant where
workers built error-detecting gauges giving a simple yes/no defect signal, and an unnamed
injection-molded-parts plant that cut a large die-change time from five to three minutes,
enabling a 40% reduction in batch size.

The authors compare this ideal state to the popular notion of mass customization — producing
virtually infinite product variation as efficiently and cheaply as possible — and describe an
idealized Toyota plant as one where a customer could drive up to the shipping dock, request a
customized product or service, and receive it immediately, at the lowest possible price, with no
defects. Any gap between an actual plant's or worker's performance and this description is framed
as a deliberate source of continuing "creative tension" driving further improvement, rather than
as simple failure.

**Organizational structure.** At Toyota's Kamigo, Japan engine-machining plant (visited summer
1998), two machine divisions with three shops apiece were organized differently: in one, process
engineers reported to the division head; in the other, engineers were distributed among the three
shops and reported to shop heads like production workers. The authors attribute this coexistence
of different structures within one plant to differing histories of the problems each division had
encountered, not to one structure being inherently superior. More broadly, the authors describe
the rule-following organization as "nested" and "modular," comparable to Russian dolls, such that
a design change in one part of the system does not unduly affect other parts — which they offer
as the explanation for how Toyota can decentralize extensive change-making authority without
producing organizational chaos.

### Limitations

The study is qualitative and observational, built on plant visits, interviews, and one author's
direct participation, rather than on a documented sampling method, controlled comparison, or
statistical analysis across the 40-plus sites studied. Criteria for classifying a plant as
operating "according to" TPS versus not are not specified beyond the illustrative cases
presented.

A substantial share of the described evidence traces to a single author's five-month embedded
engagement at one supplier plant, alongside a small number of named deep-dive sites (Aisin
Seiki's mattress line, the Kamigo engine plant, Toyota's Georgetown seat-installation line), which
the article treats as representative of the broader 40-plant sample without presenting comparative
data from the remaining sites.

The authors note a transferability constraint of their own account: because the rules were never
written down and are taught through situational, Socratic questioning, successful transfer to
non-Toyota organizations has so far depended on receiving managers replicating that same teaching
process, a claim the article does not further quantify or bound with cases of failed transfer.

The illustrative examples used to support each rule are drawn overwhelmingly from Toyota Group
entities (Toyota assembly plants, Aisin Seiki, unnamed Toyota suppliers); non-Toyota, non-TPS
comparison plants are referenced as part of the underlying 40-plant sample but are not described
with matching illustrative detail, so the article's contrast between TPS and non-TPS operation
rests mainly on the authors' summary characterization rather than paired case narratives.

As a Harvard Business Review piece aimed at practitioners, the article is structured around
illustrative narrative and sidebar exhibits rather than methodological detail, and does not
report effect sizes, comparison-group outcomes, or statistical tests for any of its claims. The
volume/issue number and original journal page range for the September–October 1999 print
appearance could not be confirmed from the document itself, which carries only its HBR reprint
pagination (Reprint 99509, reprint pages 1-12).

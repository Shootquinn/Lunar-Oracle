# Self-Determination Theory and the Facilitation of Intrinsic Motivation, Social Development, and Well-Being

## Abstract

Review article presenting self-determination theory (SDT) as an account of human motivation, self-regulation, and well-being.

Organizes prior laboratory and field research around three posited innate psychological needs: competence, autonomy, and relatedness.

Covers two SDT subtheories: cognitive evaluation theory (CET), on conditions that enhance or undermine intrinsic motivation, and organismic integration theory (OIT), on a continuum of extrinsic-motivation types differing in degree of internalization.

Also reviews work linking need satisfaction to mental health, life-goal content, and well-being, with applications across education, health care, work, sport, religion, and psychotherapy.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The article opens by framing a tension in accounts of human nature: people can be proactive, curious, and self-motivated, or passive, alienated, and apathetic.

The authors argue this variability is largely a function of social-contextual conditions, not dispositional difference or biological endowment alone.

As illustrative examples of non-optimal functioning, the article points to people who sit passively before televisions for hours a day, students who stare blankly from the back of classrooms, and workers who wait listlessly for the weekend, contrasted with the more normative case of people showing considerable effort, agency, and commitment in their lives.

They position self-determination theory (SDT) as a research program concerned with the social conditions that facilitate versus forestall natural self-motivation and healthy psychological development.

The stated aim of the article is to review SDT-guided research on three outcomes: intrinsic motivation, self-regulation of extrinsic motivation, and the impact of psychological need fulfillment on health and well-being.

The organizing claim is that this body of work converges on three innate psychological needs — competence, autonomy, and relatedness — as the constructs that explain both flourishing and diminished human functioning.

The authors also frame SDT as accounting for both sides of the activity/passivity debate in psychology: humans are assumed to have an inclination toward activity and integration but also a vulnerability to passivity, and the research program specifies the conditions that support the former versus elicit or exploit the latter.

The three needs are each traced to a distinct prior literature: competence to Harter (1978) and White (1963), relatedness to Baumeister and Leary (1995) and Reis (1994), and autonomy to deCharms (1968) and Deci (1975).

### Methods and scope

The paper is a narrative literature review, not a report of new primary data collection.

It synthesizes findings from SDT research conducted mainly by the authors and collaborators at the University of Rochester over roughly two and a half decades.

The empirical tradition described is explicitly experimental ("Baconian"): social-contextual variables such as rewards, feedback, choice, deadlines, and controlling versus autonomy-supportive interpersonal styles were directly manipulated to observe effects on internal processes and behavior.

Evidence types cited include laboratory experiments on reward and feedback effects on intrinsic motivation, field studies in classrooms, families, health care settings, and workplaces, and one meta-analysis of reward effects on intrinsic motivation (Deci, Koestner, & Ryan, 1999).

Also cited are correlational and longitudinal survey studies relating life-goal content to well-being, cross-cultural replications (U.S., Russian, and Korean samples), and within-person daily-diary studies analyzed with hierarchical linear modeling.

The review is structured around the two named subtheories of SDT current at the time of writing, cognitive evaluation theory and organismic integration theory, plus a third section on basic psychological needs and mental health that the authors describe as unifying the constructs used in the first two.

Domains of application surveyed in the text include education, health care (medication adherence, weight-loss maintenance, diabetes glucose control, addiction-treatment attendance), work organizations, sport, religion, political and environmental activism, intimate relationships, and psychotherapy.

The work reported was supported in part by National Institute of Mental Health research Grant MH-53385, as stated in the article's author note, which also credits the University of Rochester's Human Motivation Research Group.

### Key findings

**The nature of motivation.** Motivation is defined in the article as encompassing energy, direction, persistence, and equifinality — the activation and intention aspects of behavior.

The authors state that people can be moved to act by qualitatively different types of factors (interest versus coercion, commitment versus surveillance), and that this contrast between authentic (self-authored or endorsed) and externally controlled motivation is meaningful across cultures, citing Johnson (1993) on the significance of this distinction in Japanese socialization specifically.

Comparisons between authentically versus externally motivated people are reported to show that the former have more interest, excitement, and confidence, translating into enhanced performance, persistence, and creativity (Deci & Ryan, 1991; Sheldon, Ryan, Rawsthorne, & Ilardi, 1997), heightened vitality (Nix, Ryan, Manly, & Deci, 1999), self-esteem (Deci & Ryan, 1995), and general well-being (Ryan, Deci, & Grolnick, 1995).

The article notes this advantage for authentic motivation holds even when people have the same level of perceived competence or self-efficacy for the activity, framing SDT's central contribution as differentiating motivation by type (not just amount) because each type has distinct consequences for learning, performance, experience, and well-being.

**Three basic psychological needs.** The review organizes its findings around three postulated innate psychological needs — competence, autonomy, and relatedness — proposed as universal nutriments.

Satisfaction of these needs is associated with enhanced self-motivation and mental health; thwarting of the needs is associated with diminished motivation and ill-being.

The authors state that all three needs must be satisfied for well-being; a context that supports competence but not relatedness is expected to produce impoverished well-being, by analogy to having water but not food.

**Cognitive evaluation theory (CET).** Presented as a subtheory of SDT (Deci & Ryan, 1985) focused on the needs for competence and autonomy, formulated to explain variability in intrinsic motivation.

Positive performance feedback was found to enhance intrinsic motivation, and negative feedback to diminish it (Deci, 1975).

Vallerand and Reid (1984) showed these feedback effects on intrinsic motivation are mediated by perceived competence.

Competence alone was found insufficient: studies (Fisher, 1978; Ryan, 1982) showed that feelings of competence enhance intrinsic motivation only when accompanied by a sense of autonomy, i.e., an internal perceived locus of causality (deCharms, 1968).

Tangible rewards, threats, deadlines, directives, pressured evaluations, and imposed goals were all found to diminish intrinsic motivation, interpreted as operating by promoting an external perceived locus of causality.

Choice, acknowledgment of feelings, and opportunities for self-direction were found to enhance intrinsic motivation by supporting a greater feeling of autonomy (Deci & Ryan, 1985).

A meta-analysis (Deci, Koestner, & Ryan, 1999) is reported as confirming that all expected tangible rewards made contingent on task performance reliably undermine intrinsic motivation.

This finding is presented as standing against contrary claims made by Eisenberger and Cameron (1996) regarding reward effects; the article characterizes the broader issue of reward effects on intrinsic motivation as having been "hotly debated" in the field prior to the meta-analysis.

The article also notes that most research on environmental effects on intrinsic motivation has concentrated on the autonomy-versus-control issue rather than the competence issue, and that this autonomy-focused line of research has historically been the more controversial of the two.

Autonomy-supportive (versus controlling) teachers were found to catalyze greater student intrinsic motivation, curiosity, and desire for challenge (Deci, Nezlek, & Sheinman, 1981; Flink, Boggiano, & Barrett, 1990; Ryan & Grolnick, 1986).

Students taught with a more controlling approach were found to lose initiative and to learn less effectively, especially for conceptual or creative processing tasks (Amabile, 1996; Grolnick & Ryan, 1987; Utman, 1997).

Autonomy-supportive parents, relative to controlling parents, were found to have more intrinsically motivated children (Grolnick, Deci, & Ryan, 1997), with parallel findings reported in sport and music (Frederick & Ryan, 1995).

Relatedness was also found to bear on intrinsic motivation: Anderson, Manoogian, and Reznick (1976) found a very low level of intrinsic motivation in children who worked on an interesting task in the presence of an adult stranger who ignored them and failed to respond to their initiations.

Ryan and Grolnick (1986) similarly observed lower intrinsic motivation in students who experienced their teachers as cold and uncaring.

The text notes that many intrinsically motivated behaviors are performed happily in isolation, so proximal relational support is framed as facilitative rather than strictly necessary for intrinsic motivation.

**Organismic integration theory (OIT) and the internalization continuum.** A second SDT subtheory (Deci & Ryan, 1985) describing forms of extrinsic motivation, illustrated in the article's Figure 1 as a continuum from nonself-determined to self-determined.

The article contrasts extrinsic motivation that carries personal endorsement with extrinsic motivation that is mere compliance using a homework example: students who do homework because they personally grasp its value for a chosen career are extrinsically motivated, as are those who do the work only to adhere to parental control, but the two cases differ in relative autonomy despite both involving instrumentalities rather than enjoyment of the work itself.

At the far left is amotivation: the state of lacking intention to act, associated with an impersonal perceived locus of causality and arising from not valuing an activity, not feeling competent to do it, or not expecting it to yield a desired outcome.

Moving rightward, external regulation is behavior performed to satisfy an external demand or reward contingency, experienced as controlled, with an external perceived locus of causality; this is the type of motivation emphasized by operant theorists (e.g., Skinner, 1953).

Introjected regulation involves taking in a regulation without fully accepting it as one's own; behaviors are performed to avoid guilt or anxiety or to attain ego enhancements such as pride, described in the article as "regulation by contingent self-esteem," with a still-external perceived locus of causality despite being internally driven.

Identified regulation reflects conscious valuing of a behavioral goal such that the action is accepted or owned as personally important, with a somewhat internal perceived locus of causality.

Integrated regulation, the most autonomous form of extrinsic motivation, occurs when identified regulations are fully assimilated to the self and brought into congruence with the person's other values and needs; it has an internal perceived locus of causality but is still classified as extrinsic because the activity is done for separable outcomes rather than inherent enjoyment.

At the far right is intrinsic motivation itself: doing an activity for its inherent satisfactions, with an internal perceived locus of causality and regulatory processes described as interest, enjoyment, and inherent satisfaction.

Figure 1 in the article lays this continuum out as a table with a "Behavior" axis running from "Nonself-Determined" to "Self-Determined," and four aligned rows beneath each of the six motivation types (amotivation; externally, introjected, identified, and integrated regulation; intrinsic motivation): a "Motivation" row, a "Regulatory Styles" row (labeled Non-Regulation, External Regulation, Introjected Regulation, Identified Regulation, Integrated Regulation, and Intrinsic Regulation, respectively), a "Perceived Locus of Causality" row, and a "Relevant Regulatory Processes" row.

The figure is schematic rather than data-bearing: it organizes and labels the taxonomy described in the surrounding text but does not present any results, statistics, or findings beyond what the prose states.

The article defines internalization as people's "taking in" a value or regulation, and integration as the further transformation of that regulation into one's own so that it subsequently emanates from the person's sense of self.

The authors note that internalization along this continuum is not framed as a developmental stage sequence that a person must progress through for a given regulation; rather, a new behavioral regulation can be internalized at any point on the continuum depending on prior experience and current situational factors (Ryan, 1995), although the overall range of behaviors a person can assimilate to the self is reported to increase over time with cognitive capacity and ego development (Loevinger & Blasi, 1991; Chandler & Connell, 1987).

The text also notes that in some studies external and introjected regulation have been combined into a "controlled motivation" composite, and identified, integrated, and intrinsic regulation combined into an "autonomous motivation" composite (e.g., Williams, Grow, Freedman, Ryan, & Deci, 1996).

Ryan and Connell (1989) tested this ordering empirically in school children and found that external, introjected, identified, and intrinsic regulatory styles were intercorrelated in a quasi-simplex pattern, taken as evidence for an underlying continuum of relative autonomy.

In that study, more externally regulated students showed less interest, value, and effort toward achievement and were more likely to disown responsibility for negative outcomes by blaming others such as the teacher.

Introjected regulation was positively related to expending more effort but also to feeling more anxiety and coping more poorly with failure.

Identified regulation was associated with more interest and enjoyment of school, more positive coping styles, and more effort.

Subsequent education studies extended these associations to greater engagement (Connell & Wellborn, 1991), better performance (Miserandino, 1996), lower dropout (Vallerand & Bissonnette, 1992), higher-quality learning (Grolnick & Ryan, 1987), and better teacher ratings (Hayamizu, 1997).

In health care, greater internalization was associated with greater adherence to medications among people with chronic illness (Williams, Rodin, Ryan, Grolnick, & Deci, 1998).

Greater internalization was also associated with better long-term maintenance of weight loss among morbidly obese patients (Williams et al., 1996), improved glucose control among diabetics (Williams, Freedman, & Deci, 1998), and greater attendance and involvement in an addiction-treatment program (Ryan, Plant, & O'Malley, 1995).

Further associations between more internalized motivation and positive outcomes are cited in religion (Ryan, Rigby, & King, 1993), physical exercise (Chatzisarantis, Biddle, & Meek, 1997), political activity (Koestner, Losier, Vallerand, & Carducci, 1996), environmental activism (Green-Demers, Pelletier, & Menard, 1997), and intimate relationships (Blais, Sabourin, Boucher, & Vallerand, 1990).

**Conditions promoting internalization.** OIT proposes that relatedness support — feeling connected to the socializing agent — is centrally important for initial internalization, since extrinsically motivated behaviors are typically prompted or valued by significant others to whom the person feels or wants to feel attached.

Ryan, Stiller, and Lynch (1994) found that children who more fully internalized regulation for positive school-related behaviors were those who felt securely connected to, and cared for by, their parents and teachers.

Perceived competence support was also found to facilitate internalization: children directed to perform behaviors before they are developmentally ready to master or understand them were predicted to only partially internalize the regulation, remaining externally regulated or introjected.

The experience of autonomy was identified as the factor specifically critical for a regulation to become integrated rather than merely introjected, since integration requires the person to grasp and synthesize the meaning of the regulation with their other goals and values.

Deci, Eghrari, Patrick, and Leone (1994) demonstrated experimentally that providing a meaningful rationale for an uninteresting behavior, along with supports for autonomy and relatedness, promoted its internalization and integration, whereas controlling contexts yielded only introjection.

Grolnick and Ryan (1989), using parent interviews, found greater internalization of school-related values among children whose parents were more supportive of autonomy and relatedness.

Strahan (1995) found that more autonomy-supportive parents promoted greater religious identification (versus introjection) in their offspring.

Williams and Deci (1996), using a longitudinal design, found greater internalization of biopsychosocial values and practices among medical students whose instructors were more autonomy-supportive.

**Autonomy distinguished from independence.** The article explicitly rejects equating SDT's construct of autonomy with individualism, independence, or detachment, noting that some other theories (e.g., Steinberg & Silverberg, 1986) treat autonomy as implying low relatedness.

Kim, Butzel, and Ryan (1998) are cited as finding a more positive relation between autonomy and collectivistic attitudes than between autonomy and individualistic attitudes in Korean and U.S. samples.

Ryan and Lynch (1989) and Ryan, Stiller, and Lynch (1994) are cited as finding positive, rather than negative, links between relatedness to parents and autonomy in teenagers.

**Alienation and its prevention.** The article frames intrinsically motivated behavior as unalienated and authentic "in the fullest sense," and frames internalized/integrated extrinsic motivation as also permitting commitment and authenticity.

Conversely, the authors attribute alienation and inauthenticity — cited examples include employees who show no initiative, teenagers who reject their schools' values, and patients who adhere poorly to treatment — to the undermining of intrinsic motivation and, "perhaps even more typically," to the failure of internalization.

The explanatory strategy proposed is to examine, for a given instance of diminished functioning, the degree to which the individual's immediate social context and developmental environment have thwarted the needs for competence, autonomy, and relatedness, an approach the authors liken to the field of developmental psychopathology (Cicchetti, 1991).

**Needs and mental health: definitions and scope.** The authors define a basic need, whether physiological (Hull, 1943) or psychological, as an energizing state that conduces toward health and well-being if satisfied and contributes to pathology and ill-being if not.

They state that satisfying competence, autonomy, and relatedness across the life span is proposed to be necessary for an individual's ongoing sense of integrity and well-being, a state they label "eudaimonia" (Ryan & Frederick, 1997; Waterman, 1993).

Three questions are identified as organizing their more recent work on needs: whether pursuit and attainment of all culturally congruent aspirations and life values is associated with well-being; whether need-related processes operate similarly across different cultural circumstances; and whether within-person variability in need satisfaction relates to variability in well-being indicators.

The authors note that exposure to commercial media can prompt a focus on materialism (Richins, 1987), which they characterize as providing only fleeting satisfaction and potentially detracting from basic need fulfillment and well-being.

**Needs, goal content, and well-being.** Kasser and Ryan (1993, 1996) examined individual differences in emphasis on intrinsic aspirations (e.g., affiliation, personal growth, community) versus extrinsic aspirations (e.g., wealth, fame, image).

Strong relative importance placed on intrinsic aspirations was positively associated with well-being indicators such as self-esteem and self-actualization, and inversely with depression and anxiety.

Strong relative importance placed on extrinsic aspirations was negatively related to these same well-being indicators.

Ryan, Chirkov, Little, Sheldon, Timoshina, and Deci (1999) replicated the intrinsic/extrinsic aspiration findings in a Russian sample.

Self-reported attainment of intrinsic aspirations was positively associated with well-being, whereas attainment of extrinsic aspirations was not (Ryan, Chirkov, et al., 1999; Kasser & Ryan, in press).

Sheldon and Kasser (1998), in a longitudinal study, found that well-being was enhanced by attainment of intrinsic goals, whereas success at extrinsic goals provided little benefit.

Kasser, Ryan, Zax, and Sameroff (1995) found that teens exposed to cold, controlling maternal care (rated by teens, mothers, and observers) were more likely to develop materialistic orientations than better-nurtured teens, who more strongly valued the intrinsic goals of personal growth, relationships, and community.

V. Kasser and Ryan (in press) found that supports for autonomy and relatedness predicted greater well-being among nursing home residents.

Baard, Deci, and Ryan (1998) found that employees' experiences of satisfaction of the needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness in the workplace predicted their performance and well-being at work.

Sheldon, Ryan, Rawsthorne, and Ilardi (1997) found that satisfaction in each of several life roles (e.g., student, employee, friend), relative to a person's own mean satisfaction, was attributable to the degree to which that role supported authenticity and autonomous functioning.

Sheldon, Reis, and Ryan (1996), using hierarchical linear modeling on daily-diary data, found that within-person daily fluctuations in satisfaction of the autonomy and competence needs predicted within-person fluctuations in mood, vitality, physical symptoms, and self-esteem.

Reis, Sheldon, Gable, Roscoe, and Ryan (in press) found that day-to-day variation in the fulfillment of each of the three needs — competence, autonomy, and relatedness — independently predicted variability in daily well-being.

**Applied significance.** In closing, the authors state that knowledge of the conditions supporting basic psychological needs is relevant to parents and educators (conditions promoting assimilation of information and behavioral regulations), to managers seeking to facilitate workplace motivation and commitment, and to psychotherapists and health professionals for whom motivation is described as "perhaps the critical variable in producing maintained change."

They conclude that attending to the presence or deprivation of supports for competence, autonomy, and relatedness allows practitioners to diagnose sources of alienation versus engagement and to facilitate both achievement and well-being.

### Limitations

As a narrative review, the article does not present new primary data or apply a systematic or meta-analytic search methodology, apart from citing one existing meta-analysis on reward effects.

Study selection and synthesis reflect the authors' own research program rather than an independent or exhaustive survey of the motivation literature.

The great majority of cited studies are authored or co-authored by Ryan, Deci, or their close collaborators, which the review itself does not treat as a limitation but which readers assessing independence of evidence may wish to note.

Several cited sources are unpublished manuscripts or listed as "in press" at the time of writing (e.g., Baard, Deci, & Ryan, 1998; V. Kasser & Ryan, in press; Reis et al., in press), meaning some summarized findings had not completed peer review in final form.

The review mixes experimental designs, which support causal inference, with correlational and survey designs (e.g., goal-content and well-being associations, workplace and nursing-home need-satisfaction studies); the text does not always flag which specific findings are correlational versus experimentally derived.

The three-needs framework is presented as universal and essential, but the review does not report effect sizes, sample sizes, or other quantitative study details for the individual studies it cites.

Cross-cultural evidence offered in support of universality is limited to a small number of samples (Russian, Korean, and U.S.), with the authors acknowledging that the salience and mode of expression of the needs may vary by culture even while claiming the underlying need-satisfaction/well-being relation is invariant.

The list of three needs is described as having been arrived at "inductively, using the empirical process" rather than derived from a prior deductive taxonomy, and the article does not specify a falsification criterion by which a fourth need could be ruled in or out.

The article is a conceptual synthesis addressed to a broad audience of researchers and practitioners; it does not include a table or appendix enumerating the specific studies, samples, or outcome measures underlying each cited finding, so readers must consult the original sources for methodological detail.

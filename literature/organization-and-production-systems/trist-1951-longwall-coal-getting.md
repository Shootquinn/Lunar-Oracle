# Longwall Method of Coal-Getting

## Citation

Trist, E. L., & Bamforth, K. W. (1951). Some social and psychological consequences of the Longwall method of coal-getting: An examination of the psychological situation and defences of a work group in relation to the social structure and technological content of the work system. *Human Relations, 4*(1), 3–38. https://doi.org/10.1177/001872675100400101

Publisher URL: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/001872675100400101

## Abstract

A Tavistock Institute field study of British coal-mining that treats the mechanized longwall
face as a single socio-technical system: a technology (coal-cutters and conveyors imposing a
rigid three-shift cycle) and a social structure (seven institutionalized occupational roles)
that jointly shape the face-worker's psychological life. Built from two years of interviews
with some twenty key informants, it contrasts the pre-mechanized hand-got system — small,
self-regulating pair-based groups with "responsible autonomy" — against the longwall
production unit of 40-50 men, whose task differentiation, role and shift segregation, and
absence of any integrated cycle group produce chronic stress, low productivity, and four
characteristic group defences. Establishes that a production method cannot be evaluated on
technological grounds alone.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The authors, following a set of coal-face work-organization innovations that had appeared
sporadically since nationalization, set out not to appraise those innovations but to make a
detailed appraisal of the standard method they modified — the mechanized "longwall" method.
Two preliminary hypotheses are weighed: that reported productivity gains reflected remarkable
attributes of the innovations, or that they reflected correctable social shortcomings within
the longwall method itself. The content of the innovations — each raising the group-relatedness
of the face-worker by simple means — points the study toward the second hypothesis.

The paper positions itself against prior medical writing (Dickson, Halliday, Morris) that had
linked mechanized mining to psycho-somatic and neurotic disorder but had not traced the
connection between the miner's health and productivity and the social structure of the work
system. The stated aim is to give an account of the social and psychological consequences of
the longwall method by regarding it simultaneously as a technological system expressive of
mass-production engineering and as a social structure of institutionalized occupational roles,
both acting as psychological forces in the life-space of the face-worker.

### Methods and scope

The method was to maintain relatively continuous contact over about two years with roughly
twenty key informants representing the range of coal-face occupations, all with wide industry
experience, through repeated discussions and long interviews. Parallel discussions were held
with management up to Area Manager level, and group discussions were run over fortnightly
periods at two Divisional schools for Colliery Managers. Three psychiatrists with experience
of miners' problems also served as informants. A footnote records that one author,
K. W. Bamforth, had himself been a miner for eighteen years at the coal-face.

The study is a single qualitative, observational field investigation, part of a larger
Tavistock Institute of Human Relations project on dissemination of new social techniques,
initiated by the Human Factors Panel of the Committee on Industrial Productivity and
administered by the Medical Research Council. Scope is the British coal industry after
nationalization, focused on thin-seam longwall working; the account describes one common
version of the system, acknowledging wide local variation in nomenclature, equipment, and
seam conditions.

The paper proceeds through eight numbered sections: an introduction from recent innovations;
the character of the pre-mechanized equilibrium and its disturbance; the features and
difficulties of the longwall production unit as a whole; the stress of mass production
underground; the special situation of the filling shift; four types of group defence; the
problems of the preparation shifts; and conclusions. Evidence is presented in text supported
by two figures of the working layout (a district plan and a face set for the filling shift),
a third figure locating the successive worker groups along the face, a fourth tracing the
travel of a roll or fault, and two tables setting out the seven-role occupational structure
(Table I) and the differential incidence of bad conditions and bad work across twenty fillers
(Table II).

### Key findings

**The pre-mechanized hand-got system.** The earlier equilibrium rested on small-group
organization at the face. The typical unit was a working pair — a hewer and his mate, with a
boy "trammer" — who made their own contract with colliery management and worked their own
short face; units sometimes extended to seven or eight when several colliers and trammers
worked together. This gave the complete coal-getting task, with its full cycle of operations,
"total significance and dynamic closure" for a single face-to-face group. Leadership and
supervision were internal; the group had a quality of *responsible autonomy* grounded in the
wholeness of its task and its contractual status. Each collier was an all-round workman able
to substitute for his mate, with craft pride and artisan independence; the specifically mining
"underground skill" was person-carried. Workmates were chosen sociometrically by the men
themselves, producing stable, long-enduring relationships often reinforced by kinship, with
"exogamous" (non-kin) choice available as a safeguard against overly oppressive ties.

**Adaptability underground.** Because such pair groups worked short faces continuously, they
could stop at any point reached by shift-end and set their own pace and targets, matching the
uneven distribution of bad conditions along a seam and the age and stamina of members. Shared
work in darkness — where external and internal dangers must be faced together with limited
communication — is offered as explaining the strong need for a small primary group underground.

**The large undifferentiated collectivity.** Balancing the small pairs was a large, relatively
undifferentiated pit collectivity, whose traditions ran above the norm for occupational groups.
Between these two extremes there was little organizational experience; nothing at the
"factory department" level existed. Rivalries between pairs (bribing the deputy for a good
"benk," disputes over tubs) channeled aggression in a way that preserved small-group loyalty,
while the large group expressed aggression through trade-union resistance. The system's "bad"
did not destroy its "good," and a social balance persisted despite hard work and meagre reward.

**Mechanization and the longwall cycle.** Coal-cutters and mechanical conveyors made possible a
single long face (typically 180-200 yds) in place of a series of short faces — economical in
thin seams (under 4 ft common in British coal). The work was broken into a rigid succession of
operations over three shifts of seven and a half hours, completing one coal-getting cycle per
24 hours, commonly yielding about 200 tons per cycle; a medium pit with three seams might run
12-15 faces simultaneously. The 40 men on an average face split 10 to the first ("cutting")
shift, 10 to the second ("ripping") shift, and 20 to the third ("filling") shift. Figures 1
and 2 depict a 3-ft working with the face 90 yds either side of the main gate, gates ripped up
to 9 ft and 7 ft, props set every 3 ft, and a 6-ft undercut; the extracted "gob" area is left
to collapse. The production unit became a cycle group of 40-50 men under a shot-firer and
shift deputies, an intermediate social magnitude for which the industry's traditions were
least prepared.

**Task differentiation.** Seven workman-level roles are linked to the component tasks: borer,
cutter, gummer, belt-breaker, belt-builder, ripper, and filler. Table I details them —
borers (2, paid per hole), cutters (2, per yard), gummers (4, day wage), belt-breakers (2, per
yard), belt-builders (2, per yard), rippers (8, by cubic measure), and fillers (20, by weight/
tonnage) — spanning 40 men, three shifts, and five methods of payment. Tasks fall into four
groups: preparing the face for shot-firing, shifting the conveyor, ripping and building the
gates, and moving the shot coal onto the conveyor. Interdependence is so close that the system
becomes vulnerable to its need for near-100% performance at each step; every stoppage can lose
some 200 tons.

The first-shift preparation tasks — boring holes for the shot-firer, driving the coal-cutter to
make an even undercut about six inches from the floor, clearing out the six inches of coal
("gummings") left in the cut, and inserting supporting "noggings" so the standing cut does not
sag to the floor — must be performed in order, and the success of the shots depends on the
efficiency of each. Faulty preparation produces named defects such as "sticky tops" (coal
clinging to the roof), "hard backs" (holes not bored to full depth), an uneven cut where the
cutter "gets up into the coal," or a wasted shot when gummings are left in. Table I also
records the status ranking within roles — the cutter's front man is senior and responsible for
the cut, the "main ripper" ranks next to the front man of the cutters, and gummers carry the
lowest standing — showing how the technological task structure and the social ranking are read
off one another.

**Segmented social organization.** Face-workers are traditionally trained in only one of the
seven roles, and this segregation is intensified by the five payment methods, exaggerated
status differences, and shift segregation — the three shifts never meet, and the preparation
groups alternate on "back shifts" while fillers alternate on days and afternoons, splitting the
community as well as the work. Four radically different primary-group patterns coexist: series
of interdependent pairs (borers, belt-builders, belt-breakers); the extended pair of cutters
with four loosely attached gummers; the self-sufficient group of eight rippers; and the
aggregate of twenty fillers spread over the face. The authors submit that the non-existence of
the cycle group as a social whole, despite the technological interdependence of tasks, is a
major contradiction of the method: reintegrating tasks into a technological whole does not
reintegrate the role-groups into a social whole.

The mass-production layout is described as mobile and large-scale: the conveyor must be shifted
forward each cycle, the props withdrawn (letting the roof sag) and thrown over for reuse, and,
in thin seams, all movement along the face takes the form of "creeping" — crawling on hands and
knees along a propped 3-ft lane. The production engineer's "simple equation" — 200 tons equals
40 men over 200 yds. over 24 hours — is contrasted with the psychological and social
difficulties that arise when a task's scale transcends what a single face-to-face group can
complete in one place at one time, so that a multiple group of sub-groups must come into being
and inter-group problems are superimposed on intra-group tensions.

**Stress of mass production underground.** Unlike the factory, the underground "ground" cannot
be held passive; unpredictable natural conditions threaten the task "figure" continuously. The
paper distinguishes "bad conditions" (natural — wet, heat, dust, rolls, faults, roof weight,
rising floor) from "bad work" (human shortcomings — sticky tops, hard backs, gummings left in).
Bad conditions tend to instigate bad work, and the two interact in circular, self-amplifying
processes that threaten to stop the cycle, so the system always works against the threat of its
own breakdown, generating tension and anxiety. Local disturbances resonate and are magnified
across a large social space. Table II maps the cumulative, uneven incidence of specific bad
conditions and bad work across a face of twenty fillers rated by skill, stamina, and
conscientiousness, with informal sub-group membership and isolates marked; Figure 4 charts how
a roll or fault travels across the face and how many shifts it stays in a given man's length.

Bad conditions also bear directly on pay. Special tasks over and above a worker's basic-pay
operation are "bye-work," remunerated at rates such that a week heavy in bye-work yields less
than one given wholly to production work; from the face-worker's standpoint bad conditions mean
more danger, harder work, less pay, and sometimes compelled overtime — staying an extra one to
three hours under conditions that can exceed what older men can endure. The differential
squeeze falls unevenly along the face, since bad conditions and bad work are distributed
unequally not only between faces but between sections and tasks within one face.

**Productivity, cycle control, and the norm of low productivity.** No systematic survey of
stoppages was possible, but on one of the best faces the fillers had failed to fill off only
three times in a year; a more usual frequency was once in two months, or five or six times a
year, with far more in "bad faces" in "bad pits." A week's lost work per year corresponds to
roughly a 2% production loss — a figure the authors read as the measure of the effort spent
holding disturbance short of a full stoppage. The main burden of preventing stoppages falls on
the deputy, whose authority is felt to be incommensurate with his responsibility; the strain
produces a group "culture" of angry, suspicious bargaining. The authors describe a settled
"norm of low productivity" as the only adaptive way of running a complicated, rigid, large-scale
work system borrowed with too little modification from factory engineering.

**The filling shift.** The filler is the modern version of the older second collier, now alone
in his own 8-10 yd stint, servicing an "absent group" of the nineteen other fillers and
depending on — but not interdependent with — the preparation workers of earlier shifts
(dependence running in one direction only). Figure 3 shows successive groups of face-workers
traversing the whole face while fillers are left "alone with each other." Fillers face unequal
conditions with equal stints, breeding chronic uncertainty and irritation and, the authors
suggest, contributing to psycho-somatic and neurotic disorder; instances are cited of men
sitting in stony silence, attacking coal in rage, or leaving the face in panic.

**Four types of group defence.** In this isolated, unequal dependence the individual erects
reactive (not adaptive) defences, elaborated in the work group: (1) *informal organization* —
private helping arrangements in twos, threes, and fours, unstable, clique-like, leaving isolates
over; (2) *reactive individualism* — personal secrecy, competitive intrigue for good places,
bribing other shifts, distrust dramatized around the "big note," the "number man," and the
"corner man"; (3) *mutual scapegoating* — passing the "buck" between shifts and onto the deputy,
a self-perpetuating system in which nothing is resolved and no one feels guilty, letting each
make an "anonymous contribution" and ascribe both his own and the group's "badness" to the
system; and (4) *self-compensatory absenteeism* — a socially structured, code-governed
withdrawal accepted as a means of prolonging working life at the face, sharpened by fear of
being "too old for the face at forty" and by strained family status.

**Problems of the preparation shifts.** The cutting team (front and back man on the cutter plus
four gummers) does not exist officially as a group; gummers, the lowest-paid, lowest-prestige,
day-wage group, become an outcast and scapegoated body with the power to sabotage fillers by
leaving gummings in. The interdependent pairs (borers, belt-builders, belt-breakers) are split
off into narrow component tasks, retaining a stable but narrow relationship. By contrast, the
ripping team of seven or eight — working in the gates, with an intelligible total task and
complete responsibility, called a "good crowd" — retains responsible autonomy and stands as a
survival of the hand-got past in the mechanical present.

**Conclusions.** With wartime economic pressure gone, miners are less tolerant of unsatisfying
conditions; socially ineffective face structures are held likely to depress morale, discourage
recruitment, and raise labour turnover. The authors call for systematic study of the
innovations already tried and argue that a qualitative change is needed so that a social as
well as a technological whole comes into existence — restoring responsible autonomy to primary
groups, developing formal small-group organization on the filling shift, resolving authority in
the cutting team, exploiting multi-role training, and noting that room-and-pillar methods and
newer machines (power loaders, strippers) also require strongly-knit primary work-groups.

### Limitations

The study is qualitative and observational, based on some twenty key informants plus management
and psychiatric contacts, without a systematic survey; the authors explicitly note that no
systematic survey of cycle-stoppage incidence was possible and that the degree of stress could
only be explored fully in a therapeutic relationship. The account describes one common version
of the longwall system and repeatedly flags wide local variation in equipment, nomenclature,
seam conditions, and productivity. The innovations that motivated the inquiry had appeared
selectively in pits with high technical management and group relations, so their élite origin
is said to warrant caution about wider applicability. The account is framed within Tavistock
psycho-social and field-theory concepts (responsible autonomy, "figure-ground," induced
forces, group "culture") that carry interpretive weight beyond directly observed data. The
comparative remarks on room-and-pillar methods and on newer machines are brief and offered as
observations rather than as findings from a matched comparison, and the productivity loss
figure of roughly 2% is derived from informant estimates rather than a compiled record.

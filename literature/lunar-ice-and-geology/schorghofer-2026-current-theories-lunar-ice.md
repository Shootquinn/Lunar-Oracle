## Abstract

Review of the classical and modern theories of cold-trapped lunar ice, compared against observational constraints. The central thesis is that the "standard model" of lunar ice, in which ice accumulates in polar cold traps once the spin-axis orientation became small enough for polar craters to be permanently shadowed, is consistent with the major observational constraints, with only a few less-established observational claims unaccounted for. The review is organized around ice-storage processes, sources and transport over time, observational constraints, and the standard model with its challenges and the key measurements still needed.

---

### Background and objective

- The review surveys the theoretical basis for lunar polar ice and tests it against observations, focusing on fundamental concepts rather than mission engineering.
- Three ice-storage processes are treated: surface cold traps, relic buried ice, and subsurface cold-trapping (vapor pumping). Each has a different notion of stability, which the review distinguishes carefully (long-lived versus truly in equilibrium).

### Methods and scope

- Structure: Section 1 (ice storage processes: cold-trapping in PSRs, the Cold Silicate Hypothesis, vapor pumping); Section 2 (sources and transport over time: polar wander, sources of water, the exospheric transport hypothesis); Section 3 (observational constraints: ice in PSRs, ice outside PSRs, the water exosphere, supervolatiles); Section 4 (the standard model, challenges to it, and predictions and key measurements needed); appendices on thermodynamics of cold-trapping and a fractal photometric paradox.

### Key findings

- Cold-trap threshold: a temperature of about 110 K is a defensible threshold for cold-trapping. Crystalline ice sublimates into vacuum at about 100 kg/m2 per billion years (about 10 cm/Ga) at 109 K and about 1000 kg/m2/Ga (about 1 m/Ga) at 114 K, so a 5 K change corresponds to a tenfold change in sublimation rate. Amorphous ice, the likely form at these temperatures, has only slightly higher vapor pressure.
- PSRs versus cold traps: permanently shadowed regions are defined by shadow, cold traps by temperature (time-integrated sublimation loss). Diviner data allow mapping cold traps independently. In practice all lunar cold traps lie within PSRs, but the total PSR area is significantly larger than the total cold-trap area because some PSRs, especially far from the pole, are heated by infrared emission from nearby sunlit terrain.
- Micro cold traps: at centimeter scales lateral heat conduction tends to warm mini-PSRs above threshold, though lunar fines are extremely insulating (thermal conductivity about 10^-3 W/m/K, versus about 4 W/m/K for solid rock), so very small cold spots can persist; but such small craters are statistically young and cannot have accumulated much ice.
- The Cold Silicate Hypothesis (Starukhina, around 2000), that implanted solar-wind hydrogen and low-temperature silicate radar properties could explain the polar signals without water ice, has been shown wrong by LCROSS (molecular water in a PSR) and MESSENGER at Mercury, though a related temperature-controlled hydroxyl-veneer idea survives in modified form.
- Vapor pumping (subsurface cold-trapping): temperature cycles can transport water from the surface into the subsurface, sequestering ice at a specific depth set by the thermal skin depth, wherever subsurface temperatures stay continuously below about 110 K. This can occur over polar areas far larger than the PSRs, requires a quasi-continuous surface water source, and has very low yield. Short-lived thermal events do not produce long-term subsurface ice.
- Space weathering (Lyman-alpha radiation, solar-wind sputtering, micrometeoroid bombardment) erodes ice at pessimistic rates of order 0.2 nm/yr, so water delivery must be fast enough to overcome loss; episodic delivery followed by impact-gardening burial is more effective than continuous delivery.
- Standard model status: the Watson, Murray, and Brown (1961) cold-trap accumulation model remains an excellent working hypothesis, neither fully proven nor disproven, and its predictions match the major observational constraints.

### Limitations

- The document is a theoretical review and preprint; it synthesizes and compares hypotheses rather than reporting new measurements, and it explicitly assumes reader familiarity with the topic.
- The standard model is described as consistent with major constraints but with a few less-established observational claims still unaccounted for, and the review identifies key measurements that are still needed rather than settled.
- Quantitative thresholds (for example the 110 K cold-trap criterion and the sublimation rates) rely on extrapolation from higher-temperature laboratory data and on the assumed ice phase.

### Topic mapping

- Section 1 defines the three storage processes and the cold-trapping thermodynamics (110 K threshold, sublimation rates, micro cold traps, Cold Silicate Hypothesis, vapor pumping). Section 2 covers polar wander, water sources, and exospheric transport. Section 3 assembles the observational constraints on ice inside and outside PSRs, the water exosphere, and supervolatiles. Section 4 states the standard model, its challenges, and the predictions and measurements needed to test it. Appendices give the thermodynamic perspective of cold-trapping and the fractal photometric paradox.

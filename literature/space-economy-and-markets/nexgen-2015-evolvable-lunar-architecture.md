## Abstract

Economic and systems analysis of an "Evolvable Lunar Architecture" (ELA), an incremental, low-cost, low-risk approach for returning humans to the Moon using commercial public-private partnerships, whose strategic objective is commercial mining of propellant at the lunar poles and its transport to lunar orbit for NASA to use in sending humans to Mars. The study asks whether the United States could return humans to the Moon, and build a permanent settlement, within NASA's existing deep-space human-spaceflight budget of about $3 to 4 billion per year by leveraging the proven COTS/CRS partnership model. It concludes that a human return is achievable in 5 to 7 years for about $10 billion, and a propellant-producing industrial base for about $40 billion, largely within a flat NASA budget.

---

### Background and objective

- The primary research question: could America return humans to the Moon and ultimately develop a permanent settlement by leveraging commercial partnerships within NASA's existing deep-space human-spaceflight budget of $3 to 4 billion per year.
- The ELA strategic objective is commercial mining of propellant from the lunar poles, transported to lunar orbit for NASA to send humans to Mars.
- The study assumes the United States leads an international partnership leveraging private industry, and uses public-private-partnership models proven by NASA (COTS/CRS) and other agencies.

### Methods and scope

- The analysis models an evolvable phased architecture: Phase 1A (robotic scouting, prospecting, site preparation); Phase 1B (human sorties to the lunar equator); Phase 2 (human sorties to the poles); Phase 3 (propellant delivery to the Earth-Moon L2 point and a permanent lunar base); and an optional Phase 4+ (a reusable orbital transfer vehicle between LEO and L2).
- It includes a technical risk assessment, life-cycle cost estimates (with ground rules, historical-data basis, and modeling against the NASA budget context), integrated-risk management strategies, and a governance analysis of an International Lunar Authority, supported by case studies (Port Authority of NY-NJ, CERN, TVA, COMSAT-INTELSAT, AT&T, National Parks, McMurdo Station).
- The partnership basis is the COTS/CRS two-part model: funded Space Act Agreements (fSAAs) with shared NASA and industry investment to demonstrate new capabilities, followed by FAR Part 12 firm-fixed-price commercial-service contracts, which produced Falcon 9 and Antares and the Dragon and Cygnus cargo vehicles at much lower cost than traditional acquisition.

### Key findings

- A human return to the surface of the Moon could be led within 5 to 7 years from authority to proceed at an estimated total cost of about $10 billion (plus or minus 30 percent) for two independent and competing commercial service providers, or about $5 billion each, using partnership methods.
- A permanent industrial base of 4 private-sector astronauts could be developed in about 10 to 12 years after first setting foot on the Moon, providing 200 metric tons of propellant per year in lunar orbit for NASA, at a total cost of about $40 billion (plus or minus 30 percent).
- These results could potentially be achieved within NASA's existing flat deep-space human-spaceflight budget.
- A commercial lunar base providing propellant in lunar orbit could substantially reduce the cost and risk of NASA sending humans to Mars, reducing the number of required Space Launch System (SLS) launches from as many as 12 to only 3.
- An International Lunar Authority, modeled after CERN and traditional public infrastructure authorities, is proposed as the most advantageous mechanism for managing the combined business and technical risks of affordable, sustainable lunar development.
- A permanent commercial lunar base might substantially pay for its operations by exporting propellant to lunar orbit for sale to NASA and others, enabling lunar economic development at small marginal cost.
- Confirming the business case requires sending robotic prospectors to the lunar poles to verify that water or hydrogen is economically accessible near the surface inside polar craters.

### Limitations

- The report explicitly states it is a limited study evaluating two specific technical approaches for one architectural strategy; it did not evaluate all alternatives for returning to the Moon, nor partnership methods for other destinations.
- The independent review team characterized the result as a conceptual architecture and a starting point for trade studies, not a validated design.
- Cost figures carry plus or minus 30 percent uncertainty and depend on the assumed COTS-style partnership savings, and the propellant business case is contingent on confirming economically accessible polar water, which had not been done.

### Topic mapping

- The executive summary states the ELA objective and the headline $10 billion return, 200 metric ton per year propellant, $40 billion base, and 12-to-3 SLS-launch findings. The Study Assumptions section develops the public-private-partnership acquisition strategy (COTS/CRS), 100 percent private ownership of lunar infrastructure, the International Lunar Authority, and the evolvable-architecture concept. The Technical Analysis section defines the phased architecture and technical risk. The Life Cycle Cost section gives the cost basis, modeling, and results. The Managing Integrated Risks and Mitigating Business Risks sections cover launch, in-space, and lander risk strategies and the International Lunar Authority governance case studies.

# The Dagger and the Gift: The Impact of the Korean War on Japan

## Abstract

Narrative-historical reassessment of the Korean War's (1950-53) impact on Japan,
organized around a recurring "dagger" (security or social burden) versus "gift"
(economic or diplomatic benefit) framing applied in turn to national security, the
economy, and society and culture. Argues against a literature that treats the war as
a "transforming event" for Japan: the true transforming event, in Ernest May's sense
of the term, was the Pacific War of 1937-45, and the Korean War instead accelerated,
confirmed, and rendered irreversible changes already set in motion by defeat and
occupation. Draws on U.S. State Department Foreign Relations of the United States
volumes, Australian Archives records, contemporary Japanese and American press, and
a wide secondary and memoir literature to trace the 1951 peace and security treaties,
a roughly four-year run of American war-related procurement spending in Japan
(June 1950-July 1954), and shifts in the size and treatment of Japan's Korean
minority and the American military presence. Concludes the war left Japan more
prosperous and more open to foreign cultural influence, but did not itself supply the
institutional "formula" behind Japan's later high-growth era, which the article
argues rested on economic groundwork already laid before June 1950.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The article opens with two 1950 magazine pieces that supply its title metaphor.
Admiral Nomura Kichisaburo, invoking his predecessor Komura Jutaro's language from
the Russo-Japanese War era, described the Korean peninsula as a dagger thrust from
the continent toward Japan's vital parts, and cast the war as compelling Japan to
side with the United States against Soviet-backed expansion. The British journalist
Hessell Tiltman, writing in the same issue of the same journal, called the war a
"great responsibility and a great opportunity," a gift Japan could convert into full
recovery of its sovereignty by serving as a secure rear base against communism.
Dingman treats both readings as premature verdicts and reframes the question, citing
the historian Ernest R. May's concept of a "great" or "transforming event," as an
open empirical one: did the Korean War set Japan's national life on a course it
otherwise would not have taken?

A second framing device is a historiographical asymmetry the article documents at
some length: American, European, Chinese, and Korean writers have treated the Korean
War as pivotal, while Japanese historians and analysts have paid it comparatively
little attention. Dingman cites the near-absence of the war from major
Japanese-authored surveys — only one dedicated Japanese monograph existed as of
writing (Okonogi Masao's 1986 study), the war goes unmentioned in a ten-volume
official Japanese military history, and it receives only passing mention in the
twentieth-century volume of the Cambridge History of Japan and in a major
English-language study of the Showa emperor's reign. Dingman's explanation is his
thesis: for Japan, the Pacific War of 1937-45, not the shorter and more peripheral
Korean conflict, was the era's genuinely transforming event, since it destroyed the
empire, remade the polity, and set in motion the economic and social forces the
Korean War would later accelerate rather than originate.

The stated objective is to test that thesis by tracing the Korean War's effect on
three aspects of Japan's national life — security, economy, and society and
culture — and to show that in each domain the war functioned simultaneously as
dagger and gift: it did not cause fundamental change, but it modified, hastened, and
in some respects locked in changes already under way.

### Methods and scope

This is a qualitative, narrative-historical article with no statistical apparatus.
Argument proceeds thematically across three main sections (security, economy,
society/culture) followed by a conclusion, each organized chronologically within its
theme and built from primary and secondary documentary sources rather than original
data collection. Primary materials cited include multiple volumes of the U.S.
Department of State's Foreign Relations of the United States (FRUS) series
(1946-1954), Australian Archives files on Japanese attitudes toward the war, State
Department public-opinion survey files ("Schuyler Foster Files"), and contemporary
press coverage (Contemporary Japan, the Nippon Times, the Asahi Shimbun, Fortune,
Newsweek, Life, Time, and the Japan Economic Weekly). Secondary sources cluster
heavily around a 1990 edited volume, William Nimmo's The Occupation of Japan: The
Impact of the Korean War, whose contributors (Sherwood Fine, Fujita Kuniko, Kondo
Shigekatsu, Suzuki Gengo) supply much of the article's economic evidence, alongside
monographs by William Borden (trade policy), Michael Schaller and Howard
Schonberger (the occupation), Richard Finn (the peace settlement), James Auer
(rearmament of maritime forces), Changsoo Lee and George DeVos (the Korean minority
in Japan), and Chalmers Johnson (industrial policy). Nakamura Takafusa's and Kosai
Yutaka's chapters in the Cambridge History of Japan and Japan's Ministry of Foreign
Affairs Statistical Handbook of Japan (1961) supply most of the quantitative figures
that appear in the economic section. Contemporary popular fiction — James Michener's
Korean War novels The Bridge at Toko-ri (1953) and Sayonara (1954) — is used as
cultural-historical evidence in the third section rather than as a data source.

The temporal scope reaches back to the Pacific War (1937-45) and the occupation
years (1945-52) as backdrop, treats the Korean War proper (June 1950 to the July
1953 armistice) as the core period of analysis, and follows several threads (troop
drawdowns, trade-share shifts, repatriation policy) into the late 1950s to assess
how durable the war's effects proved to be. No tables, charts, or econometric
estimates appear; all figures are narrative citations to government statistics,
archival documents, or other historians' compilations. There is no image-only table
in the source requiring separate figure extraction.

### Key findings

**Security.** Dingman argues the war did not create Japan's postwar security
architecture but forced the conversion of an emerging U.S.-Japan consensus into
formal arrangements. He documents that by 1947-50 — well before the war —
Japanese and American officials had already converged on the idea that U.S. forces
would remain in Japan after a peace treaty: Foreign Minister Ashida Hitoshi
solicited Allied views on this as early as 1947, and by May 1950 Finance Minister
Ikeda Hayato had privately told Detroit banker Joseph Dodge that Prime Minister
Yoshida Shigeru would formally request continued U.S. troop presence. The war's
distinct contributions, in Dingman's telling, were four: it made an abstract
security problem urgent (General MacArthur's 8 July 1950 order created a National
Police Reserve of more than 80,000 men, drawing more than five applicants per slot,
and a September 1950 poll found roughly 54 percent of Japanese — barely ninety
days into the fighting, in a country with a renouncing-war constitution — now
favored rearmament); it gave Yoshida political cover to face down both left and
right domestic opposition, letting the 1951 San Francisco Peace Treaty and U.S.-Japan
Security Treaty pass quickly; it reshaped foreign perceptions of Japan from defeated
enemy to Cold War partner (American public favorability toward the Japanese people
rose, per polling cited from Bernard Cohen's 1957 study, from about half of college
graduates and a third of high-school graduates in April 1949 to roughly two-thirds
and 60 percent respectively by January 1953), while wartime troop contributions from
Australia, New Zealand, and the Philippines — the states most fearful of a rearmed
Japan — drew them into cooperative relationships that eased their acceptance of the
settlement; and it locked in a large, durable American military presence whose
draw-down took years. On this last point Dingman cites U.S. Department of Defense
personnel data showing American forces in Japan reached the low 260,000s
(one figure given in text is 350,000) by 1953, when the fighting stopped, and did not
return to pre-Korean-War levels until 1958 — five years after the armistice — a
persistence Dingman attributes partly to the security treaty South Korean President
Syngman Rhee extracted from Washington (the August 1953 U.S.-ROK Mutual Defense
Treaty) as the price of accepting the truce. The article's assessment: the war
"blunted" the security threat Japanese and Americans believed Korea posed, but the
scale and duration of the resulting American presence guaranteed decades of
controversy over defense burden-sharing rather than delivering costless security.

**Economy.** This section supplies the concrete procurement figures most relevant to
questions about the scale of the Korean War boom. Dingman reports Japan's GNP grew,
on average, slightly more than 10 percent per year over the war years; an index of
industrial production (1934-36 = 100) rose from 94 in June 1950 to 142 by July 1951,
implying more than a 50 percent increase in industrial output achieved with under 10
percent more non-agricultural workers; Toyota's average wage, within a year of the
armistice, exceeded two and a half times its pre-war level; and, in the first two
years of the war, the value of Japanese exports rose 53 percent and total foreign
trade value rose 84 percent, with the shipping line Nippon Yusen Kaisha expanding
from Korea-only service to regular routes across North America, Australia, the
Persian Gulf, and South and Southeast Asia. Contemporary Japanese officials framed
the war favorably from its earliest days: Finance Minister Ikeda predicted a
favorable short-term economic effect within ten days of the outbreak, a major bank
president forecast a "colossal" flow of American military spending into Japan within
a month, and the Bank of Japan's president later called American procurement orders
"divine aid," while Toyota's president called Korean War orders "Toyota's salvation."
On the total scale of the spending, Dingman cites Chalmers Johnson's comparison of
the Korean War's economic effect on Japan to the Marshall Plan's effect on Western
Europe, and reports that between June 1950 and July 1954 the United States spent
nearly three billion dollars in Japan on war-related goods and services — a sum
Dingman describes, following Johnson, as amounting to nearly 60 percent of what
Washington had spent across all of non-Communist Europe over a comparable span.
William Borden is cited as calling the war "the decisive event" in reviving and
redirecting Japanese trade, on the grounds that war spending closed Japan's
trade-deficit gap and American Cold War pressure diverted Japanese trade away from
China and toward the United States and Southeast Asia.

Dingman's own argument qualifies this picture rather than simply repeating it. He
contends the essential design of Japan's recovery predated the war: the 1947
"priority production program" (reviving least-damaged heavy industry first,
exporting its output to fund imports), a late-1948 SCAP-Japanese plan for
self-reliant recovery, and the 1949 "Dodge Line" (Detroit banker Joseph Dodge's
program of monetary deflation, fiscal stabilization, and market-driven
rationalization) were all in place before June 1950 and, in his words, supplied the
"basic design and critical elements" the war years would build on rather than
invent. He also stresses the war's economic benefits were uneven rather than a
smooth stimulus — arriving "in spurts, with sudden jumps and dismaying slumps" as
demand for jeeps, jute and barbed wire, railroad rolling stock, and foodstuffs and
textiles rose and fell with the fighting's fortunes — and that it took until March
1951 for Japanese and American economic managers even to agree on adjustments to
the recovery plan, a compromise later judged by a Japanese economic historian to
have had "no material effect" on the country's subsequent development. He further
argues each economic "gift" carried an offsetting "dagger": renewed inflation risk;
a raw-materials price run driven by the same shipping boom that enriched Japanese
merchant lines, which raised import costs and eroded the competitiveness of
manufactured exports made from those materials; dollar inflows that substituted for,
rather than built, durable Japanese capital investment, deepening dependence on the
United States; and a broader wartime reshaping of world trade that made commerce
with Western Europe harder and pushed Japan toward less-developed Southeast Asian
markets. On Sino-Japanese trade specifically, Dingman uses trade-share data to argue
the decline usually attributed to the Korean War was already under way beforehand:
China (excluding Taiwan) accounted for roughly 14 percent of Japan's pre-Pacific-War
trade; by 1950, all of China including Taiwan and Hong Kong supplied only about 10.4
percent of Japan's trade; by 1952 that figure had fallen to just under 7 percent; and
by 1958, five years after the truce, it had fallen further to roughly 2 percent of
Japan's total world trade — a multi-decade trend, in his reading, rather than a
Korean War effect. By 1953, Dingman notes, Japan had still not returned to its own
benchmark target of 1934-36 levels of overall economic activity, and sustained,
decade-long high growth remained, in his phrase, "much more dream than reality." His
summary judgment is that the war tested and likely strengthened the capacities of
the bureaucrats, bankers, and politicians who would later build the high-growth
economy, but that on the whole it "accelerated and rendered permanent" economic
changes already forced on Japan by the Pacific War, rather than originating a new
economic trajectory of its own; he does not credit the procurement boom itself with
supplying a "formula" for the following two decades of growth.

**Society and culture.** The war affected two "alien" populations resident in Japan
since the 1930s-40s wars: ethnic Koreans and Americans in uniform. Korean residents
had numbered about 700,000 in 1937 before 1939 Diet legislation permitted forced
labor importation, swelling the community to nearly two million by August 1945; by
June 1950 the population had roughly halved from its wartime peak through voluntary
return and a harsher postwar job market. On the eve of the Korean War, Koreans and
Americans together made up barely 1.5 percent of Japan's population. The war
intensified pressure on the Korean community: more than 8,000 Koreans were forcibly
deported during the fighting, representing, per Dingman's source, roughly a quarter
of all postwar Korean deportations from Japan, and in April 1951 Yoshida told John
Foster Dulles he wanted "almost all" Koreans in Japan sent "to their home." Formal
repatriation to North Korea did not begin until December 1959, nearly a decade after
the war's outbreak, while continued cool relations with Seoul ruled out any
comparable program for those wishing to return south. On the American side, military
personnel in Japan rose from under 100,000 in June 1950 to more than 250,000 during
the war — over two and a half times the pre-war level — with the U.S. Air Force
alone operating 35 separate sites in Japan by September 1950 and the Navy adding
Atsugi as a fourth base that October. Unlike pre-war plans for a smaller, more
dispersed residual force, the wartime buildup clustered around Tokyo, Japan's
cultural and political hub, and brought a more skilled, more visibly present
population of pilots and mechanics. Dingman links this concentrated presence to the
durability of institutions and exchange programs that either began or expanded
during the war years: continuation of the English-language Armed Forces Radio
Service, the founding of International Christian University (with fundraising
organized by former Ambassador Joseph Grew), GARIOA-funded study-abroad and
technical-exchange programs, and the International House of Japan, whose
foundations John D. Rockefeller III helped lay during a January 1951 visit
accompanying Dulles. He uses James Michener's best-selling Korean War-era novels The
Bridge at Toko-ri (which reached an estimated five million readers on its original
1953 Life magazine publication) and Sayonara (1954) — both translated and widely
read in Japan — as evidence of how thoroughly this cross-cultural entanglement had
entered popular consciousness on both sides. His conclusion for this domain mirrors
the others: the war did not create Japan's Korean minority or the American military
presence, nor did it destroy the underlying myth of Japanese racial purity and
uniqueness, but it consolidated both populations' presence and, in doing so, helped
keep postwar Japanese society more open to foreign cultural influence than it might
otherwise have become.

**Overall conclusion.** Dingman characterizes the Korean War as "a secondary, not a
primary, event in modern Japanese history," subordinate to the Pacific War as the
era's true transforming event. Absent the Korean War, he argues, Japan and the
United States would still have concluded peace and security treaties and American
forces and bases would still have remained on Japanese soil — but at smaller scale.
The war's net effect across all three domains was to accelerate, confirm, and
extend forces already unleashed by 1945, leaving Japan more prosperous, more
securely (if more controversially) allied to the United States, and more
internationally engaged in its popular culture, without itself providing the
institutional design behind the growth Japan would later sustain.

### Limitations

- The article is a narrative synthesis, not an original quantitative study; all
  economic figures (GNP growth, the industrial production index, export and trade
  values, the roughly three-billion-dollar procurement total) are drawn from other
  historians' compilations or government statistical handbooks rather than computed
  independently, and Dingman does not himself calculate procurement's share of
  Japanese GNP, exports, or foreign-exchange earnings — the comparisons offered
  (e.g., to Marshall Plan spending in Europe) are cross-national dollar comparisons,
  not GDP- or export-share ratios.
- Two figures for wartime U.S. troop strength in Japan appear in the article without
  reconciliation: the main text states numbers "soared to 350,000" by 1953, while an
  endnote citing Defense Department data states forces "based in Japan, including
  Okinawa, peaked at 263,350" that same year; the discrepancy may reflect different
  geographic or command scope but is not explained in the text as OCR'd.
- The article's own historiographical premise — that Japanese historians have
  written comparatively little on the Korean War's domestic impact — means its
  economic-impact evidence leans heavily on English-language secondary sources and
  American archival collections (FRUS, Australian Archives) rather than on
  Japanese-language primary economic data or scholarship, despite the article's
  explicit interest in Japanese perspectives.
- Some interpretive claims rest on a small number of named individuals' retrospective
  statements (e.g., the Bank of Japan president's and Toyota president's later
  characterizations of procurement as decisive) rather than on systematic
  contemporaneous evidence, a limitation Dingman implicitly acknowledges by
  contrasting these "first cut" impressions with his own longer-run argument.
  Similarly, the claim that the March 1951 recovery-plan compromise had "no material
  effect" on Japan's subsequent development is attributed to an unnamed "leading
  Japanese economic historian" without the citation appearing unambiguously adjacent
  to the claim in the scanned text.
- The article's temporal focus is the Korean War era proper (1950-53) and its
  immediate aftermath through the late 1950s; it does not analyze the 1958-1960
  security-treaty consolidation or the Ikeda Income Doubling Plan in any depth, and
  offers no independent test of when, or whether, a growth-trajectory break point
  occurred — its argument is about the character and durability of the war's effects
  across security, economic, and social domains, not about identifying a statistical
  turning point in Japan's growth series.

## Abstract

Two-sector neoclassical growth model (Cass-Koopmans style, with Stone-Geary
preferences encoding Engel's law) calibrated to Japan, 1956-1990, extending
Hayashi and Prescott's (2008) framework to incorporate government policy
instruments: agricultural price and investment subsidies, and non-agricultural
industrial policy via the Fiscal Investment and Loan Program (FILP). The
calibrated model reproduces observed postwar GNP growth, the decline in
agricultural employment share, and the relative capital-labor reallocation
across sectors. Counterfactual removal of agricultural subsidies and
FILP-style industrial policy produces negligible change in aggregate output
and the capital-output ratio; a hypothetical urban-living-cost subsidy
accelerates labor migration out of agriculture without improving aggregate
output; a counterfactual labor-mobility floor (14 million workers held in
agriculture) lowers simulated 1990 output by close to 18% relative to the
benchmark. The paper attributes the structural transformation and rapid
growth primarily to sectoral total factor productivity (TFP) paths —
especially high non-agricultural TFP growth — rather than to the modeled
policy instruments themselves.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The paper situates itself within two strands of literature on Japan's
postwar development: empirical/quantitative economic history (Ohkawa and
Rosovsky, 1973; Minami, 1994) and dualistic development theory applying
Lewis (1954), Jorgenson (1961), and Ranis and Fei (1961) models to identify
Japan's "turning point" from labor abundance to labor shortage. It also
notes multi-sector growth-theory work from the 1960s-1970s (Shinkai, 1960;
Uzawa, 1961, 1963; Inada, 1963; Inada and Uzawa, 1972; Inada, Sekiguchi and
Shoda, 1993) addressing Japan's aggregate industrial development pattern.

A gap is identified: despite extensive debate over the role of targeted
industrial policy in Japan's growth (citing Johnson, 1982; Krugman, 1987;
Komiya, Okuno and Suzumura, 1988; Lee, 1993; Beason and Weinstein, 1996;
Miwa and Ramseyer, 2004), there was almost no formal quantitative
counterfactual framework for evaluating such policies, with Hayashi and
Prescott (2008) cited as the main exception. The stated objective is to
extend the Hayashi-Prescott two-sector growth model with explicit government
policy instruments, calibrate it to postwar Japanese data, and use it to run
counterfactual policy experiments assessing whether agricultural subsidies
and industrial policy were material to Japan's rapid 1956-1973 growth and
subsequent structural change.

### Methods and scope

The model is a discrete-time, two-sector (agricultural and non-agricultural)
neoclassical growth model with a representative household, competitive firms
in each sector, and a government subject to a balanced-budget constraint.

Household structure:

- Rural families (5 members: 2 in agriculture, 3 in non-agriculture) and
  urban families (4 members, all non-agricultural), following evidence in
  Hayami and Godo (2002, 2005).
- Urban residence carries a per-worker living cost, captured by a parameter
  denoted τ in the model.
- Preferences are Stone-Geary, imposing Engel's law (a subsistence level of
  the agricultural good).

Production and policy instruments:

- Firms in each sector use Cobb-Douglas technology with sector-specific TFP;
  the agricultural sector's production function also includes a fixed land
  input.
- Government instruments modeled explicitly: lump-sum and capital/labor
  income taxes; an agricultural output price subsidy; and capital-rental-cost
  subsidies for both sectors, with the non-agricultural subsidy rate
  calibrated from FILP interest-rate-subsidy data in Cargill and Yoshino
  (2003).

Solution and calibration:

- The model is detrended and reduced to a two-equation dynamic system, solved
  with a perfect-foresight shooting algorithm as in Hayashi and Prescott
  (2002, 2008).
- Calibration uses Japanese data for 1956-1990 (one model period = one year),
  targeting: a 1990 capital-output ratio of 1.87 (Hayashi and Prescott, 2003);
  a steady-state growth rate of 2%; a non-agricultural capital tax rate of
  0.35 (Mendoza, Razin and Tesar, 1994); a resulting discount factor of
  β = 0.963; a 1956 agricultural output share of 12% (used to set the
  subsistence parameter, ā = 63.2); a non-agricultural capital-share
  parameter of 0.33 (Hayashi and Prescott, 2008); an agricultural
  capital-share parameter of 0.36; and a land-share-related parameter of
  0.45 (using Hayami et al., 1975 data).
- Exogenous inputs fed into the model include sectoral TFP paths (computed
  as Solow residuals), population, employment, hours, depreciation rates,
  the government-expenditure share, tax rates, and the calibrated subsidy
  rates. These extend the Hayashi-Prescott (2008) dataset, with additional
  agricultural capital-stock series drawn from Hitotsubashi University's
  Long-Term Economic Statistics (LTES), Ohkawa and Shinohara (1979), and
  RIETI's JIP2008 database.
- After 1990, exogenous variables are held at their 1990 levels, and the
  simulation deliberately stops at 1990, excluding Japan's 1990s "Lost
  Decade."

Counterfactual experiments performed:

1. Zeroing all government subsidies (the agricultural price subsidy and both
   sectors' capital-rental subsidies), jointly and individually.
2. Introducing a hypothetical subsidy covering 30% of the cost of urban
   living.
3. Imposing a labor-mobility barrier requiring a minimum of 14 million
   agricultural workers — the mechanism used in Hayashi and Prescott (2008)
   to explain the delayed onset of Japan's growth miracle.

Four further robustness checks are described narratively only (results
stated as "available from the authors upon request," not reported in the
paper): a small open-economy variant (tradable goods, non-tradable capital);
a version without agricultural TFP growth; versions equalizing sectoral TFP
growth rates; and a version removing the family/migration-cost structure
(treating the agricultural employment share as exogenous).

### Key findings

Stylized facts of the postwar Japanese economy that the model is built to
reproduce:

1. **Output growth.** GNP growth averaged 7.4% per year between 1956 and
   1973, the year of the first oil crisis and global inflation. Growth then
   slowed to an average per-capita rate of 2.8% between 1973 and 1990.
2. **Agricultural employment share.** This fell from about 34% in the 1950s
   to roughly 12% by 1973, then continued declining more slowly to around
   6% by 1990.
3. **Relative capital-labor ratio.** The capital-labor ratio in agriculture
   rose relative to non-agriculture, linked to rapid postwar
   "mini-tractorization" — adoption of tractors under 10 horsepower (Hayami
   et al., 1975).
4. **Wage gap and slow migration.** A persistent agriculture/non-agriculture
   wage gap coexisted with labor migration that took more than 15 years,
   attributed to the rise of part-time farming: the national share of farm
   households fell 40% between 1960 and 1995, while part-time (type-2) farm
   households rose more than 20% over the same period (Hayami and Godo,
   2002); on average only about one-quarter of farm-household income came
   from agricultural activity.
5. **Sectoral TFP.** Both agricultural and non-agricultural TFP rose
   significantly until the first oil crisis, with non-agricultural TFP
   growing faster. Agricultural TFP gains are attributed to diffusion of
   technology accumulated before the war (Hayami and Ruttan, 1985);
   non-agricultural TFP gains are attributed to adoption, imitation, and
   assimilation of foreign technical know-how, with absorptive capacity
   linked to human capital accumulation (Ohkawa and Kohama, 1989; Benhabib
   and Spiegel, 2005).

Policy instruments described in the source:

- **Rice pricing.** Prices were set by the Food Agency (Shokuryo Cho) under
  a producer-procurement/consumer-sale scheme financed through the Food
  Control Special Account (Shokkan Kaikei). The procurement-sale price gap
  held near 5% between 1957 and 1960; after a 1960 revision of the
  procurement-price formula (driven by political pressure from farmers'
  organizations) tying prices to production costs, the gap rose to an
  average of 25.55% between 1962 and 1980, becoming a major source of
  government budget deficits.
- **Agricultural investment subsidies.** Direct transfers plus
  subsidized-interest production loans covered an estimated 26% of total
  agricultural investment in 1960, rising to 45% in 1970 and 58% in both
  1980 and 1990 (Hayami and Godo, 2002); roughly half of all farm investment
  after 1970 was government-subsidized.
- **Fiscal Investment and Loan Program (FILP / Zaisei Tou Yushi).**
  Channeled postal-savings and social-security surplus funds through public
  financial institutions — the Japan Development Bank, Export-Import Bank,
  Housing Loan Corporation, and Small Business Finance Corporation — and to
  public enterprises including the National Railways and Nippon Telegraph
  and Telephone, targeting industries such as sea transport, electric
  power, shipbuilding, automobiles, machinery, iron and steel, coal mining,
  and petroleum refining (Ogura and Yoshino, 1988). The paper cites Hayami
  and Godo (2005) for the claim that government-directed FILP credit
  amounted to less than ten percent of total loans to industry, and Komiya,
  Okuno and Suzumura (1988) for the view that industrial policy's
  contribution operated mainly through information-sharing dialogue between
  government and industry via committees and councils, rather than through
  direct capital allocation.

Model fit: the calibrated model reproduces the rapid pre-1973 decline in
agricultural employment share, the high 1956-1973 growth rate and its
post-1973 slowdown (with some over-prediction of early growth), and the
broad path of the capital-output ratio (stable to 1970, rising through the
1970s, stabilizing in the 1980s). Fit is described as weaker for the
agricultural capital share, relative capital per worker across sectors, and
the relative agricultural price, which is attributed partly to short-run
volatility in agricultural TFP.

Counterfactual results:

- **Removing subsidies.** Zeroing agricultural price subsidies and both
  sectors' capital-rental subsidies (jointly or individually) produces only
  a slightly higher agricultural employment share and a substantially
  higher relative agricultural price (mechanically following from removing
  the price subsidy), with no material change to aggregate output or the
  capital-output ratio.
- **Hypothetical urban-cost subsidy.** A 30%-of-cost subsidy for urban
  living produces a marked decline in the agricultural employment share
  during the transition (though not in the long run) and increases the
  agricultural capital share, but leaves aggregate output and the
  capital-output ratio essentially unchanged. This is attributed to the
  model's Engel's-law and capital-mobility assumptions, under which reduced
  agricultural labor is offset by increased agricultural capital.
- **Labor migration barrier.** A floor of 14 million agricultural workers
  produces a much higher and more slowly declining agricultural employment
  share, a lower agricultural capital share (capital reallocating to
  non-agriculture instead of labor), and a materially slower output growth
  path: simulated 1990 output is reported as nearly 18% lower than in the
  data/benchmark case. The paper characterizes this as consistent with
  Hayashi and Prescott (2008), presenting removal of such a mobility
  barrier as one of the more important contributors to Japan's postwar
  growth acceleration.

Robustness checks (narrative results only): a small open-economy variant
preserves the main qualitative conclusions; removing agricultural TFP growth
leaves results close to baseline, pointing to non-agricultural TFP growth as
the principal driver; forcing both sectors to grow at the (lower)
agricultural TFP rate produces much slower output-per-capita growth, while
forcing both to grow at the (higher) non-agricultural rate leaves
conclusions largely unchanged; and removing the family/urban-cost structure
(treating the agricultural employment share as exogenous) leaves the main
conclusions intact.

Stated conclusions: (1) agricultural price and investment subsidies, and
industrial policy in the form of FILP, have limited impact on Japan's
aggregate growth performance in the model; (2) a hypothetical urban-cost
subsidy could have accelerated agricultural-to-non-agricultural labor
migration but would not have improved aggregate performance; (3) a
counterfactual labor-mobility barrier would have produced substantially
lower GNP growth and a large negative long-run level effect, so its absence
is presented as an important contributor to the postwar miracle. The authors
state that, to the extent policy affected postwar growth, the mechanism
should be understood as operating through TFP rather than through the
specific channels modeled.

### Limitations

The authors identify two explicit caveats:

1. The model does not incorporate certain other policy interventions
   discussed in the literature, including special capital depreciation
   schemes (Ogura and Yoshino, 1988), broader public infrastructure
   investment, and agricultural trade protection; effects of such omitted
   policies are implicitly folded into the model's TFP terms rather than
   isolated.
2. Sectoral TFP is treated as exogenous throughout. The authors note this
   could be relaxed by endogenizing human-capital-driven technology
   diffusion (Benhabib and Spiegel, 2005), firm R&D decisions (Romer, 1990),
   or government agricultural research and extension activities (Rustichini
   and Schmitz, 1991), and flag a companion paper (Aoki et al., 2009) as
   pursuing this direction.

Beyond the authors' own caveats, several features of the analysis are
apparent from the document itself: the baseline analysis rests on a single
closed-economy calibration, with the open-economy case treated only as a
robustness check; the data are aggregate and national-level rather than
firm- or industry-level; the reported fit and counterfactual results depend
on a specific Stone-Geary/Cobb-Douglas functional-form and calibration-target
set whose sensitivity is not separately reported; and four of the paper's
robustness exercises are summarized narratively without the underlying
quantitative results being presented in the document.

counterfactual output effects of specific policy and labor-mobility
scenarios) for postwar Japan, 1956-1990.

## Abstract

A contemporary (1977) book review, not a study: R. S. May's assessment in *The Economic Journal* of Denison and Chung's *How Japan's Economy Grew So Fast* (Brookings, 1976). The review restates the book's headline growth-accounting decomposition of Japanese national income growth for 1953-1971, notes the book's overlap with Chapter 2 of Patrick and Rosovsky's *Asia's New Giant* (1976), and offers May's own critical assessment of the residual "advances in knowledge" category and of the book's output-per-worker comparison with the United States. Secondary source: it reports numbers from Denison and Chung rather than deriving them.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The review covers Denison and Chung's *How Japan's Economy Grew So Fast*, described as the fourth in Denison's series of growth-accounting studies, following his 1962 US study, his 1967 nine-country comparison, and his 1974 US 1929-1969 study. The book applies that same analytical framework, with methodological refinements from the 1974 US study, to Japan's growth from 1953 to 1971. May frames the book's purpose as both a study of Japan in its own right and a basis for comparing Japan's growth sources with other industrialised countries, chiefly the United States, though he flags that the comparison is hampered by mismatched time periods (European data run only to 1962).

### Methods and scope

As reported by May, the book's method is standard Denison-style growth accounting: national income growth is decomposed into five major categories of growth resources (capital inputs, labour inputs, economies of scale, improved resource allocation, and advances in knowledge), each further broken into constituent elements, with full derivations in appendices that "exceed the text in number of pages." Calculations are repeated for the non-residential business sector and for two sub-periods, 1953-1961 and 1961-1971. The book also includes an additional section, not found in the overlapping *Asia's New Giant* chapter, that decomposes why output per worker is lower in Japan than in the United States. May's own method, as reviewer, is a narrative critique of assumptions and interpretation rather than any re-estimation.

### Key findings

**The book's headline decomposition (as relayed by May).** For 1953-1971, Japan's national income grew at 8.77% per year, attributed to: capital inputs 2.10 points, advances in knowledge 1.97 points, economies of scale 1.94 points, labour inputs 1.85 points, and improved resource allocation 0.95 points. May reports this last figure — "improved resource allocation," 0.95 percentage points — as an explicit named category in the book's own accounting, not merely a residual; this is a directly citable figure for the labour/resource-reallocation contribution.

**Sub-period comparison.** The "advances in knowledge" contribution is reported as higher in 1961-1971 (2.43 points) than in 1953-1961 (1.42 points). The book offers only tentative explanations for the difference (more receptive attitudes to new methods, less worker resistance, more effective management), which May treats as unresolved.

**International comparison.** Japan's "advances in knowledge" contribution (1.97 points) is reported as exceeding the United States (0.19 points) and all ten other industrialised countries in the comparison set.

**Overlap with Asia's New Giant.** May notes that most of the book's chapter text and tables duplicate Chapter 2 of Patrick and Rosovsky's *Asia's New Giant: How the Japanese Economy Works* (Brookings, 1976), also authored by Denison and Chung, though the book under review adds the extensive appendices and the output-per-worker comparison section, neither of which appear in *Asia's New Giant*.

**Sustainability of growth.** The book concludes that only about one-third of the contributing growth elements identified would be expected to persist, implying a steady decline in Japan's growth rate over the remaining decades of the twentieth century.

**Reviewer's assessment.** May calls the book "a thorough and careful analysis" that provides "a wealth of new statistical data," and judges the underlying proportionality assumption (earnings track marginal products) reasonable given the difficulties of growth accounting. He is more critical of the "advances in knowledge" category, noting it is a residual that absorbs unmeasured effects and measurement error and cannot capture quality improvements in final products. He also flags the output-per-worker comparison as resting on simplifying assumptions he considers questionable — that education was equally appropriate to later economic participation and of similar quality in both countries — and notes the comparison is sensitive to judgments about the effect of Japan's longer working hours.

### Limitations

- This document is a book review, not the underlying study; all growth-accounting figures are the book's, only relayed (and, per May, sometimes only briefly explained) here.
- May's own critique is impressionistic and qualitative; he does not re-run or independently verify any of the book's calculations.
- The review does not explain how "improved resource allocation" is defined or estimated within the book beyond naming it as one of the five categories; the mechanism behind the 0.95-point figure would need to be read from the book itself.
- Scan quality: OCR of the numeric decomposition (e.g., "2 I 0%", "I 97%") required interpretation as 2.10%, 1.97%, etc.; figures are reported here as read, consistent with the sum being close to the stated 8.77% total.

## Abstract

Traces Japan's economic trajectory from wartime collapse (1945) through the end of high-speed growth
(the 1973 oil shock).

- Scope: Occupation-era institutional reform; macroeconomic growth conditions; industrial-structure transformation; enterprise and financial-system characteristics; government economic policy.
- Method: narrative synthesis of official Japanese statistical series and secondary economic-history scholarship, organized around twenty numbered data tables; no new data collected.
- Endpoint: the 1971-73 exchange-rate shocks, the 1973 OPEC price increase, and the inflation that closed the high-growth era.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The chapter opens by stating that Japan's real GNP grew at an average annual rate of 9.6 percent
from 1946 to 1973 — a figure derived by linking and recalculating discontinuous national-income
series, since measurement standards changed over the period. It frames the period in two parts: an
initial decade of recovery from wartime devastation, and a subsequent era of "rapid economic growth"
(kodo seicho jidai) running from roughly 1955 to 1972.

Real GNP per capita had fallen to 55 percent of the 1934-6 level by 1946 and did not regain that
level until 1953. The chapter notes that, despite Japan's dramatic postwar rebound relative to its
own collapse, it actually took longer for Japan's per-capita GNP to recover to prewar levels than it
did in Western Europe: in 1951 Japanese per capita national income stood at one-twelfth that of the
United States and two-fifths that of West Germany.

The high-growth era is subdivided into 1955-65 (three booms: the "Jimmu boom," the "Iwato boom," and
the "boom that did not feel like one") and 1966-72 (steady growth ending in the 1973 "oil shock" and
subsequent "crazy inflation," kyoran infure). By 1975 Japanese per-capita GNP had reached 62 percent
of the U.S. level and 65.9 percent of West Germany's, having overtaken Britain and Italy.

The chapter's stated objective is to trace how this growth trajectory was produced: through
Occupation-era institutional reform, a sequence of government stabilization and industrial policies,
favorable macroeconomic conditions (labor, savings, demand), a restructuring of industry toward
heavy/chemical and then export-oriented production, distinctive Japanese enterprise practices, and a
two-track government policy mixing macroeconomic orthodoxy with sectoral intervention — closing with
an account of how that policy mix contributed to the inflation and balance-of-payments dynamics that
ended high growth in 1973.

### Methods and scope

The chapter is a narrative synthesis rather than an original empirical study. Its sources include:

- Official Japanese government statistical compilations, principally the Economic Planning Agency's (Keizai kikakucho) *Gendai Nihon keizai no tenkai* (1976), Toyo Keizai Shinposha's *Showa kokusei soran* (1980), and Ministry of Finance (Okurasho) publications including *Showa zaiseishi*.
- OECD National Accounts data and Japanese Economic Planning Agency income-distribution studies.
- Japanese-language secondary scholarship, including Kosai Yutaka, Komiya Ryutaro, Miyazaki Giichi, Tachibanaki Yoshiaki, Tsuruta Toshimasa, and Ueno Hiroya.
- English-language works, including R. P. Dore's *Land Reform in Japan* (1959), Eleanor M. Hadley's *Antitrust in Japan* (1959), and Chalmers Johnson's *MITI and the Japanese Miracle* (1982).

Twenty numbered data tables (10.1-10.20) are presented, covering: wholesale/consumer price indices
(10.1); population and employment by sector (10.2); labor-force participation and unemployment
(10.3); wartime damage to national wealth by asset category (10.4); surviving productive capacity at
defeat (10.5); international balance of payments by component (10.6); national income distribution
by income type (10.7); "middle-class consciousness" survey results (10.8); demand-item growth rates
(10.9); urban household consumption patterns (10.10); consumer-durable diffusion rates (10.11);
technology-import volumes and payments (10.12); trade/foreign-exchange liberalization rates (10.13);
heavy- versus light-industry production and exports (10.14); major export items by value (10.15);
major import items by value (10.16); flow of funds in 1965 (10.17); labor unions and labor disputes
(10.18); inter-firm wage differentials by enterprise size and age (10.19); and the Fiscal Investment
and Loan Program budget (10.20).

All tables are reproduced as text in the source PDF (an OCR-scanned document); no chart, plot, or
image-only figure was present requiring separate visual analysis.

Topical scope runs chronologically from the immediate postwar collapse (1945-6) through the 1973 oil
shock and yen float, organized into five thematic sections: postwar economic reform and recovery;
macroeconomic performance; the changing industrial structure; characteristics of Japanese
enterprises; and economic policy.

### Key findings

#### Aggregate growth trajectory

- The chapter's headline figure: Japan's real GNP grew at an average annual rate of 9.6 percent over the period 1946 to 1973 — stated by the chapter as a recalculated figure obtained by linking discontinuous national-income series across changing measurement standards (old and new SNA).
- Separately, the chapter gives a rounder figure of approximately 10 percent annual growth for the wider 1945-1973 span, distinct from the precise 9.6 percent recalculated figure it attributes specifically to 1946-1973.
- Real GNP per capita fell to 55 percent of the 1934-6 level in 1946 and did not regain that level until 1953.
- In 1951, Japanese per capita national income (at prevailing exchange rates) stood at one-twelfth that of the United States and two-fifths that of West Germany.
- By 1975, Japanese per-capita GNP had reached 62 percent of the U.S. level and 65.9 percent of West Germany's, having overtaken Britain and Italy.

#### Occupation-era institutional reforms

- Land reform: before reform, 45.9 percent of Japan's agricultural land was tenanted. A December 9, 1945 Occupation memorandum prompted the "second land reform," enacted October 21, 1946.
- The reform limited landholding, mandated government purchase of absentee landlords' holdings, and required resale to tenant farmers.
- Results: 1.916 million hectares (37.5 percent of agricultural acreage) changed hands; land was purchased from over 3.7 million landlords; tenanted land fell to only 10 percent of agricultural land.
- Prewar tenancy disputes had peaked at six thousand in 1935, and tenant rent had amounted to about 5 percent of national income; the chapter reports that tenant disputes effectively disappeared after the reform.
- Zaibatsu dissolution: by June 1947, 83 companies were designated as holding companies (28 reorganized as family holding companies).
- The Mitsui and Mitsubishi Trading Companies were ordered dissolved on July 3, 1947; the April 1947 Anti-Monopoly Law established the Fair Trade Commission.
- Under the December 1947 Law for the Elimination of Excessive Economic Concentration, 18 large firms were split apart — e.g., Japan Steel into Yawata Steel and Fuji Steel; Mitsubishi Heavy Industries into three firms; Tokyo Shibaura Electric divested 27 of 43 plants; Hitachi divested 19 of 35 plants.
- Several later recombined: Mitsubishi Trading Company (1954), Mitsui Trading Company (1959), Mitsubishi Heavy Industries (1964), New Japan Steel (1970) — reconstituting as bank-centered keiretsu rather than zaibatsu-style holding companies.
- The chapter notes that firms with no prewar zaibatsu affiliation — including Toyota, Hitachi, New Japan Steel, Kawasaki Steel, Sumitomo Metals, Honda, Sony, and Matsushita — grew into major postwar enterprises, which it associates with a more competitive industrial structure following dissolution.
- Labor reform rested on three 1945-47 laws: the Labor Union Law, the Labor Relations Adjustment Law, and the Labor Standards Law.
- By 1948, industrial-sector unionization exceeded 50 percent (6.677 million enrolled); a planned February 1, 1947 general strike was banned by Occupation authorities; a 1948 ordinance (following a letter from General MacArthur) ended public employees' right to strike.
- The routinized "spring offensive" (shunto) wage-bargaining pattern began in 1955.

#### Postwar inflation and stabilization

- Wholesale prices rose from 16.27 times the 1934-6 average in 1946 to 246.8 times in 1950 (Table 10.1); consumer prices rose from 50.6 times to 219.9 times over the same span.
- Economist Arisawa Hiromi's "priority production method" concentrated capital and materials first in coal, then steel, then reinvested steel output into coal production; it became government policy in 1947-8, implemented via the newly created Reconstruction Finance Bank.
- The 1947 coal-output target of 30 million tons was barely met; steel production was only 740,000 tons in 1947, versus 100 million tons in the 1970s.
- In March 1949, U.S. advisor Joseph Dodge set out a five-point stabilization program: a balanced budget, termination of Reconstruction Finance Bank lending, reduced subsidies/price controls, a fixed ¥360/$1 exchange rate, and a return to private-channel trade.
- The Dodge line governed policy for roughly the next two and a half years, and is described as establishing the balanced-budget, orthodox-finance side of policy that persisted through the high-growth period.
- The initial postwar reparations policy, recommended in the December 1945 Pauley Report, called for removing equipment from Japan's war-related industries and drastically reducing capacity in defense-linked sectors such as steel, machine-tool production, and merchant shipping; these goals were subsequently modified as U.S. policy shifted from punitive measures toward economic rehabilitation, which the chapter treats as a precondition for the retention of the heavy/chemical industry base that the priority production method later relied on.

#### Labor supply and employment

- Population reached 78.101 million in a 1947 special census, up from 71.933 million in 1940; the total employment rate had declined from 45.2 percent to 42.7 percent of population over the same span.
- The working population grew 2.6 percent annually between 1948 and 1955, then slowed to 1.3 percent annually between 1955-65 and 1965-73 (Table 10.3).
- Agricultural employment fell by 1.7 million (1945-55), 4 million (1955-65), and 4.2 million (1965-75); manufacturing employment rose by 4.6 million between 1965 and 1975.
- Full employment was reached by 1967, when job openings first exceeded job seekers economy-wide.
- Compulsory education was extended from six to nine years during this period, which the chapter links to the improving intellectual quality of the labor force alongside the shift from short-tenure female textile workers toward long-tenure skilled male workers in iron, steel, and machinery.

#### Savings, capital, and the balance-of-payments constraint

- Wartime damage destroyed 25.3 percent of national wealth overall (¥64,278 million of ¥253,130 million, Table 10.4), but damage was highly uneven by asset class: shipping lost 80.6 percent of its prewar value, versus only 9.8 percent for railroad and rolling stock and 10.8 percent for electrical/gas equipment.
- Surviving productive capacity relative to 1941 levels (Table 10.5) likewise varied by sector: hydroelectric power stood at 116.1 percent, ordinary steel materials at 107.1 percent, and aluminum at 115.3 percent, while petroleum refining stood at only 61.2 percent, cotton-spinning capacity at 20.2 percent, and cotton-weaving capacity at 31.4 percent — i.e., heavy-industry capacity was comparatively preserved while textile capacity was comparatively depleted.
- Urban household consumption fell from over 100 percent of income in 1946-7 to 90.8 percent by 1955, near the 1934-6 average of 88.3 percent, while the savings rate rose to roughly twice the prewar level.
- Investment was financed through domestic (chiefly individual) savings rather than foreign capital.
- Balance-of-payments deficits forced tight-money responses in 1954, 1957, 1961, and 1963 (Table 10.6).
- After 1965 a current-account surplus emerged and grew; foreign-exchange reserves rose from $900 million (1952) to $1.8 billion (1960) to $2 billion (1967) to $18.3 billion (1972).
- The yen was revalued to ¥308/$1 in late 1971 and floated in February 1973.

#### Price trends and income distribution

- After Korean War-driven inflation subsided by 1954, prices were comparatively stable in 1955-60, followed by a "productivity-gap" pattern after 1960 in which wholesale/export prices stayed stable (under the fixed exchange rate) while consumer prices in labor-intensive sectors rose.
- By fall 1973, wholesale prices had risen over 20 percent year-on-year even before the OPEC price increase.
- National income share going to employees rose from 30.7 percent (1946) to 56.0 percent (1965) and 54.3 percent (1970); self-employed income share fell from 65.4 percent (1946) to 20.0 percent (1970); corporation income share fluctuated, reaching 15.8 percent by 1970 (Table 10.7).
- Citing Tachibanaki's calculation from Sawyer's OECD data, the chapter reports Gini coefficients for gross income distribution of 0.335 for Japan, versus 0.356 (Sweden), 0.396 (West Germany), 0.404 (United States), and an OECD average of 0.366 — described as the most equal distribution among advanced economies of the period.
- Self-reported "middle-class" identification rose from 72 percent (1958, combining "upper" through "lower middle") to 87 percent (1964 and 1967) (Table 10.8).

#### Consumption and demand

- Black-and-white television ownership rose from 7.8 percent (1957) to 95.0 percent (1965) of nonfarm households; refrigerators from 2.8 to 68.7 percent; washing machines from 20.1 to 78.1 percent (Table 10.11).
- Later in the period: color television ownership rose from 2.2 percent (1967) to 90.9 percent (1975); automobile ownership from 11 percent to 37.4 percent; air conditioners from 2.6 to 21.5 percent.
- The Engels coefficient (food share of household spending) fell from 44.5 percent (1955) to 32.4 percent (1970) (Table 10.10).
- Between 1955 and 1970, the number of employees rose from 18.17 to 33.39 million and the urban population share rose from 56.3 to 72.1 percent.
- The rate of students continuing education beyond the compulsory level rose from 47.4 percent (1955) to 79.4 percent (1970) and to 91.9 percent (1975); college/university entrance rates rose from 17.2 percent (1955) to 24.2 percent (1970) and 38.4 percent (1975).

#### Industrial rationalization and technology absorption

- The first steel-industry rationalization plan (early 1950s) had projected ¥63 billion in investment (1951-54) but achieved ¥120 billion by 1953, including Kawasaki Steel's new integrated waterfront mill in Chiba (1953).
- The second rationalization plan (1955-65) used over ¥500 billion in investment; by its conclusion, Japan's iron and steel output ranked second only to the United States, ahead of France, Germany, and Britain, and first in pure-oxygen-converter efficiency.
- The government's toolkit for targeted industries included the Japan Development Bank (established March 1951), the 1952 Enterprises Rationalization Act, special tax measures, subsidized interest for shipbuilding, import quotas, foreign-capital controls, and "administrative guidance."
- The chapter explicitly states that final investment decisions remained with individual business firms, and cautions against characterizing the period as a "planned economy" or the government as an "economic general staff."
- Technology imports rose from 1,141 items and $69 million in payments (1949-55) to 10,789 items and $3.205 billion (1971-75) (Table 10.12), acquired through licensing/technical tie-ups (e.g., Nissan-Austin, Isuzu-Hillman, NEC-RCA, Toyo Rayon-DuPont) rather than direct foreign capital investment, which Japan did not encourage.
- The government layered sector-specific measures on top of general rationalization policy, including the 1955 Petrochemical Industry Development Policy and the 1956 Temporary Measure for the Promotion of the Machine Tool Industry.

#### Energy transition in heavy industry

- The coal industry, despite wartime and Korean War-era profitability, failed to achieve cost reductions through rationalization investment because of poor domestic coal-deposit quality; a lengthy coal strike in fall 1952 accelerated a shift by key industries toward oil and electric power.
- In the electric power industry, hydroelectric generation (7.48 million kilowatts in 1955, versus 4.1 million kilowatts of thermal capacity) was overtaken by thermal generation by 1961 (9.444 million kilowatts hydro versus 9.75 million kilowatts thermal); thermal plant capacity additions rose from 58,000 kilowatts (1958) to 2.2 million (1960) to 3.75 million (1965).
- In the chemical industry, a carbide-based production chain reliant on hydroelectric power (supplying vinyl chloride and acetate production) was supplanted in the late 1950s by a new petrochemical industry using imported naphtha and naphtha-cracking technology to produce polyethylene and polystyrene.
- Falling world crude oil prices, new Middle East oil field discoveries, and the introduction of high-speed supertankers further reduced the cost of imported energy relative to domestic coal.

#### Automobile mass production and its spillovers

- Around 1950, policy debate over whether to rely on automobile imports or to develop a protected domestic industry was resolved in favor of domestic development, pursued partly through technical tie-ups with foreign manufacturers.
- Toyota's 1955 five-year modernization plan initially targeted monthly production of only three thousand vehicles; by around 1960, new compact-car plants built by Toyota (Motomachi) and Nissan (Oppama) each targeted monthly production of ten thousand vehicles, supported by new mass-production equipment such as transfer machines.
- Mass automobile production drove related-industry growth: thin-plate steel production and strip-mill investment in the iron and steel industry, and expansion of the specialty steel and machine-tool industries — machine-tool output value rose twenty-fold, from ¥5 billion (1955) to ¥100 billion (1962).
- In 1956, Toyota and its subcontracting network began using the kanban method (also called "zero inventory" or "just-in-time" production); subcontractors financed mechanization partly by purchasing secondhand machinery from parent companies.

#### Trade, exchange, and capital liberalization

- The liberalization rate for foreign trade/exchange rose from 40 percent (March 1960) to 88 percent (October 1962), reaching 97 percent by April 1967 (Table 10.13), following the June 1960 Plan for the Liberalization of Foreign Trade and Foreign Exchange.
- Japan became an IMF Article 8 country in 1963 and joined the OECD in 1964.
- Capital liberalization proceeded in stages from 1967 (50 percent in 33 industries, 100 percent in 17 others including shipbuilding and steel) through 1974 (autos in 1971, computers/real estate in 1974).
- Heavy-industry production overtook light-industry production by 1955 (Table 10.14); by 1965 heavy industry exported a higher share of its output than light industry.
- Automobile exports reached 1.78 million units in 1971, a 63.7 percent increase over the prior year.
- Domestic energy self-sufficiency fell from 76 percent (1955) to 33.8 percent (1965) to 16.5 percent (1970), with imports making up the remainder.
- Prime Minister Ikeda Hayato's Income Doubling Plan (announced around 1960), proposing to double national income within ten years, is credited with accelerating the modernization-investment boom of the early 1960s, alongside the announcement of trade/exchange liberalization plans.
- In the mid-1960s a wave of large-scale bankruptcies occurred, most notably the management crisis of the Yamaichi Securities Company; the Bank of Japan extended special loans to avert a broader financial crisis, after which growth continued, increasingly sustained by exports.
- Rising export competitiveness produced trade friction with the United States, leading Japan to impose voluntary export restrictions on iron and steel in 1968 and on textiles in 1971.
- Capital-liberalization pressure in the late 1960s coincided with a wave of major corporate mergers: Mitsubishi Heavy Industries (1964), Nissan and Prince Motor (1965), the Nissho and Iwai trading companies (1968), Yawata and Fuji Steel into New Japan Steel (1970), and the Daiichi and Nihon Kangyo banks (1971).
- Export-value growth by commodity (Table 10.15, in ¥100 million units) illustrates the shift toward heavy-industry exports: iron and steel rose from 1,397 (1960) to 10,237 (1970) to 30,165 (1975); automobiles rose from 281 (1960) to 4,815 (1970) to 18,392 (1975); ships rose from 1,037 (1960) to 5,075 (1970) to 17,803 (1975).

#### Demand growth by component (Table 10.9)

- Annual growth rates varied markedly by demand component and sub-period: gross national expenditure grew 9.4 percent (1946-50), 8.9 percent (1950-55), 8.5 percent (1955-60), 10.0 percent (1960-65), 12.3 percent (1965-70), and 5.0 percent (1970-75).
- Export growth was 99.5 percent annually in 1946-50 (from a near-zero postwar base), then 18.1 percent (1950-55), 16.9 percent (1955-60), 13.2 percent (1960-65), and 17.1 percent (1965-70).
- Private enterprise plant-facility investment growth peaked at 22.2 percent annually in both 1955-60 and 1965-70, before falling to 1.1 percent in 1970-75.
- Public capital formation growth ran from 49.2 percent (1946-50, off a low base) down to roughly 10-16 percent annually through the 1950s-60s.
- Final private consumption growth ran steadily between about 6 and 10 percent annually across every sub-period from 1946 to 1975, the least volatile of the demand components reported.

#### Enterprise organization and finance

- Per Miyazaki Giichi's 1970 data, 57.1 percent of limited stock companies were controlled by other corporations, 20.7 percent by managers, and only 20.5 percent by individual stockholders — reflecting keiretsu cross-shareholding structured around city banks.
- Miyazaki's "one-set hypothesis" holds that bank-led enterprise groups competed to acquire affiliated firms across every industry, producing intense investment competition; an alternative view holds that city banks conducted profitable "credit rationing" toward heavy/chemical-industry borrowers while cooperating with government policy to keep interest rates low.
- Exceptions to the keiretsu/institutional-ownership pattern existed among growth-industry leaders that remained founder- or family-controlled, including Matsushita, Sony, Honda, Toyota, Toyo Kogyo, Suntory, Kyoto Ceramics (Kyocera), and Daiei.
- After 1965 in particular, the ratio of equity capital to total assets rose among Japanese enterprises, which needed higher capitalization to secure loans even while remaining heavily dependent on borrowed capital.
- A 1965 flow-of-funds table (Table 10.17) shows individuals with ¥2.6 trillion in savings surplus over investment (depositing ¥2.7 trillion), while enterprises borrowed ¥4.0 trillion in loans against only ¥1.1 trillion of self-generated savings surplus — the "indirect financing" model, in which bank deposits intermediated household savings into corporate investment.
- Labor practices — lifetime employment, seniority wages, enterprise unions — applied to roughly 30 percent of employees, concentrated in large firms.
- Wage differentials between large and small enterprises narrowed for younger workers as the labor market tightened (e.g., for firms with 10-99 employees, the wage index for 20-24-year-olds, large-firm base = 100, rose from 89.7 in 1961 to 114.1 in 1965) but remained wide for older workers.
- A 1976 survey found 82.2 percent of large manufacturers relied on outside subcontractor suppliers, and 58.1 percent of small/medium manufacturers had subcontracting relationships with large firms; Toyota's "just-in-time" supply system is cited as a case of this arrangement.

#### Labor unions and disputes (Table 10.18)

- The number of registered labor unions rose from 32,012 (1955) to 65,448 (1973); union membership rose from 6.166 million to 12.098 million over the same period, with an estimated organization rate fluctuating between about 31.5 percent (1959) and 37.2 percent (1955).
- The number of labor disputes rose from 1,345 (1955) to a period peak of 9,459 (1973); the number of participants in disputes rose from 3.748 million (1955) to 14.549 million (1973).
- Workdays lost to disputes fluctuated without a clear trend, from a high of 6.052 million days (1958) down to a low of 1.83 million days (1967) and back up to 6.029 million days (1971).

#### Economic policy structure and its evolution

- The chapter characterizes 1950s-60s policy as combining "small government" macroeconomic orthodoxy — balanced budgets under the 1949 Fiscal Law, no public bond issuance, a target of tax revenue not exceeding 20 percent of national income per a 1960 commission — with interventionist sectoral/industrial policy aimed at catch-up growth.
- As the balance-of-payments constraint eased in the late 1960s, fiscal policy loosened: the government began issuing public bonds after the 1965 recession.
- Fiscal Investment and Loan Program (FILP) total allocations grew from ¥299.8 billion (1955, in units of ¥100 million = 2,998) to ¥9.31 trillion (1975, = 93,100 units), shifting emphasis from key industries/export promotion toward housing and welfare facilities (Table 10.20).
- Within FILP, the housing allocation grew roughly 48-fold, from ¥41.5 billion (1955) to ¥1.997 trillion (1975), while the key-industries allocation grew only about sixfold, from ¥47.1 billion to ¥276.4 billion, over the same span — a relative reallocation from industrial financing toward social infrastructure.
- Prime Minister Tanaka Kakuei's 1972 "Plan to Reconstruct the Japanese Archipelago" is identified as marking the peak of this fiscal liberation, contributing to land-price and consumer-price inflation.
- Foreign-exchange reserves rose from $2 billion (1967) to $18.3 billion (1972); the Bank of Japan alternated tight- and loose-money responses, but sustained money-supply growth of about 20 percent annually (1970-73) combined with the fixed exchange rate to generate inflation.
- The chapter concludes that Japan revalued the yen too late relative to the change in its balance-of-payments position, and that this policy misstep, compounded by the 1973 OPEC price increase (a fourfold rise in crude oil prices) and the shift to a floating exchange rate in February 1973, brought the high-growth era to a close.

### Limitations

- The chapter is an interpretive historical synthesis of secondary and official statistical sources rather than an original quantitative analysis; it does not present regression results, formal growth-accounting decompositions, or counterfactual estimates.
- Several data series are explicitly flagged within the text as discontinuous or methodologically inconsistent: national income/GNP figures required linking and recalculating across changing measurement standards (old vs. new SNA), and a note attached to the labor-force table states that the statistics are not continuous, making accurate comparison difficult.
- The chapter relies heavily on Japanese-language government compilations, some from the 1970s-80s, whose underlying methodology is not detailed in the excerpted text.
- The chapter explicitly notes that scholarly interpretation of Japan's industrial policy is contested, citing several conflicting Japanese-language assessments without resolving the disagreement, and directly cautions against two popular characterizations — "Japan Incorporated" and "planned economy" — as overstatements of the government's role relative to that of private firms.
- Some figures in the underlying export/import commodity tables show internal inconsistencies attributable to OCR/column-alignment artifacts in the source scan; where such ambiguity was identified during review, the affected figures were omitted from this summary rather than reported with uncertain attribution.
- As discussed under Citation above, the source PDF as supplied contains only the chapter body (pages 494-537 of visible text) with no title page, table of contents, or contributor list; author, book title, and year were confirmed via the project source ledger rather than the document's own front matter, and the volume editor remains unspecified.

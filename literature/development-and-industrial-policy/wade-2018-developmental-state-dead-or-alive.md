# The Developmental State: Dead or Alive?

## Citation

Wade, R. H. (2018). The developmental state: Dead or alive? *Development and Change,
49*(2), 518–546. https://doi.org/10.1111/dech.12381

Publisher URL: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/dech.12381

## Abstract

Essay-length "Debate" contribution closing a *Development and Change* Forum on
financialization and economic development. Contrasts the mainstream neoliberal account of
East Asian capitalist growth (South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, with reference to Japan)
against the "developmental state" account, arguing the latter better explains the catch-up
decades. Reviews claims that these states have since converted to close-to-neoliberal
governance and rebuts them, arguing the developmental state has "evolved" rather than
died. Draws on historical narrative, secondary literature, author interviews/fieldwork
anecdotes, and selected macro data (investment ratios, growth-rate records,
capital-formation shares) rather than a formal empirical test. Closes with policy
guidelines for present-day middle-income countries and four open questions about global
economic governance.

## Summary

### Background and objective

Wade frames a shift in mainstream prescription for developing-country catch-up: before the
1980s the state was assigned a leading role in "governing the market"; from the 1980s the
World Bank, IMF, OECD/DAC and most UN development agencies moved toward a
"framework-providing" role for the state in a deregulated, maximally open economy — the
Washington (or "Western") Consensus. He traces this ideological shift to Hayek's writings
and to statements by Western business and political figures (e.g., Percy Barnevik of ABB,
Bernard Arnault of LVMH, Margaret Thatcher), which he characterizes as expressing a
"neoliberal mindset" prioritizing exit over loyalty and minimal state direction of
resource allocation.

Against this backdrop, some East Asian capitalist economies grew fast enough in the 1980s
to appear on track to reach "developed" status within a foreseeable horizon. Two rival
explanations emerged: mainstream economists credited adherence to the Western/neoclassical
prescription; other scholars (political scientists, political economists, historians)
credited "the developmental state." The essay's stated objective is to compare these two
explanations for the East Asian catch-up decades, concluding in favor of the
developmental-state account for that period, and then to assess what happened afterward —
specifically, claims that South Korea, Taiwan and Singapore have since transformed into
close-to-neoliberal states. Wade argues they have adapted and evolved but have not become
neoliberal, retaining market-steering "societal mission" roles beyond neoliberal limits,
and closes by drawing lessons for other developing countries today.

### Methods and scope

The piece is a discursive, non-empirical review/debate essay rather than a study with an
original data set or formal test. It synthesizes existing literature (political economy,
economic history, and mainstream economics), the author's own earlier fieldwork-based work
(South Korea, Taiwan, and inside the World Bank), personal communications with World Bank
staff (2010, 2014) and a Taiwanese central bank official (1999), and selected published
statistics — country income ratios (Table 1: income as % of US real average income, 1990
Geary-Khamis dollars, 1965 and 2005), gross capital formation as % of GDP (Table 2: China,
East Asia & Pacific minus China, and other low/middle-income countries, for 1970, 1990,
2012, and 1980–2014 average), and investment/GDP time series for Taiwan and Korea. The
core geographic focus is South Korea, Taiwan and Singapore ("the East Asian three"), with
Japan treated as an antecedent model and Hong Kong explicitly set aside as a 150-year
British colony with a different trajectory. Comparator cases used contrastively include
the Philippines, Chile, India, DR Congo, Greece, the UK, Germany, New Zealand, the US and
China. The essay explicitly acknowledges that "neither side has produced 'knock-out
evidence'" in the mainstream-vs-developmental-state debate (citing Rodrik, 2012) and that
rigorous causal testing is hampered by the lack of exogenous sources of variation.

### Key findings

**Mainstream account and its institutional history.** The World Bank's *East Asian
Miracle* (1993) study covered eight "high-performing Asian economies" — Japan, South
Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, Thailand, Malaysia, Indonesia and Hong Kong — and concluded that
governments contributed mainly by securing "the fundamentals" (low inflation, competitive
exchange rates, human capital, sound financial systems, low price distortions, foreign
technology, low bias against agriculture), while "strategic" interventions promoting
specific industries "generally did not work" (World Bank, 1993: 354); the Bank's takeaway
was that "openness to international trade, based on largely neutral incentives, was the
critical factor" (ibid.: 292). Wade traces the study's origin to Japan's Ministry of
Finance (MOF) objecting to the Bank's dismissal of directed credit — which MOF viewed as
central to the postwar "Japanese miracle" — and to Japan, as the Bank's number-two
shareholder, funding the study with US$1.2 million. After publication, the Bank announced
the matter settled and redirected research effort toward promoting market liberalization
elsewhere, especially Latin America and Africa. A later episode is cited: Justin Yifu Lin,
appointed World Bank Chief Economist in 2008 (its first from a non-G7 country), tried to
reopen space for selective industrial policy via a "Competitive Industries Program," but
faced internal resistance; Lin himself estimated fewer than 10 percent of Bank economists
were sympathetic, and the program was later closed by his successors' appointees.

**Developmental-state model, as inferred from the three cases.** The concept originates
with Chalmers Johnson's 1982 study of Japan (coining "developmental state"). Wade
characterizes an inferred elite consensus around: (1) high priority on fast, sustained
growth to catch up within decades; (2) very high investment/GDP ratios; (3) state
coordination of the catch-up strategy, promoting some sectors ahead of others; (4) curbing
consumption growth to free resources for investment; (5) strenuously promoting exports
while also managing trade to concentrate imports on capital/intermediate goods rather than
free trade. Institutionally, this model featured centralized bureaucratic power (e.g.,
Japan's MOF and MITI), non-independent central banks (Taiwan's central bank governor held
cabinet rank), large state-owned banking presences, tight capital controls, and "embedded
autonomy" (Evans, 1995) — close state-business ties combined with capacity to discipline
capital. Wade contrasts this with the Bank of England, which he describes as also
"embedded" and autonomous but oriented toward exposing UK firms to global competition
rather than steering production — illustrating that embedded autonomy is necessary but not
sufficient for a developmental state. He notes labor was largely excluded from this
"embedding" in Korea and Taiwan before democratization in the late 1980s. Policy
instruments included directed credit, fiscal incentives (e.g., tax rebates tied to
frontier technology), trade protection paired with tariff-rebate/duty-drawback systems for
exporters, and hard bargaining with inbound multinationals (e.g., local content
requirements). A distinguishing feature versus other protectionist regimes (e.g., India's
long-unconditional protection of Ambassador-model automobiles, or pre-1984 New Zealand)
was that East Asian assistance carried performance conditions and was withdrawn once
producers approached import price/quality parity.

**Political settlement and "luck."** Shared features across the three cases: centralized,
often authoritarian rule during fast-industrialization decades (Taiwan one-party until the
late 1980s; Korea military rule until the late 1980s; Singapore a long-standing
near-one-party state; Japan near-one-party rule for most of the period since 1955); land
reform that removed landed-elite opposition (contrasted with the Philippines, where no
significant reform occurred); and dense top-down penetration of society (e.g., Taiwan's
mandatory industry associations such as the Taiwan Feather Exporters Association; Korea's
Saemaul Undong village movement, headquartered in the Blue House, combining local
development projects with political socialization). Wade lists several "lucky" conditions
of timing and location: proximity to communist adversaries, which he says supplied
elite/popular cohesion and drew heavy US support — Taiwan received roughly US$600 million
per year (2016 dollars) in US financial transfers from 1951–1965, described as the largest
per-capita aid flows in the world at the time, compared with much smaller flows to the
Philippines; absence of a natural-resource curse (contrasted with DR Congo, whose
paved-road network fell from about 112,000 km in 1949 to about 1,000 km by 2004); an
early-mover position in labor-intensive manufacturing before China, Vietnam and other
populous low-cost entrants; and industrialization occurring before global norms and
institutions (WTO, free-trade/investment-treaty regimes, rising demands for political
participation) constrained state intervention as heavily as today.

**Quantified economic effects.** Investment/GDP ratios: Taiwan rose from 14% (1954) to 21%
(1964) to about 40% (1974); Korea rose from 10% (1960) to 36% (late 1970s, peak of the
Heavy and Chemical Industries drive), dipped, then reached 39% (1991–96); by comparison
the UK figure for 1990 was 19% and the US figure 17% (cited via Pirie, 2017). Table 2
gross capital formation as % of GDP: China 33% (1970), 36% (1990), 47% (2012), 40%
(1980–2014 average); East Asia & Pacific minus China 23%, 32%, 30%, 28% for the same
years/period; other low- and middle-income countries minus East Asia 22%, 22%, 25%, 23%.
Table 1 shows country income as % of US real average income (1990 Geary-Khamis dollars):
Japan rose from 45% (1965) to 75% (2005); Taiwan from 15% to 65%; South Korea from 15% to
60%; Malaysia from 15% to 30%; Indonesia stayed at 10%. Growth-duration records: Taiwan
sustained continuous growth above 6% per year for 32 years (1962–94), a world record until
2010; South Korea for 29 years (1962–91); China broke the record in 2010 with 33
continuous years above 6% (citing Pritchett and Summers, 2014). A World Bank (2013) study
found only 13 of 101 countries classified "middle-income" in 1960 had reached
"high-income" status by 2008 (Equatorial Guinea, Greece, Hong Kong, Ireland, Israel,
Japan, Mauritius, Portugal, Puerto Rico, South Korea, Singapore, Spain, Taiwan). Wade
cites Nathan Lane's (2017) input-output analysis of Korea's Heavy and Chemical Industries
(HCI) drive (formal period 1973–1979, prompted by the security concerns following the
c.1970 US troop drawdown under Nixon, ended after President Park's 1979 assassination):
targeted industries grew faster in output and productivity than non-targeted industries
relative to the pre-1973 period; these differences persisted after the policy's major
elements ended in 1979; and downstream sectors with strong input-output linkages to
targeted sectors grew faster than weakly linked sectors, via lower input prices, while
evidence for upstream sectors was more mixed.

**The "death" debate.** Iain Pirie (2017) is presented as the main proponent of the
"developmental state is dead" position, arguing that the shift from capital scarcity to
capital abundance removed the basic rationale for state-led capital mobilization; he is
quoted (via short paraphrase) as concluding the developmental state is "dead" once capital
allocation and capital-labor relations are largely marketized. Supporting figures cited:
Taiwan's investment/GDP fell from about 32% (1974–81) to 24% (1982–88) to 22% (2001–12);
Korea's investment/GDP rose from about 31% (1976–80) to 35% (1986–90) then fell to 31%
(1998–2014), with persistently low profit rates until the 2000s due to high capital-output
ratios. Pirie's evidence for "neoliberalization" includes state withdrawal from
labor-capital negotiations, independent central banks, and credit allocation shifting away
from the state (Taiwan's still largely state-owned banks now lend mostly to consumers;
Korean conglomerates are cash-rich and borrow little). Wade also reports supporting social
indicators: Taiwan's wage share of national income fell from 55% (1995) to 48% (2011);
public social spending and unionization remain below OECD averages in both countries;
income inequality has risen since the 1980s; Taiwan's current account surplus is roughly
8% of GDP (comparable to Germany's), Korea's roughly 3% over the 2000s, both mostly
vis-à-vis China. Against this, Wade cites Elizabeth Thurbon (2014) arguing that what
distinguishes developmental states is not the mere existence of intervention (which all
states practice) but developmental ambition, elite consensus and institutional capacity —
and reports her finding of "little evidence" of rising anti-statist opposition in Korean
industrial policy. He offers illustrative counter-evidence: Korea's late-2000s "green
growth" push (electric vehicles, smart grids) as part of a wider "577" program (5% of GDP
into R&D across seven priority areas, aiming for top-seven global science/technology
status); Taiwan's Science and Technology Advisory Group (STAG), created in the early 1980s
with a cabinet minister as chair and 7–10 foreign advisors, reorganized in 2012 into the
Board of Science and Technology (BOST) chaired by the Premier; and Taiwan's Industrial
Technology Research Institute (ITRI), which by the mid-1980s had roughly 10,000 staff
across specialized institutes (e.g., the Electronics Research and Service Organization)
alongside a secret military-research counterpart of roughly 20,000 staff. Wade's own
position is that the developmental state has not died but transformed "from 1.0 to 2.0,"
rejecting Pirie's "post-developmental state" label as understating continuity of
developmental intent, and citing similar conclusions from Dent (2007) and Weiss (2003, on
Taiwan's Financial Merger and Acquisitions Law).

**Broader anti-catch-up forces and policy guidelines.** Wade argues several global forces
reinforce a "core-periphery" structure limiting other developing countries' catch-up
prospects today: global value chains (GVCs) that offer market access but also erect entry
barriers (e.g., tariff escalation) absent lead-firm invitation; concentration of patented
knowledge among Western firms; financialization (growth of finance/insurance/real estate
sectors and "maximize shareholder value" corporate norms); and Bretton Woods-era
asymmetric adjustment rules that pressure deficit countries but not surplus countries. He
cites evidence that dollar-appreciation cycles (1981–85, 1995–2002, 2008–09, 2012–15)
correlate with falling commodity prices, slower developing-country growth, and more
external crises. The essay closes with roughly eight policy guidelines for middle-income
countries, including: treat World Bank/Western industrial-policy advice cautiously
(quoting a 1991 internal World Bank memo calling "fine-tuning" trade/industrial
interventions "generally not feasible"); prioritize new-product creation over static
learning-by-doing, targeting a small number of sectors at a time; treat export promotion
and import replacement as complements rather than alternatives; use protection as a
time-limited buffer rather than permanent insulation; bargain hard with incoming
multinationals (citing an Enos and Park 1988 example in which Korea, unlike Chile or Hong
Kong, pressed Dow Chemical harder on national staffing for an ethylene plant); invest in
domestic innovation capacity beyond comparative advantage; curb financialization; manage
exchange rates against commodity-driven overvaluation; and recognize that the US itself
maintains a low-visibility developmental/industrial policy behind neoliberal rhetoric. The
essay ends with four open questions about why developing-country governments accept
policy-space-restricting trade/investment agreements, how to manage growing fragmentation
of global governance rules, whether China will use its influence to expand or replicate
restrictions on policy space, and how global-imbalance adjustment asymmetries might be
addressed.

### Limitations

The piece is an essay/debate contribution rather than a study with an original empirical
design; Wade himself states that neither side of the mainstream-versus-developmental-state
debate has produced "knock-out evidence" and that rigorous causal testing is hampered by
the absence of exogenous variation. Several claims rest on personal communications and
anecdotes that are not independently verifiable within the text (e.g., unnamed World Bank
economists' 2010 and 2014 remarks; a 1999 conversation with a Taiwanese central bank
official about covert capital controls). The empirical base for "economic effects" is
narrow — investment/GDP ratios for two countries (Taiwan, Korea) and a single input-output
study (Lane, 2017) covering only Korea's Heavy and Chemical Industries episode; the author
notes comparable studies for Japan and Taiwan have not been conducted. Singapore, though
named among the three core cases, receives comparatively little country-specific
evidentiary detail relative to Korea and Taiwan. Hong Kong is explicitly excluded from the
main analysis as a distinct colonial case. The policy-guidelines section is
prescriptive/advocacy in character rather than descriptive, and the essay overall argues
toward a stated conclusion (that the developmental state persists in adapted form) rather
than presenting a neutral test between the two explanations it compares.

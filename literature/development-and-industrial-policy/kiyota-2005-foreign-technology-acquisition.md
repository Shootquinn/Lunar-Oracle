## Abstract

Firm-level panel study of Japan's foreign-technology-licensing regime, 1957–1970.
Merges technology-import records (Japan Economic Research Institute) with financial
data (Japan Development Bank database) for Tokyo Stock Exchange-listed firms to
estimate:

- determinants of technology acquisition, via random-effects probit and Poisson
  regressions, and
- effects of acquisition on firm performance, via growth regressions.

Finds that the Ministry of International Trade and Industry (MITI) licensing system
screened applicant firms on industry affiliation and prior acquisition experience;
that screening criteria shifted after deregulation in 1961 and 1968; and that acquired
technology raised capital accumulation and labor productivity, without a confirmed
effect on total factor productivity (TFP).

## Summary

### Background and objective

The paper addresses a gap between two existing literatures on Japanese industrial
policy:

- macro/industry-level descriptive studies of the technology-licensing system (e.g.,
  Ozaki, 1972; Peck & Tamura, 1976), and
- microeconometric studies of technology transfer that treat firms as largely
  homogeneous (Odagiri, 1983; Montalvo & Yafeh, 1994).

The authors note that most firms, even in the industries most targeted for technology
imports, never acquired any foreign technology. This implies that government screening
of applicants — a "micro-aspect" of industrial policy — is central to understanding
how the policy worked. The object of the paper is to jointly model:

1. what determined which firms received licenses to acquire foreign technology, and
2. whether acquired technology subsequently improved firm performance,

using firm-level panel data spanning periods of strict control and periods of
deregulation.

The legal basis for the policy was the Foreign Investment Law of 1950, which required
Japanese firms seeking foreign technical-assistance contracts (lasting over one year,
paid in foreign currency) to obtain a license from the Minister of Finance and the
relevant ministry, following review by the Foreign Investment Council. Article 8 of
the law directed approval toward acquisitions that would aid the balance of payments
or the development of "important industries," while barring acquisitions deemed
unfair, illegal, or harmful to Japan's economic recovery.

### Methods and scope

**Sample and data sources.** Sample firms are those listed on the Tokyo Stock Exchange,
1957–1970 (an unbalanced panel). Technology-acquisition events come from the Japan
Economic Research Institute's 1973 compilation *Kigyobetsu Gaishi Donyu Soran*
(Complete List of Capital Imports by Firm); other firm financials come from the Japan
Development Bank (JDB) database. After merging on firm name and dropping firms with
missing/incomplete data or that disappeared and reappeared in the database, the panel
grows from 349 firms in 1957 to 1,150 in 1970, with 1,702 technologies acquired in
total and 9,415 firm-year observations used in the regressions.

**Institutional/administrative process traced.** A firm applied through the Bank of
Japan, which routed the application to MITI's Industrial Fund Section; that section
consulted relevant bureaus, the Institute of Industrial Technology, and the Agency of
Patent, then coordinated a MITI position. The Ministry of Finance and Bank of Japan
formed separate positions; all views converged at a secretary meeting of the Foreign
Investment Council, which could approve, request withdrawal, or issue a formal
rejection.

**Deregulation timeline identified.** Three periods are distinguished:

1. **1957–1961** — strict control under a "positive criteria" test (only "desirable"
   technology approved).
2. **1962–1968** — following a 1961 revision that abolished positive criteria in favor
   of a "not harmful" negative-criteria standard. Approval was denied only if the
   technology impeded existing domestic technology, harmed small/medium firms,
   disrupted industry order, or exceeded the acquiring firm's absorptive capacity.
3. **1969–1970** — following a 1968 procedural overhaul in which individual
   case-by-case examination was retained only for aircraft, weapons, explosives,
   nuclear power, space development, computers, and petrochemicals. A $50,000 price
   threshold triggered review for other technologies; below it, the Bank of Japan
   approved automatically.

**Empirical models.**

1. A dynamic random-effects probit (extending Roberts & Tybout's, 1997, export-entry
   model) for the binary decision to acquire technology in year *t*, with one-year
   lagged regressors: TFP, market share (proxy for firm size, defined at the two-digit
   industry level), capital intensity, R&D intensity, cash flow (liquidity), a lagged
   technology-acquisition dummy or cumulative technology count (past experience),
   subsidies and JDB loans (industry-level policy variables, following Beason &
   Weinstein, 1996), and deregulation-period dummies (1962–1968; 1969–1970), including
   cross-terms with the period dummies.
2. A random-effects Poisson model (same regressors) for the count of technologies
   acquired per firm-year.
3. A growth-rate regression (following Bernard & Jensen, 1999) regressing 1-year and
   3-year growth in TFP, labor productivity, value-added, employment, wages,
   capital-labor ratio, and R&D-sales ratio on a lagged technology-acquisition dummy
   interacted with deregulation-period dummies.

TFP itself is constructed as a multilateral chained index (Caves, Christensen &
Diewert, 1982; extended by Good, Nadiri, Roller & Sickles, 1983) with 1957 as the base
year (hypothetical firm TFP = 1).

### Key findings

**Aggregate trend (Fig. 1).**

- Firms acquiring technology rose from 46 (1957) to 259 (1970).
- Acquired technologies rose from 70 (1957) to 686 (1970).
- Acquisitions jumped sharply around each deregulation: from 136 (1962) to 291 (1963),
  and from 505 (1969) to 686 (1970).

**Sectoral concentration (Table 1).**

- Over 1957–1970, three sectors accounted for 53.0% of the 1,702 total acquired
  technologies: electric machinery (326, 19.2%), general machinery (275, 16.2%), and
  chemicals (301, 17.7%).
- The combined share of these three sectors fell from 57.0% (1957–1961) to 49.6%
  (1969–1970).
- Textiles rose from 2.6% to 4.9% and wholesale trade from 3.3% to 6.8% of
  acquisitions over the same comparison, indicating diffusion of licensing activity
  into new sectors after deregulation.
- Of 1,041 total sample firms, only 257 (24.7%) ever acquired a technology.
- Acquisition rates by sector (share of firms with any acquired technology): 36.3% in
  electric machinery, 30.2% in general machinery, 42.7% in chemicals. The highest
  rates were in drugs/medicines and precision equipment (42.9% each).

**Productivity gap between importers and non-importers (Table 2).**

- In 1970, firms with acquired technology had TFP 33.1% higher and labor productivity
  33.9% higher than firms without.
- This gap reversed before 1961: e.g., in 1958 TFP was 0.98 for technology-acquiring
  firms versus 3.32 for non-acquirers; in 1959, 0.93 versus 2.85.
- From 1962 onward, acquirers consistently show higher TFP (1962: 5.16 vs. 2.12; 1970:
  3.22 vs. 2.42) and higher labor productivity (1970: 6,595 vs. 4,925).

**Determinants of acquisition (Table 3, probit; Table 4, Poisson).**

- Deregulation-period dummies are positive and significant in both models, larger for
  1969–1970 than for 1962–1968 (probit period coefficients of 0.700 and 1.178 in the
  baseline specification; z-statistics 10.06 and 14.31).
- TFP alone is not a significant predictor of acquisition, but its interaction with
  the 1962–1968 period dummy becomes significantly positive (0.009, z = 1.75),
  indicating productivity became relevant to acquisition only after the first
  deregulation.
- Market share (firm-size proxy) is strongly positive throughout (7.624, z = 12.53),
  and its interaction with deregulation periods is even larger (6.602 for 1962–1968;
  14.646 for 1969–1970), suggesting large firms' advantage in obtaining licenses grew
  after deregulation.
- The cumulative number of past acquisitions has a strong positive main effect (0.141,
  z = 12.93) but a negative, significant interaction with both deregulation periods
  (−0.099 and −0.094): firms lacking prior experience became increasingly able to
  acquire technology once controls were relaxed.
- Cash flow (liquidity) is positive and significant (1.089, z = 2.13).
- JDB loans show a negative, only marginally significant coefficient (−10.040,
  z = 1.67, 10% level) in the acquisition-probability model, and a negative, more
  robust coefficient in the count model (as low as −14.746, z = 2.31). Subsidies and
  R&D intensity are not statistically significant in either model.

**Effects of acquisition on firm performance (Table 5).**

- In the pre-1961 regulated period, having acquired technology is associated with
  significantly faster 1-year growth in labor productivity (2.868, z = 2.02),
  value-added (3.540, z = 2.56), capital-labor ratio (3.099, z = 2.95), and R&D-sales
  ratio (0.034, z = 1.67) — but not in TFP, employment, or average wages.
- These positive effects are reversed by significant negative cross-terms with the
  1962–1968 and 1969–1970 deregulation dummies (e.g., capital-labor ratio cross-term
  −3.947, z = 3.39, for 1962–1968): the performance advantage of technology acquirers
  over non-acquirers disappeared after deregulation.
- Three-year growth results show the same pattern (capital-labor ratio: 2.323,
  z = 4.09, offset by a 1962–1968 cross-term of −2.324).
- The authors interpret this pattern as a capital-augmenting rather than
  TFP-augmenting effect of technology acquisition, and attribute the pre-1961
  concentration of gains partly to possible first-mover advantage or rents from
  restricted access, rather than to the technology's intrinsic productivity content.

**Interpretation of financing tools.** The paper explicitly separates technology
licensing from JDB loans and subsidies as industrial-policy instruments. The
negative/insignificant coefficients on JDB loans and subsidies are attributed to those
instruments being directed toward adjustment of declining industries and
infrastructure construction — citing Development Bank of Japan (2002, pp. 854–855) on
mining, transportation, and electricity receiving large shares of JDB lending in the
1950s–60s — rather than toward promoting growing/targeted industries, whereas
technology licensing was applied to industries in a growing phase.

**Stated conclusions.** The government's screening in technology-acquisition
licensing operated on (i) industry affiliation and (ii) past acquisition experience.
Before deregulation this favored larger, more experienced but not necessarily more
productive firms; after deregulation, higher-productivity firms lacking prior
experience gained access. Technology acquisition contributed to capital accumulation
and faster labor-productivity growth in the regulated period, but no significant TFP
effect was confirmed — leading the authors to caution against directly generalizing
the policy's applicability to other developing countries without further work
distinguishing rent effects from genuine technology-quality effects.

### Limitations

- The study does not model aggregate/supply-side effects, such as growth in the world
  supply of new technology available for licensing (citing Wakasugi, 1997, as one
  treatment of that channel).
- Contract-level detail is unavailable, so the analysis cannot separate cases where a
  single license bundled multiple patents.
- R&D stock (a theoretically preferable measure of absorptive capacity) is proxied by
  current R&D expenditure, because firm-level R&D stock could not be constructed.
- Capital user-cost and depreciation rates are assumed common across firms
  (differentiated only by industry) due to data constraints.
- Firms that merged or were acquired during the sample period are dropped entirely,
  because the JDB database cannot be traced through such changes.
- TFP is measured using value-added (not gross output) as the output concept, because
  appropriate price deflators for non-manufacturing sector inputs/outputs were
  unavailable.
- The performance-effects model assumes technology-acquisition benefits appear
  starting the year immediately following acquisition, without allowing for lagged
  effects specific to particular industries or technology types.

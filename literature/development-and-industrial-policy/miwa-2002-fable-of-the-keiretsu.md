## Abstract

The authors argue that the "keiretsu" corporate groups long treated as a defining structural feature
of the postwar Japanese economy lack the economic substance routinely attributed to them. Tracing
the concept to 1960s Marxist economists searching for evidence of "monopoly capital" domination,
they show that the standard membership rosters (compiled by the Economic Research Institute, and by
the marketing firm Dodwell's) disagree substantially with each other and rest on shifting, debt-
based definitions rather than any stable ownership or governance criterion. Using firm-level loan
and shareholding data from the mid-1960s and a firm-level financial panel from 1968-1982, they find
cross-shareholdings within the groups trivial, main-bank ties frequently absent even among "member"
firms, and most econometric associations between keiretsu status and firm outcomes (volatility,
interest rates, profitability) statistically fragile or non-reproducible once the rosters and
periods are varied. They describe the keiretsu as, in their words, a "figment of the academic
imagination."

## Summary

### Background and objective

The paper opens by cataloging how central the keiretsu concept had become to accounts of the
Japanese economy by the 1990s: cited by Calder (1993) as a driver of Japan's industrial
transformation "since the early 1950s," by Caves and Uekusa (1976) as "a major and conspicuous
force," and by Hoshi, Kashyap, and Scharfstein (1991) as coordinating member-firm investment.
Sociological accounts (Dore 1987; Lincoln et al. 1996, 1998) go further, describing the groups as
networks of relational, quasi-familial obligation that infuse Japanese commerce with non-market
social ties. By the authors' account, keiretsu-affiliation dummies had become close to mandatory in
econometric studies of Japanese industrial organization and corporate finance.

The authors' thesis is that this entire literature rests on a construct invented, not discovered.
They trace the term to 1960s-vintage Marxist economists and journalists who, having identified
prewar "monopoly capital" with the zaibatsu family conglomerates (Mitsui, Iwasaki/Mitsubishi,
Sumitomo, Yasuda), needed a postwar successor once the US occupation dissolved the zaibatsu holding
structures and dispersed their ownership after 1945-1952. The obscure think-tank "Economic Research
Institute" (Keizai chosa kai) began compiling an annual roster in 1960, the Research on the Keiretsu
(ROK; Keiretsu no kenkyu), explicitly framed in its own 1960 description as tracking "monopolistic
organizations of giant firms" that pursue "domination of capital through loans" and consolidate it
"through equity." A second, English-language roster from the Tokyo marketing firm Dodwell's later
supplied Western scholars with an alternative list, based on the invitation rosters of monthly
"lunch club" meetings among group-firm presidents (e.g., the Mitsubishi "Friday Club," the Mitsui
"Second Thursday Club").

The paper's stated aim is to test whether either roster - or the underlying keiretsu concept itself
- captures any real, otherwise-unobservable characteristic of Japanese corporate organization:
coordinated lending, meaningful cross-shareholding, or any mechanism by which "membership" could
plausibly generate the performance effects the literature claims to find. The analysis explicitly
excludes the "vertical" manufacturer-supplier keiretsu (automobile assembler groups and similar),
which the authors address separately in a companion piece (Miwa and Ramseyer, 2000).

### Methods and scope

The paper is a data-driven critique built in three layers, each targeting a different link in the
standard empirical chain.

**Roster construction.** The authors work directly from the ROK's own published tables (they use the
1966 edition, covering the 1965 fiscal year, described as the "supposed heyday" of the keiretsu,
before the capital-market liberalization of the 1980s and the 1990s recession that many observers
cite as the era of keiretsu weakening) to reconstruct the four distinct group definitions the ROK
used simultaneously across different tables in the same publication, none stated explicitly as "the"
definition. They cross-tabulate the firm rosters each definition produces for the six principal
groups (Mitsui, Mitsubishi, Sumitomo, Fuji, Sanwa, Daiichi/DKB), and separately reconstruct
Dodwell's rosters (using its earliest available 1975 edition) and the "lunch club" invitation lists
(earliest available list dated 1967, cross-checked at intervals through 1996).

**Loan and shareholding data.** For 1965 the ROK's underlying securities-disclosure data cover Tokyo
Stock Exchange Section 1 nonfinancial firms (625 firms that year). The authors tabulate, by firm and
financial institution, the percentage of each keiretsu financial institution's loan book going to
group firms and the percentage of each group firm's debt coming from group institutions (their Table
I, spanning 1965-1990), then compute correlation coefficients among the loan and equity holdings of
the financial institutions within each group (Tables II and IV) and direct measures of intragroup
cross-shareholding frequency and magnitude (Tables III, V, VII, VIII, XVI). For the "main bank"
question, they construct firm-level vignettes and a systematic 1975 tabulation (Table IX) of which
financial institution is each firm's single largest lender, compared against ROK and lunch-club
"membership."

**Panel regressions.** To re-examine the literature's headline regression results, the authors
assemble a firm-level panel from the Nikkei NEEDS financial database, covering all TSE Section 1
firms from 1968-1982, dropping firms with two or fewer years of data and extreme outliers, leaving a
sample of 1,015 firms. They code three separate keiretsu-affiliation measures for 1975 (the ROK
roster under Definition 3, lunch-club membership, and main-bank affiliation) and re-run regressions
of profit volatility, interest rates, firm size, and profitability on each measure plus total-asset
and industry controls, splitting the sample at 1975 (dividing the high-growth 1968-1975 period, when
real GNE rose 54 percent, from the slower 1976-1982 period, when it rose 22 percent) to test whether
results are stable across the definitions and across time. They do not attempt a full replication of
every published keiretsu study; instead they focus on Hoshi, Kashyap, and Scharfstein's (1991)
investment-cash flow study, the Lawrence (1991, 1993) trade-exclusion claim, and the broader
volatility/interest-rate/profitability literature associated with Nakatani (1984), Caves and Uekusa
(1976), and others.

### Key findings

**Origins and definitional instability.** The ROK simultaneously used at least four distinct
definitions of keiretsu "membership" in the mid-1960s, all built overwhelmingly on the source of a
firm's borrowed funds rather than shareholding, personnel exchange, or trading ties. Under the
broadest (Definition 1: keiretsu institutions collectively the largest lender), the Mitsui group
comprised 82 firms and the Mitsubishi group 79. Under the narrowest (Definition 2, requiring either
a three-year lending majority plus 20 percent group ownership, a 40-percent-plus lending share, or
membership "by tradition"), the counts fell to 48 (Mitsui) and 46 (Mitsubishi). A third definition
(excluding firms 30-percent-owned by rival groups) produced 71 (Mitsui) and 67 (Mitsubishi), and a
fourth, unexplained definition used only for an industry table produced yet another count (53
nonfinancial Mitsui firms, with 14 firms present under this definition but absent from the 48-firm
Definition-2 roster, and 9 firms the reverse). The authors note that Toyota entered the Mitsui
Definition-2 roster only through the catch-all "tradition" criterion, since the company (as the
paper puts it) had almost no debt and would not otherwise qualify. Well-known studies (Nakatani
1984; Hoshi et al. 1991) that describe themselves as using the more nuanced Definition 2 appear, on
the authors' inspection, to actually use one of the cruder loan-based definitions instead.

**Loan concentration: the core lending claim is quantitatively thin.** Contrary to the assumption
that keiretsu financial institutions channel a dominant share of credit to "their" firms, the 1965
data show the opposite. That year the Mitsui Bank directed 31.0 percent of its loans to Mitsui-group
borrowers and the Mitsui Trust Bank directed 24.5 percent to the group - but on the borrowing side,
Mitsui firms obtained only 14.3 percent of their total debt from the Mitsui Bank and 9.3 percent
from the Mitsui Trust Bank. Summed across all four Mitsui-affiliated institutions (bank, trust bank,
life insurer, marine and fire insurer), Mitsui firms drew roughly one quarter of their debt from the
group's own financial institutions in 1965; the authors' Table I shows this share fell steadily
thereafter, down to roughly 16 percent by the mid-1980s. Bank lending capacity was not the
constraint: in 1965 the Mitsubishi Bank's single largest borrower was Mitsubishi Heavy Industries at
31 billion yen, but its second-largest borrower, at 16 billion yen, was Tokyo Electric - not a
keiretsu member at all. More broadly, the Mitsubishi Bank directed less than a fourth of its total
loan book to keiretsu firms under Definition 3; of 168 firms that borrowed more than 100 million yen
from the bank in 1965, 61 were keiretsu members and 107 were not, and of firms borrowing a billion
yen or more, the split was 42 to 41.

**Cross-shareholdings: trivial under every measure tested.** Among nonfinancial firms, intragroup
equity holdings of any kind - let alone reciprocal cross-holdings - are rare. In the Mitsubishi
group, nonfinancial firms collectively held only 4.9 percent of the outstanding shares of other
Mitsubishi group firms in 1965 (16.5 percent including the financial institutions); the comparable
nonfinancial figures were 3.5 percent for Mitsui, 6.1 percent for Sumitomo, and 2.0 percent for
Fuji. Of the 2,070 possible pairwise investment opportunities among the 46 Mitsubishi nonfinancial
firms, only 219 pairs (10.6 percent) involved any investment at all, and only 61 pairs (3.0 percent)
involved a holding above 1 percent; the comparable 1-percent-or-more shares were 2.6 percent for
Mitsui, 3.7 percent for Sumitomo, and 1.8 percent for Fuji. Genuine reciprocal cross-shareholdings
(both firms holding at least 1 percent of each other) were rarer still: 11 such pairs at Sumitomo
(the highest of any group), 6 each at Mitsui and Sanwa, 4 at Mitsubishi, 3 at Fuji, and 2 at
Daiichi. The Antimonopoly Act capped financial institutions' equity stakes in any one firm at 10
percent, but the data show the institutions rarely approached even that ceiling - mean holdings by
the six main "money-center" banks in their own group's nonfinancial firms ranged narrowly from about
2.3 to 4.5 percent - and nonfinancial firms' reciprocal holdings of "their" banks were negligible
(means under 1 percent in every group, e.g., 0.43 percent for Mitsubishi Bank, 0.56 percent for
Mitsui Bank, 0.71 percent for Sumitomo Bank).

**Loan and shareholding coordination among group financial institutions: mostly absent.** If
keiretsu financial institutions acted as a coordinated bloc, their lending and shareholding patterns
should be positively correlated across the group's firms. The authors report the correlation matrix
is instead haphazard: among Mitsubishi institutions, Meiji Life's loans correlate positively with
the Mitsubishi Trust Bank's (r = 0.344) but negatively with the Mitsubishi Bank's (r = -0.264);
among Mitsui, only the Mitsui Bank and Mitsui Trust Bank loan patterns correlate significantly (r =
0.471); among Fuji institutions, none of the loan correlations are significant. Shareholding
correlations are similarly scattered - none significant among Mitsubishi financial institutions,
while Mitsui Trust Bank holdings correlate with the Mitsui Bank (r = 0.772) and Taisho Marine (r =
0.517). The cross-correlation between a given institution's loans and its own shareholdings in the
same firms is, in the authors' words, mostly insignificant across all six groups (Table IV).

**ROK, Dodwell's, and the lunch clubs disagree with each other.** If any of these rosters captured a
real underlying group characteristic, the alternative membership lists should broadly agree. They do
not. Comparing the 1975 ROK Definition-3 roster against Dodwell's contemporaneous list, the share of
ROK members also appearing in Dodwell's ranges from 48 to 65 percent across the six groups, and the
share of Dodwell members also appearing in the ROK ranges from 49 to 55 percent - so roughly half of
either roster's membership is absent from the other. The authors cite Weinstein and Yafeh's (1995)
finding of only a 0.31 correlation between the two rosters as independent corroboration. The "lunch
club" invitation lists (Dodwell's four-star members) are smaller still: against Mitsui and
Mitsubishi ROK/Dodwell rosters of around 80 firms apiece, only 24 firms belonged to the Mitsui lunch
club and 27 to the Mitsubishi club (with comparable counts of 16 for Sumitomo, 29 for Fuji, 37 for
Sanwa, and 30 for DKB).

**The lunch clubs: small, historically arbitrary, and not obviously consequential.** Membership in
the clubs also turned over substantially: the Sanwa club, with only 23 members in 1967, added 17 new
members over the next decade and 6 more the decade after; even the "traditional" Mitsui club added 5
firms and dropped 8 of its original 27 between 1967 and 1976. As of the earliest available 1967
invitation list, the clubs still included small, historically prewar-linked firms such as the
Hokkaido Colliery and Steamship company (1965 market capitalization 6.9 billion yen) while omitting
some of the era's largest and most dynamic firms entirely: Toyota (135 billion yen market cap in
1965), Toshiba (91 billion), Honda (42 billion), Bridgestone (42 billion), and Kajima Construction
(37 billion), plus Matsushita, Sharp, Sony, Kyocera, Suzuki, Canon, and Nikon - none of which
belonged to any of the six principal lunch clubs. Even Toyo Kogyo (Mazda), whose 1970s rescue by the
Sumitomo Bank became a celebrated illustration of keiretsu solidarity in the secondary literature
(citing Pascale and Rohlen 1983), was not itself a lunch-club member. Aggregated across all member
firms, 1973 employment for the entire Mitsubishi lunch club (269,147) was comparable to IBM alone
(268,130); no group's combined employment approached General Motors (804,571). On substance, the
authors report that of eleven outside-speaker presentations at the (reputedly most disciplined)
Mitsubishi club's 90-minute monthly meetings in 2000, only two were plausibly business-related; the
rest ranged from "Tales of London" to "Cloning" to "Angkor Wat and the Japanese."

**Main-bank ties frequently do not track keiretsu "membership."** Because the ROK sorts firms by
whichever pooled set of group institutions lends the most in aggregate, a firm can be assigned to a
keiretsu whose bank is not even its largest individual lender. The paper documents this with named
firm examples for 1975: Tobu Railroad, with total institutional debt of 192,942 million yen, was
placed in the Fuji group because its combined Yasuda Trust Bank and Fuji Bank loans exceeded its
Mitsui or Mitsubishi totals - yet the Fuji Bank was only its fifth-largest individual lender (24,059
million yen from Mitsui Trust Bank and 21,844 million from Mitsubishi Trust Bank each individually
exceeded the 15,404 million yen it borrowed from Fuji Bank). Chori was placed in the Mitsui group on
the strength of 4,434 million yen from the Mitsui Bank plus 9,013 million from the Mitsui Trust
Bank, even though it borrowed more from the Fuji Bank (11,121 million), the Daiichi Kangyo Bank
(10,440 million), and the Sanwa Bank (9,809 million) individually. Nippon TV was assigned to the
Mitsubishi group on 936 million yen from the Mitsubishi Bank and 892 million from the Mitsubishi
Trust Bank, despite borrowing 987 million from the Fuji Bank. A systematic 1975 tabulation (Table
IX) of each group's actual main-bank distribution shows the pattern is general: the Mitsui Bank
served as main bank for only 40.1 percent of ROK-defined Mitsui-group firms, the Mitsubishi Bank for
42.7 percent of Mitsubishi-group firms, and the Sumitomo Bank for 47.6 percent of Sumitomo-group
firms - meaning in each case a majority, or near-majority, of "member" firms relied on some other
institution as their principal lender. (The Fuji, Sanwa, and DKB groups showed higher own-bank main-
bank shares, at 56.4, 61.5, and 86.0 percent respectively.)

**Re-running the standard regressions.** Using the 1,015-firm NEEDS panel (1968-1982), the authors
re-test the literature's main claimed associations under all three affiliation measures (ROK, lunch
club, main bank) and find them largely unstable:

- *Volatility.* Nakatani (1984) and Khanna and Yafeh (2000) reported keiretsu firms show lower
  profit-variance ("mutual insurance"). The authors find no such pattern for either period under any
  of the three rosters; the one partial exception, higher volatility for the DKB group in 1968-1975,
  disappears in 1976-1982.
- *Interest rates.* Caves and Uekusa (1976) and Weinstein and Yafeh (1998) reported keiretsu firms
  pay higher loan rates. The authors find keiretsu firms do not pay higher rates under almost any
  roster or period; the sole exception is a positive DKB coefficient under the main-bank roster in
  1968-1975 only.
- *Firm size.* ROK-defined keiretsu firms, and lunch-club members, are robustly and significantly
  larger than independents (the strongest and most consistent result in the paper, with adjusted
  R-squared up to about 0.19). But under the main-bank affiliation measure, keiretsu firms are not
  significantly larger than independents in either period - the size effect is an artifact of how
  the ROK and lunch-club rosters are constructed, not a property of "true" main-bank groupings.
- *Profitability.* For 1968-1975, the ROK rosters reproduce the literature's standard finding (Caves
  and Uekusa 1976; Nakatani 1984; Khanna and Yafeh 2000; Lincoln et al. 1996; Weinstein and Yafeh
  1998) that keiretsu firms earn significantly lower profits - true for five of the six groups. But
  for 1976-1982 this holds for only two of six groups; under the lunch-club definition, none of the
  six groups show significantly lower profitability in 1968-1975 (Sumitomo lunch-club firms are
  significantly more profitable); and under the main-bank definition, only the DKB group is
  significantly less profitable, and only in 1968-1975. The authors also cite Morck, Nakamura, and
  Shivdasani's (2000) finding of no significant keiretsu effect on Tobin's Q or operating income
  using the same Nakatani ROK roster.
- Across the volatility and interest-rate specifications, adjusted R-squared values sit close to
  zero (roughly 0.00 to 0.04); even the firm-size regressions, the most explanatory, reach only
  about 0.19.

**Trade exclusion claim reversed on respecification.** Lawrence (1991, 1993) had argued, from
sectoral trade regressions, that keiretsu presence depressed imports without affecting exports,
concluding the groups were exclusionary. The paper reports that when Saxonhouse (1991, 1993)
respecified the model to correct a simultaneity problem, the keiretsu trade effect disappeared, and
that Weinstein and Yafeh (1995), examining the underlying margins directly, found keiretsu firms'
profit/cost margins were, if anything, lower than independents' - the opposite of what a market-
power or exclusion story would predict.

**Intragroup trade: modest and dominated by trading-company pass-through.** Citing a 1992 Japan Fair
Trade Commission survey of the lunch-club members (not the authors' own regression evidence),
manufacturing firms in the six keiretsu sold a mean of 12.58 percent of output within their own
group - but only 2.38 percent once the group's general trading company (which resells goods onward
to outside buyers) is excluded from the calculation. The range across groups ran from 5.57 percent
(Sanwa) to 31.67 percent (Sumitomo) gross, collapsing to 1.49 and 0.61 percent respectively net of
the trading company. On the purchasing side, the same firms sourced a mean 6.71 percent of supplies
within-group (2.24 percent excluding the trading company), ranging from 3.67 percent (Fuji) to 15.87
percent (Mitsubishi) gross.

**Headline conclusion.** The authors' summary judgment is that, aside from firm size - itself an
artifact of how the loan-based rosters happen to select larger firms - the keiretsu dummy's
coefficients are "generally insignificant" once results are checked across rosters, periods, and
outlier-sensitivity, which they argue is exactly what standard theory predicts when a variable
captures nothing real: a correctly estimated coefficient of zero. They attribute the published
literature's contrary "significant" findings to a combination of misspecification, outlier
sensitivity, and publication bias toward significant results, and close by framing the episode as a
caution about substituting econometric technique for direct knowledge of the underlying data.

### Limitations

- The core loan, shareholding, and correlation analysis (Tables I-VIII, XV-XVI) is anchored on 1965
  data, described by the authors as the keiretsu's "supposed heyday"; while they state that a
  companion working paper (Miwa and Ramseyer, 2001b) reproduces similar patterns for 1975, that
  underlying analysis is not reproduced in full within this article itself.
- The panel regressions (Section 5.3) cover only 1968-1982 and TSE Section 1 (large, listed) firms
  via the Nikkei NEEDS database; smaller and unlisted firms, and the post-1982 bubble and post-
  bubble periods most associated with keiretsu "unraveling" narratives, fall outside the regression
  sample (though addressed narratively in the conclusion).
- The paper's re-analysis targets specific published studies (chiefly Hoshi, Kashyap, and
  Scharfstein 1991; Nakatani 1984; Caves and Uekusa 1976; Lawrence 1991/1993) rather than
  exhaustively re-running every keiretsu regression in the literature; it explicitly declines to
  replicate Hoshi et al. directly, instead relying on Hayashi (2000) and Hall and Weinstein (2000)
  for that critique and testing the main-bank premise underlying it instead.
- The vertical (manufacturer-supplier) keiretsu - a distinct and, by the authors' own account,
  analytically different phenomenon - are explicitly excluded from this paper's scope and treated in
  a separate article (Miwa and Ramseyer, 2000).
- Some of the paper's own supporting data (e.g., the 1992 intragroup-trade survey, cross-
  shareholding appendix tables) are drawn from secondary compilations (JFTC survey results, an
  unpublished Mitsubishi memorandum) rather than the authors' primary data construction used
  elsewhere in the piece.
- Several large data tables in the source PDF (Tables I, II, XV, XVI) are set as rotated (landscape-
  in-portrait) blocks; figures drawn from these tables in this summary were cross-checked against
  the paper's own prose restatements of the same numbers wherever the text provided them, but the
  full underlying tables were not independently re-verified cell-by-cell beyond that spot-checking.
- As a piece explicitly built to overturn a dominant paradigm, the paper's framing is polemical
  (e.g., describing the keiretsu concept's origins as ideologically motivated); the numerical
  findings reported above are the authors' own calculations and are presented here as their claims,
  not as adjudicated between them and the literature they are contesting.

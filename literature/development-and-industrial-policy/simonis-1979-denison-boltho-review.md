## Abstract

A short (four-page) joint review by Udo E. Simonis of two mid-1970s books analyzing the sources of Japan's rapid postwar growth. This document is itself a secondary source: it presents no new data of its own but reports, summarizes, and critiques the findings of Edward F. Denison and William K. Chung's growth-accounting monograph (1976) and, more briefly, Andrea Boltho's more descriptive economic survey (1975). Its chief value for this literature review is that it transmits Denison & Chung's headline growth-decomposition figures — otherwise inaccessible here because the original Brookings monograph (ledger entry 11) could not be obtained as a PDF.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The review opens by situating both books within the broader interest in Japan's "miraculous" economic and cultural rise, noting that observers of the period were drawn variously to the growth rate itself, to Japan's structural/industrial policy, or to the social and ecological consequences of rapid growth. Denison and Chung's book, the reviewer states, belongs squarely to the first group: its stated aim is to measure the sources of Japan's economic growth between 1953 and 1971 and to compare the results with those previously estimated for Western industrialized countries. The study extends Denison's own prior growth-accounting frameworks — *Why Growth Rates Differ* (1967, covering nine countries) and *Accounting for United States Economic Growth, 1929–1969* (1974) — to the Japanese case, and follows on from a chapter both authors contributed to the Brookings-organized *Asia's New Giant: How the Japanese Economy Works* (Patrick and Rosovsky, eds., 1976). Boltho's book, by contrast, is described as part of an Oxford "Economies of the World" series aimed at university, business, and government readers, offering a survey of Japan's economic development from 1953 to 1973 that the reviewer characterizes as more selective than comprehensive, and less ambitious in scope than Denison & Chung's.

### Methods and scope

Denison and Chung's approach is described as predominantly macroeconomic, relying heavily on official statistics and the standard Denison-style growth-accounting method, and explicitly leaving non-economic characteristics aside. The book's structure, per the review: Chapter 2 compares Japan's output level internationally; Chapter 3 presents the growth record; Chapter 4 estimates the sources of growth; Chapter 5 gives an overview of the sources of growth of national income per person employed (the book's central theme); subsequent chapters examine each source in turn — labor input, capital input, advances in knowledge, reallocation of resources, and economies of scale; a final chapter (Chapter 12) asks whether the growth rate can be sustained. Fifteen appendices (from p. 129) document data sources and estimation procedures.

Boltho's book is organized in two parts: Part I gives a descriptive account of the economy's growth record, cyclical fluctuations, and industrial structure (with attention to economic dualism and employment practices), closing with an assessment of strengths and weaknesses of Japan's development process. Part II, described by the reviewer as paralleling Denison & Chung "but different in approach," attempts to isolate the factors contributing most to Japan's growth rate, again centering on labor and capital inputs but also examining the role of government intervention, foreign trade, and income distribution.

### Key findings

**Denison & Chung's decomposition (the primary value of this source for FA1).** The review reports Denison and Chung's central conclusion that no single factor explains Japan's high growth rate over the period studied; rather, all five identified sources contributed, and did so more than in any other industrialized country examined. For the standardized growth rate of national income over 1961–1971 of **9.56 percentage points per year**, the review states the authors' estimated contributions (in percentage points) as:

| Source of growth | Contribution (pp) |
|---|---|
| Labor input | 1.78 |
| Capital input | 2.57 |
| Advances in knowledge | 2.43 |
| Economies of scale | 1.14 |
| Contraction of agricultural inputs | 0.62 |
| Contraction of non-agricultural self-employment | 0.19 |
| Reduction in international trade barriers | 0.01 |

As transcribed in the review, these seven listed components sum to 8.74 percentage points, roughly 0.8 points short of the stated 9.56 total; the review does not reconcile this gap, which likely reflects sub-components of Denison's fuller standard taxonomy (e.g., further breakdowns of labor and capital inputs) that are not individually itemized in this condensed account. This is a property of the review's reporting, not necessarily of the original book.

The review also reports Denison & Chung's decomposition of the same 9.56-point growth rate into a "transitional" component — defined as growth attributable to Japan's position as a late developer or to one-time elimination of inefficiencies — and a "sustainable" component expected to persist. Per the review (citing the book's Table 12–1, p. 115): **sustainable sources contributed 3.24 percentage points**, and **transitional sources contributed 6.32 percentage points**, of the 9.56-point total. The authors link Japan's still-comparatively-low output per worker to its high growth rate as a "catching up" phenomenon relative to more advanced economies.

**Reviewer's assessment of Denison & Chung.** Simonis calls the book valuable reading, particularly for readers interested in growth rates and quantitative economics. He is nonetheless critical on two main counts. First, he questions whether transitional growth sources can be convincingly identified and their future prospects assessed without examining Japan's particular political, social, and economic institutions — which the book's non-institutional, macro-only approach does not do. Second, he faults the book for presenting figures to two decimal places despite the authors' own admission (his account: on p. 3 of the book) that most series are approximations subject to considerable percentage error — in the reviewer's words, this practice risks "asserting a persuasive power they do not possess." He further notes that topics such as cartels, dualism, infrastructure, pollution, and welfare are absent from both the book's index and its text.

**Boltho — secondary findings.** The review describes Boltho's conclusion that Japan's postwar strategy was one of growth maximization, "highly successful by its own standards," but that this came at the cost of neglecting social infrastructure, land use, and pollution control over two decades — an imbalance Boltho attributes to resources being channeled toward private-sector infrastructure rather than social goods. The reviewer relays Boltho's illustrative citation of a Japanese Economic Planning Agency publication contrasting a blast furnace's fixed capacity with the elastic crowding of passengers onto Tokyo's Yamate Loop Line, as an emblem of the growth-maximization mindset. Boltho is described as taking a more sympathetic final view than a simple "failure" critique would suggest, noting that similar imbalances (e.g., assuming cheap natural resources, undervaluing environmental costs) arose across the industrialized world in this period, not uniquely in Japan.

**Shared criticism.** The reviewer applies the same institutional-omission critique to both books, and adds that neither Denison & Chung nor Boltho engages with the European-language literature on Japan, which he states — contrary to the authors' apparent view — is not scanty. The review closes by quoting Saburo Okita's warning (from the foreword to K. Bieda's 1970 book) that foreign economists relying solely on documents and statistics may draw false conclusions about an economic system, such as Japan's, whose institutions have been substantially adapted to the country's own culture.

### Limitations

This document is a four-page joint book review, not primary research, and its account of Denison & Chung's methodology and results is necessarily condensed and selective (e.g., it gives headline decomposition figures but not the fuller sub-component breakdown of labor and capital inputs that the original monograph's chapters presumably contain). The listed contribution figures, as transcribed here, do not sum exactly to the stated 9.56-point total — a roughly 0.8-point gap that this source does not explain. No tables or figures from either underlying book are reproduced beyond the in-text summary numbers given by the reviewer. OCR extraction of the source PDF rendered two page-number headers ambiguously ("i8o," "i8i"); these are read as 180 and 181, consistent with the EconStor-supplied page range (178–181). No other figures, equations, or image-only tables appear in the source; the entire review is running text.

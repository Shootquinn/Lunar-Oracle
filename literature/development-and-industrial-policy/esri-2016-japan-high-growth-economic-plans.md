## Abstract

The note examines Japan's high-growth period, roughly the mid-1950s to the early 1970s, with emphasis on the indicative role of successive national economic plans in coordinating government policy and signaling direction to the private sector. It also covers related fiscal, monetary, and industrial policy, short-term policy coordination tools, and the annual and monthly economic reports used to communicate the government's economic assessment. The authors frame the material as a development-economics case study intended to be useful to government officials and private-sector actors in catching-up economies.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The note opens by observing that Japan's real GDP/GNP growth rate frequently exceeded 10 percent during a roughly twenty-year span from the mid-1950s to the early 1970s.

- Growth settled to around 5 percent in the 1970s after the high-growth era ended.
- Growth fell further, to roughly 1-3 percent, after the early-1990s bubble collapse.

The authors position their contribution against existing growth-theory literature on postwar Japan, citing Kosai (1980), Hayashi (1997), Yoshikawa (1992), and Ohkita (2010). Their claim is that most prior work applies general growth theory to Japan's postwar experience without examining the machinery of economic planning itself, or the development-economics features of the case. Two supporting works are named as exceptions that do take this approach: Sadahiro (1991, 1992) and Ohno (2006).

The stated purpose is twofold:

1. To document how Japan's postwar economic plans functioned as coordination devices between government fiscal, monetary, and industrial policy on one side, and private-sector investment and consumption expectations on the other.
2. To extract lessons that may be transferable to developing countries pursuing catch-up growth, for both government authorities and private-sector businesspeople.

The paper is organized into fourteen numbered chapters plus two appendices:

### Methods and scope

This is a descriptive historical and institutional study, not an econometric test of causal mechanisms.

- It synthesizes official retrospective histories published by the Economic Planning Agency: EPA (1972) *Documents 25 Years of Economic White Paper*, EPA (1976) *Evolvement of Contemporary Economy in Japan: Thirty-Year History of the Economic Planning Agency*, and EPA (1997) *Trajectory of Postwar Economy in Japan: Fifty-Year History of the Economic Planning Agency*.
- It draws on Japanese-language secondary literature, including Sadahiro (1991, 1992), Komiya (1975, 1984, 1988), Hoshino (2003), Asai (2000, 2015), and Okazaki (1998), among many others listed in a substantial references section.
- Numerical tables are reproduced from these sources, in some cases "with some correction by the authors" (their own phrasing for Table 4-2).
- The authors are Cabinet Office/ESRI staff economists, not independent academics; the note carries a standard disclaimer that its views are those of the authors, not of ESRI, the Cabinet Office, or the Government of Japan.

Topically the note traces, in order:

1. The Dodge Line stabilization of 1948-49 and the immediate postwar economic collapse.
2. The founding of the Economic Council Agency (August 1952) and its successor, the Economic Planning Agency (July 1955), and the formulation procedure by which the Prime Minister's Economic Council deliberated a draft plan before Cabinet approval.
3. Six sequential national economic plans spanning FY1956 through FY1975, each summarized with planned versus actual macroeconomic outcomes.
4. The fiscal system's "small government, balanced budget" principle and the Fiscal Investment and Loan Program (FILP).
5. The tax system built on the 1949 Shoup recommendation.
6. The segmented, indirect-financing, main-bank financial system.
7. The linkage between economic plans and MITI's industrial policy.
8. Trade and capital liberalization and GATT/IMF reintegration.
9. Infrastructure building and the Comprehensive National Development Plan.
10. Human-resource development and the buildout of universal health and pension insurance.
11. The Economic Council's consensus-building role among business, labor, academic, and consumer representatives.
12. The succession of quantitative planning methodologies, from the Colm approach through alternative-growth-rate selection to econometric and turnpike models.
13. Short-term economic outlook forecasting and crisis-response policy packages.
14. The Economic White Paper and Monthly Economic Report as recurring communication tools.

The two appendices place the plans against Harrod-Domar and Solow-Swan growth-theory scaffolding, and describe the composite-index and Stock-Watson stochastic-indicator methodology used for Japan's official business-cycle dating.

### Key findings

#### The Dodge Line and postwar stabilization

Wartime destruction, per the authors' citation of Furuya (1979), cost Japan roughly a quarter of national wealth and a third of production capacity. Hyperinflation followed, driven by Reconstruction Finance Cash Office (RFCO) bond issuance underwritten directly by the Bank of Japan.

Joseph Dodge, a banker dispatched by GHQ as economic adviser to General MacArthur, unveiled the **Nine-Point Economic Stabilization Program** in December 1948:

1. Balance of budget.
2. Reinforcement of tax collection.
3. Restriction on credit extension.
4. Wage stabilization.
5. Price control.
6. Control on foreign trade and exchange.
7. Material allocation for promotion of exports.
8. Enhancement of production of domestic raw materials/products.
9. Reinforcement of food collection.

Implementing this program, the Diet approved "over-balanced" budgets for FY1949 and FY1950:

- The initial budget rose from ¥414.4 billion in FY1948 to ¥704.9 billion in FY1949, a 70.1 percent increase.
- The increase reflected projected nominal expense growth from continuing inflation, plus the transfer of a previously hidden export/import price-adjustment subsidy into the General Account.
- The **360 yen-per-dollar fixed exchange rate** was established under the Bretton Woods regime as part of this stabilization.

Severe deflation followed from deficient demand, resolved by Korean War (1950-53) special procurement:

- Cumulative special procurement orders, June 1950-May 1955, totaled US$1,618,736 thousand (merchandise US$974,607 thousand plus services US$644,129 thousand).
- Against a reported FY1954 GNP of ¥7,241 billion (from the Five-Year Plan for Economic Self-Support), annual special-procurement demand ran at roughly 1-2 percent of GNP using the 360 yen/dollar rate.
- By around 1955, the economy had recovered its prewar (1934-36) activity level, prompting the 1956 Annual Economic Report's declaration that growth "is no longer termed postwar."

#### The balanced-budget principle, 1949-1965

The note states directly that after the 1949 Dodge stabilization, the government "kept the balanced budget principle until 1965 without dependence on government bonds."

- Deficit-financing government bonds were issued in 1965 to cover a current-account shortfall.
- No further deficit-financing bonds were issued until 1973; in the interim only construction government bonds, used to finance capital-account deficits, were issued.
- The authors characterize this as maintaining "sound fiscal management."

The Fiscal Investment and Loan Program (FILP), described by the authors as the "second national budget," tied social savings (chiefly postal savings and public pension reserves) to public investment:

- Scale expanded from roughly ¥300 billion in 1955 (about 30 percent of the general account budget) to ¥10 trillion in 1975 (about 50 percent of the same).
- Tax burden itself stayed low: preferential tax measures reduced total tax revenue by only 0.3 percent in 1950, rising to a peak relative burden of 12.1 percent in 1955, then declining to 3.2 percent by 1975 as the absolute tax base grew (Table 5-3).

Each plan also used a different estimation methodology, tracked in the same source table:

- Five-Year Plan for Economic Self-Support: the "Colm approach."
- New Long-Range Economic Plan and National Income Doubling Plan: the "Alternative Growth Rates Approach."
- Medium-Term Economic Plan, Economic and Social Development Plan, and New Economic and Social Development Plan: the "Econometric model approach."

Pattern in the outcomes:

- Five of the six plans were overtaken by faster-than-planned actual growth, in some cases dramatically. The first plan's industrial-growth target of 7.4 percent was more than doubled by an actual 15.6 percent.
- Each of the first five plans was abandoned and replaced years before its own nominal end date, because actual performance had already outrun its targets.
- The sixth plan (May 1970) is the sole reversal: its 10.6 percent GDP/GNP growth target and 12.4 percent industrial-growth target both went unmet, landing at an actual 6.1 percent and 3.6 percent respectively.
- The note attributes the sixth plan's shortfall to the collapse of the Bretton Woods fixed-rate regime — the yen was revalued from 360 to 308 per dollar in December 1971, after the United States suspended gold-dollar convertibility in August 1971 — compounded by the first oil crisis (triggered by the fourth Middle East/Yom Kippur War). The note places the end of the high-growth era in the first half of the 1970s.

#### The National Income Doubling Plan in detail

The National Income Doubling Plan was formulated directly because of the inauguration of Prime Minister Ikeda, tied to his so-called "Monthly Salary Doubling Policy" campaign platform.

- Decided by Cabinet in December 1960, covering FY1961-70.
- Target growth rate: **9 percent for the first three years (FY1961-63)** and **7.2 percent for the remaining seven years**, averaging to the 7.2 percent headline figure over the full ten years.
- A draft version, based on the 1956-58 average as its base year, had initially implied a flat 7.8 percent rate for a 13-year span (1957-70); the note attributes the shift to a 9 percent near-term figure to pressure "from the highest level," associated with economist Osamu Shimomura, one of Ikeda's advisers.

Other targets embedded in the plan:

- Labor productivity growth assumed at nearly equal rates across sectors: 5.6 percent (primary), 5.5 percent (secondary), 5.5 percent (tertiary), implying labor migration from low- to high-productivity sectors.
- Agricultural population share expected to fall from 36 percent (1960) to about 25 percent (1970).
- Industrial production expected to grow 3.3-fold from 1960 to 1970.
- Human-capital target: raise the share of junior-high graduates advancing to senior high school from 59.8 percent (1960) to 72 percent (1970). The note reports the actual figure reached 82 percent by 1970 and exceeded 90 percent by 1974.
- R&D target: raise R&D expenditure (government, university, and private combined) from 0.9 percent of GNP to 2.0 percent by 1970, roughly matching the contemporary average of other developed countries.
- Science/engineering graduate targets: raise annual university graduates in science/engineering by 16,000 over the base of 29,000, and annual engineering high-school graduates by about 85,000 over a base of 95,000, within the ten-year period.

Real growth was underestimated in every one of the six years shown; balance-of-payments forecasts, by contrast, showed no consistent direction of error. The note connects the growth-underestimation pattern to an "announcement effect" argument it attributes to Sato (1990): private firms treated the government's official growth forecast as a floor rather than a ceiling, set their own more ambitious internal targets against it, and the resulting aggregate outcome routinely beat the official projection.

#### Trade and capital liberalization

Table 7-2 (Outline of Trade Liberalization Process, 1960-86) traces the liberalized proportion of import items:

- 1960.4: 41 percent liberalized (1,443 items), including coffee beans and household sewing machines.
- 1961.4: 62 percent liberalized (2,645 items), including raw cotton, radios, and bicycles.
- 1965.10: over 93 percent liberalized, including passenger cars for the first time.
- 1971.6: over 94 percent liberalized.
- 1971.10: close to 95 percent liberalized.

Institutional milestones of re-integration:

- Japan joined GATT as a provisional member in 1953, obtained full membership in December 1955, and transitioned to GATT Article 11 status in 1963, barring import restriction as a balance-of-payments excuse.
- Japan joined the IMF in 1952 and completed transition to IMF Article 8 status in 1964, barring foreign-exchange controls as a balance-of-payments excuse.
- Japan joined the OECD in 1964, the same year it hosted the Tokyo Olympic Games and opened the Shinkansen bullet train (Tokyo-Osaka), financed by an IBRD loan.

#### Other quantified structural shifts

- Agricultural land reform cut the proportion of tenant-farmed land from 45.9 percent (1945) to 9.9 percent (1950), citing Miwa and Ando (2010, p.145).
- Durable consumer goods diffusion (Table 4-3): urban-household television ownership rose from 7.8 percent (1957) to 93.5 percent (1964); farm-household ownership rose from 2.6 percent (1958) to 81.7 percent (1964). Urban washing-machine ownership rose from 20.2 percent (1957) to 75.8 percent (1964); urban refrigerator ownership rose from 2.8 percent (1957) to 66.2 percent (1964).
- Universal health insurance and pension coverage was achieved by 1961, following the National Healthcare Insurance Act (1958) and National Pension Act (1959).
- Direct government control over the economy was wound down rapidly (Table 7-1): the number of production goods subject to government allocation fell from a peak of 252 (February 1946) to 7 by March 1952; price-controlled items fell from a peak of 2,129 (March 1949) to 148 by March 1952.
- The Korean War-era foreign exchange crisis of 1957-58 (Table 12-3) was met with a sequence of tightening measures: the official discount rate was raised from 7.30 percent to 7.67 percent (March 1957), then to 8.40 percent (May 1957), before being eased back down to 7.67 percent, 7.30 percent, and 6.94 percent through 1958-59 as the crisis abated.

#### Industrial policy linkage

Table 6-1 (sourced to Okazaki 1998) ties named long-term sectoral industrial policies directly to the first plan, the Five-Year Plan for Economic Self-Support:

- Electric power: "Electric Power Six-Year Plan" (January 1956), under the Electric Power Development Coordination Council.
- Steel: "Long-Term Twenty-Year Plan of Supply and Demand of Steel" (May 1956), MITI.
- Petrochemistry: "Support Measures to Petrochemistry" (July 1955) and "Handling on Petrochemical Corporatization Plan" (February 1956), MITI.
- Synthetic fabric: "Fiber Six-Year Plan" (September 1955), Fiber Industry Council.
- Machinery: Act on Temporary Measures concerning the Promotion of Machinery Industry (May 1956).

The National Income Doubling Plan forecast industrial production would grow roughly 3.3-fold from FY1960 to FY1970, and set six industrial-policy targets spanning international competitiveness, energy systems, agricultural/fishery modernization, small- and medium-firm modernization, labor migration among industries, and reduction of low-income groups. The note flags an ongoing scholarly dispute over whether industrial policy actually promoted Japan's high growth, citing Okazaki (1998), Ohno (2006), Aghion and Griffith (2005), and Krugman (1994) on the affirmative side against Flath (2005), Kohama and Watanabe (1996), Miwa (1996), and Tsuruta (1982) on the skeptical side; it also quotes the World Bank's (1993) *East Asian Miracle* conclusion that "the evidence that industrial policy systematically promoted sectors with high productivity change is weak."

#### Infrastructure investment allocation

Chapter 8 reports that public investment allocation across sectors was tracked explicitly, plan by plan, starting with the National Income Doubling Plan (Figure 8-1, covering the National Income Doubling Plan, Medium-term Economic Plan, Economic and Social Development Plan, and New Economic and Social Development Plan). Road construction had the largest share among public-works sectors, funded in part by earmarked gasoline-tax revenue under a program initiated by lawmaker Kakuei Tanaka, later Prime Minister (1972-74). The first Comprehensive National Development Plan was compiled in 1962, designating 15 "New Industrial Cities," mostly outside the existing Pacific Belt Zone (Tokyo, Nagoya, Osaka, Kita-Kyushu); a second national development plan followed in 1969, leading to the expressway network, the wider Shinkansen network, and the Honshu-Shikoku bridges.

#### Human resources, social security, and consensus-building institutions

- Postwar dislocation was severe: the Ministry of Health and Welfare estimated 8.8 percent of households, roughly three million people, needed aid as of December 1945, against an estimated unemployed population ranging from five million to 13 million.
- The Unemployment Insurance Act (1947) allowed up to six months of benefits before a claimant would fall back to the Public Assistance Act (1946, revised 1950).
- The National Healthcare Insurance Act (1958) and National Pension Act (1959) achieved universal coverage by 1961; the Social Security Subcommittee estimated the maturing pension system would accumulate roughly four trillion yen in funds, against a reported 1960 GNP of around 16 trillion yen.
- The Economic Council that deliberated each plan had roughly 30 core members, but for the National Income Doubling Plan the government stood up four additional committees — the Overall Policy Committee, a ten-subcommittee Public Sector Committee, a seven-subcommittee Private Sector Committee, and a Committee for Quantitative Analysis — bringing total participants in the planning process to more than 200.
- The Economic Planning Agency, acting as the Council's secretariat, distributed each plan draft to all ministries of government and received, in the note's account, "no less than 1,000 comments" that had to be reconciled before a plan was finalized.

#### Quantitative planning methods evolved with the plans themselves

- The first plan (1955) used the Colm approach: an assumed 2 percent annual labor-force growth rate combined with 3 percent labor-productivity growth to derive a 5 percent target GNP growth rate.
- The second plan (1958) used an "Alternative Growth Rates Approach," testing three candidate rates — 4.5 percent (near the prewar average), 8.9 percent (near the postwar average), and 6.5 percent (the midpoint) — against employment, external-balance, and domestic-savings constraints before selecting 6.5 percent. Actual private investment growth (17.4 percent) far outran the plan's 4 percent assumption, and actual GNP growth (10.5 percent) likewise diverged sharply from the target.
- The National Income Doubling Plan (1960) also used the Alternative Growth Rates Approach, settling on 7.2 percent after testing factors including labor, energy, foreign exchange, and domestic savings.
- From the Medium-Term Economic Plan (1965) onward, the EPA adopted formal econometric modeling: a "Medium-term Macro Model" of roughly 40 equations and 60 variables, linked to an input-output analysis model derived from Wassily Leontief's framework.
- For the plan covering the second half of the 1970s (1976), the EPA built a 15-sector Medium-term Multi-sector Econometric Model (15 commodities, 14 industries), incorporating production functions, a Phillips-curve wage mechanism for steel and machinery sectors, and capacity-utilization-driven pricing.
- A separate "Turnpike Model," grounded in Tsukui's (1966, 1968) application of turnpike theorem to Japan, tested whether the Japanese economy of 1955-59 tracked a theoretically efficient balanced-growth path; Tsukui found the discrepancy between the computed and actual growth paths was very small.

### Limitations

- The note is explicitly labeled "Preliminary" on its own internal cover page and carries the standard ESRI disclaimer that its views belong to the two named authors, not to ESRI, the Cabinet Office, or the Government of Japan. It has not been through external peer review as far as the document itself indicates.
- It is a descriptive and institutional history, not a causal analysis. Although it documents that five of six plans were outperformed by actual growth, it does not attempt to isolate how much of that overperformance the plans themselves caused, via the "announcement effect" mechanism it cites from Sato (1990), versus how much reflects exogenous drivers — export demand, labor migration off the farm, technology catch-up — that the plans arguably tracked rather than caused.
- Much of the empirical material is drawn at second hand from the Economic Planning Agency's own retrospective institutional histories (EPA 1972, 1976, 1997), rather than from independently audited primary statistics. This raises a possibility of institutional self-narration bias in a document written by EPA's Cabinet Office successor about EPA's own historical record.
- The note flags an internal methodological caution about its own subject matter without fully resolving it: it states that large-scale econometric models, central to plan formulation from the mid-1960s onward, "have steadily lost their potency in the academic world since the Lucas critique" (Lucas 1976), without reconciling this admission with the otherwise favorable framing given to Japan's econometric planning apparatus elsewhere in the note.
- Coverage is almost entirely macroeconomic and institutional. Firm-level or regional mechanisms are treated only briefly — for example, the designation of fifteen New Industrial Cities outside the Pacific Belt Zone, or labor migration toward the three major urban areas (greater Tokyo, Osaka, Nagoya) — without firm-level data to substantiate the mechanisms described.
- The document is English-language ESRI output whose citation base leans heavily on Japanese-language secondary sources; those underlying sources have not been independently checked as part of this summary.
- Some numeric series carry acknowledged caveats in the source tables themselves, e.g., Table 4-1's inflation figures are marked "n.a." (not available/not set as a plan target) for the first three plans, meaning no planned-versus-actual inflation comparison exists for roughly the first decade of the high-growth period.

## Abstract

Panel-data study testing whether investment-cash flow sensitivity differs between
Japanese manufacturing firms with close "main bank" (keiretsu) ties and firms with
weaker banking relationships, as a test of information/incentive-based financing
constraints. Sample: Tokyo Stock Exchange-listed manufacturers, fiscal years 1977-1982,
121 keiretsu-affiliated firms and 24 independent firms (plus 192 firms of intermediate
affiliation status), drawn from a base panel of continuously listed firms 1965-1986.
Estimates fixed-effects investment regressions of gross investment/capital on Tobin's
average q, lagged production, and two liquidity measures (cash flow, short-term
securities), separately by affiliation class. Finds liquidity coefficients eight to
twelve times larger for independent firms than for group firms, a difference significant
at the 1 percent level, and reports that this pattern survives checks for industry
composition, measurement error, group-membership endogeneity, and an overinvestment
(free-cash-flow) alternative explanation.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The paper investigates whether information and incentive problems in capital markets
shape corporate investment, using the availability of internal funds (liquidity) as the
diagnostic variable. The authors motivate the test by summarizing two theoretical
traditions predicting that liquidity should matter more for investment when capital-market
frictions are severe: Jensen and Meckling (1976), emphasizing incentive costs of outside
finance that dilute managerial ownership, and Myers and Majluf (1984), emphasizing
adverse-selection costs when managers know more about firm prospects than investors. Both
traditions imply that firms facing these frictions will prefer internal funds and will
show investment more sensitive to internally generated cash.

The authors note that liquidity-investment correlations have long been documented (citing
Meyer and Kuh, 1957, as an early example) but are open to the standard criticism that
liquidity simply proxies for unobserved investment opportunities — a firm doing well has
both high cash flow and good prospects, so the correlation need not reflect financing
constraints. Including Tobin's q as a forward-looking control for investment
opportunities (following Hayashi, 1982) does not fully resolve this, since q is measured
with error and rests on strong assumptions (perfect capital markets, constant returns to
scale, no market power). The paper's identification strategy follows Fazzari, Hubbard,
and Petersen (1988), who split firms a priori by dividend-retention behavior and compared
liquidity sensitivity across groups; Hoshi, Kashyap, and Scharfstein instead split firms
by an institutional feature of the Japanese economy — membership in a bank-centered
industrial group (keiretsu) — arguing this produces a cleaner a priori sort between firms
expected to face capital-market frictions and firms expected not to. The stated logic is
that even if the liquidity coefficient is biased in both subsamples (e.g., because q is
mismeasured), the difference between the two subsamples' coefficients is an unbiased
estimate of the true difference in liquidity effects, provided the bias is common to both
groups. The paper's second aim is to document a role for banks and financial
intermediaries in the investment process, building on Diamond (1984) on banks as
delegated monitors.

### Methods and scope

**Institutional setting.** The paper describes the six largest keiretsu — Mitsubishi,
Mitsui, Sumitomo, Fuyo, Dai-ichi Kangyo, and Sanwa — three descended from prewar zaibatsu
and three formed later around a core bank. By the authors' account, 89 of the top 200
Japanese industrial firms had strong ties to one of these six groups, accounting for 40 to
55 percent of total sales in natural resources, primary metals, industrial machinery,
chemicals, and cement. Group firms borrow disproportionately from their group's core
bank(s), which (at the time, before the Revised Anti-Monopoly Act of 1977 phased equity
holdings down to a 5 percent ceiling by 1987) also held up to roughly 10 percent of member
firms' equity and placed bank employees in client-firm management. The six groups'
core banks included, per a 1989 American Banker survey, five of the world's five largest
banks and nine of the top fourteen. The paper cites case evidence (Sheard, 1985;
Abegglen and Stalk, 1985) of main banks organizing rescues of distressed affiliates (e.g.,
Sumitomo's intervention at Mazda) and of the absence of a main bank being blamed, in the
Japanese financial press, for postwar failures such as Osawa Shokai.

**Sample and classification.** The base sample is Japanese manufacturing firms
continuously listed on the Tokyo Stock Exchange from 1965 to 1986 with fiscal years ending
in March, drawn from Nikkei Financial Data tapes (construction detailed in a companion
paper, Hoshi and Kashyap, 1990). Basic selection rules yield 353 firms; 16 more are
dropped for having |Tobin's q| exceeding 50 in any accounting year. Group affiliation is
assigned using Nakatani's (1984) refinement of the Keiretsu no Kenkyu classification
scheme, which sorts firms into the six largest groups based on borrowing concentration
from group banks/insurers (a group bank as largest lender in three consecutive years with
group shareholding above 20 percent, or the largest lender providing at least 40 percent
of bank debt, or historical affiliation) and excludes firms that switched groups. This
yields 121 group firms and 24 independent firms in the regression sample; the authors note
that, per Keiretsu no Kenkyu, only 83 of 859 nonfinancial TSE-listed firms were classified
as fully independent as of 1981, explaining the small independent count. The remaining
192 sample firms are treated separately as three "hybrid" subcategories: 25 firms
belonging to a group's President's Council (a monthly cross-firm forum) without meeting
Nakatani's financial-affiliation criteria; 152 "quasi-independent" firms neither clearly
grouped nor clearly independent; and 15 firms classified as subsidiaries of group firms.

**Regression specification.** The dependent variable is gross investment in depreciable
assets divided by beginning-of-period capital stock. Regressors are: cash flow (after-tax
income plus accounting depreciation minus dividends, normalized by capital); short-term
securities held at the beginning of the period (normalized by capital), as a stock measure
of liquidity; Tobin's average q (tax-corrected: market value of debt plus equity minus
market value of nondepreciable assets such as land, divided by the replacement cost of
depreciable capital, with replacement cost built via a recursive inflation-adjusted
procedure starting from March 1965 book values, and land valued separately from a March
1974 base); and lagged production (sales plus the change in finished-goods inventories,
normalized by capital), included as an atheoretical "accelerator" control. All
regressions include firm fixed effects and year dummies and cover fiscal years 1977-1982.
The authors state explicitly that they do not attach a structural interpretation to the
coefficients and instead rest their inference on the estimated difference in liquidity
coefficients across affiliation classes.

### Key findings

**Summary statistics (Table I, fiscal years 1977-1982).** Group firms (n=121) versus
independent firms (n=24): median investment/capital 0.130 vs. 0.148; mean firm-level
standard deviation of investment/capital 0.110 vs. 0.138; median cash flow/capital 0.240
vs. 0.291; mean firm-level standard deviation of cash flow/capital 0.106 vs. 0.120; median
production/capital 7.23 vs. 7.79; median Tobin's average q 1.04 vs. 1.23; median
debt/equity 0.97 vs. 0.66 (the largest gap in the table); median capital stock (millions
of 1982 yen) 13,037 vs. 13,388; median sales growth 0.069 vs. 0.081; median short-term
securities/capital 0.145 vs. 0.116. The authors caution against reading financing-
constraint conclusions directly off these univariate comparisons, noting none of the
mean differences is statistically significant and that the comparisons do not condition on
other firm characteristics.

**Baseline regressions (Table II, dependent variable investment/capital, 1977-1982).**
Pooled sample (n=145): cash flow coefficient 0.106 (SE 0.032); short-term securities
0.096 (0.025); Tobin's q 0.006 (0.002); production 0.019 (0.003); adjusted R-squared
0.391. With production included, group firms (n=121): cash flow 0.041 (0.033, not
significant); short-term securities 0.061 (0.024); q 0.007 (0.003); production 0.022
(0.003); adjusted R-squared 0.432. Independent firms (n=24): cash flow 0.501 (0.084);
short-term securities 0.512 (0.085); q 0.007 (0.004, not significant); production -0.022
(0.009); adjusted R-squared 0.458. Dropping production to check robustness: group firms,
cash flow 0.060 (0.035), short-term securities 0.081 (0.026), q 0.011 (0.003), adjusted
R-squared 0.362; independent firms, cash flow 0.451 (0.083), short-term securities 0.441
(0.081), q 0.003 (0.004), adjusted R-squared 0.435. The headline result: both liquidity
coefficients are roughly eight to twelve times larger for independent firms than for group
firms, with the difference significant at the 1 percent level in both specifications
(reported t-statistics on the difference: 5.10 for cash flow, 5.11 for short-term
securities). The authors reject the null hypothesis of equal liquidity coefficients
across the two firm classes. Production is significantly more important for group firms
than independent firms (and even enters negatively, though imprecisely, for independent
firms), a pattern the authors describe as difficult to interpret given the absence of a
strong theoretical rationale for the production term.

**Robustness checks noted but not tabulated in the article.** The authors report — without
presenting full tables in the published text, referring instead to an earlier MIT Sloan
Working Paper No. 2071-88 — that results are materially unchanged when: additional lags
of liquidity and production are added; Tobin's q is measured at the end rather than the
beginning of the period (this variable enters small and insignificant); and cash flow is
instrumented with its lagged values to purge the component correlated with within-period
information not reflected in beginning-of-period q.

**Hybrid-status firms (Table III, 1977-1982).** President's Council/non-group firms
(n=25): cash flow 0.406 (0.162); short-term securities 0.110 (0.072, not significant); q
-0.002 (0.004); production 0.032 (0.008); adjusted R-squared 0.408. The cash-flow gap
between these firms and group firms is significant at the 5 percent level. Quasi-
independent firms (n=152): cash flow 0.245 (0.042); short-term securities 0.082 (0.033);
q -0.000 (0.004); production 0.012 (0.004); adjusted R-squared 0.227 — liquidity
coefficients fall between the group and independent estimates, with the cash-flow gap
versus both group and independent firms significant at the 1 percent level, while the
short-term-securities coefficient is not statistically distinguishable from the
independent-firm estimate. Subsidiary firms (n=15): cash flow -0.082 (0.049, not
significant); short-term securities 0.105 (printed SE 0.0131, likely an OCR-affected
digit given the sample's other standard errors); q 0.019 (0.008); production 0.022
(0.007); adjusted R-squared 0.582. Subsidiaries' cash-flow coefficient differs
significantly (1 percent level) from independent, quasi-independent, and President's
Council firms alike; the authors read this ordering — group firms and their subsidiaries
least liquidity-sensitive, President's Council/quasi-independent firms intermediate,
fully independent firms most sensitive — as consistent with a monotonic relationship
between closeness to group banks and ease of financing investment.

**Industry-composition check (Section V.1, Table IV).** Across seven broad industry
categories, group firms range from 5 to 26 firms per category (121 total) and independent
firms from 0 to 6 per category (24 total), with roughly similar proportional distributions
across the two classes (e.g., electric/precision machinery is 21 percent of group firms
and 25 percent of independent firms; oil/chemicals/rubber/drugs is 20 percent vs. 25
percent). Within-industry regressions (pooling independent and quasi-independent firms
for power) show the cash-flow coefficient statistically insignificant for group firms in
all seven industries, and significant in six of seven for the pooled non-group firms; the
group-vs-nongroup difference in cash-flow coefficients is significant in three of seven
industries, and the short-term-securities difference is significant in one of seven. The
authors conclude industry composition does not drive the main finding.

**Measurement-error check (Section V.2).** Considers whether group firms' accounting cash
flow is a noisier signal (e.g., non-market intragroup transfer pricing, income-shuffling
to smooth reported earnings). The authors find this explanation unconvincing given the
lack of an institutional mechanism to coordinate such transfers (citing a Sumitomo
executive's remark, via Gerlach 1987, that the group President's Council could not run a
company's internals) and report that the standard deviation of cash flow is not
statistically significantly different between group and independent firms (per Table I),
which they say provides no support for an income-smoothing story.

**Group-membership endogeneity check (Section V.3).** Addresses the concern that firms
selecting into or out of group membership might differ systematically in unobserved growth
opportunities. The authors argue most firms' group ties predate the sample by 25-plus
years and were essentially fixed during 1977-1982, that firm fixed effects absorb
time-invariant unobserved opportunity differences, and that investment, sales growth, and
q levels are not statistically different between group and independent firms over the
sample period (per Table I). They further note that if group affiliation originally
sorted the most liquidity-constrained firms into groups (a capital-rationing-era
explanation), this would bias the results against finding a group/independent difference,
strengthening rather than undermining their inference.

**Overinvestment vs. underinvestment (Section V.4, Table V).** Tests Jensen's (1986)
free-cash-flow/overinvestment alternative — that liquidity-investment sensitivity in
group firms could reflect bank-restrained overinvestment among firms with poor prospects,
not underinvestment among constrained firms — by splitting the sample on whether average
1977-1982 Tobin's q is above or below the sample median and adding liquidity interactions
with a high-q dummy. Non-group firms (independent plus quasi-independent, n=176): cash
flow 0.137 (0.058); cash flow × high-q dummy 0.205 (0.072); short-term securities 0.084
(0.042); short-term securities × high-q dummy 0.040 (0.058, imprecise); q 0.001 (0.003);
production 0.011 (0.004); adjusted R-squared 0.243. Group firms (n=121): cash flow 0.102
(0.065); cash flow × high-q dummy -0.080 (0.075); short-term securities 0.084 (0.069);
short-term securities × high-q dummy -0.025 (0.073); q 0.008 (0.003); production 0.021
(0.003); adjusted R-squared 0.431. The overinvestment hypothesis predicts negative
interaction coefficients (liquidity should matter less for high-q, i.e., good-prospect,
firms); instead the interaction is positive for non-group firms. The largest and only
statistically significant contrast: total cash-flow sensitivity for high-q non-group
firms is 0.342 (0.137 + 0.205) versus 0.022 (0.102 - 0.080) for high-q group firms — the
opposite of the overinvestment prediction that the group/non-group gap should be largest
for low-q (poor-prospect) firms. The authors state this evidence tends to reject the
overinvestment hypothesis for their sample.

**Authors' stated interpretation.** The authors read the overall pattern as evidence that
information and incentive problems in the capital market materially affect investment,
and that close bank relationships — the keiretsu main-bank arrangement — mitigate these
problems by concentrating debt and equity claims in an informed monitor, reducing
free-rider and conflict-of-interest costs relative to diffusely held arm's-length finance.
They connect the finding to macroeconomic propagation mechanisms in Greenwald, Stiglitz,
and Weiss (1984) and Bernanke and Gertler (1989), in which capital-market imperfections
amplify output fluctuations, and to Bernanke's (1983) account of bank-liquidity collapse
deepening the Great Depression, suggesting an analogous channel could run through bank
liquidity more generally. They speculate, with explicit caution, that Japanese
institutional arrangements may confer a capital-cost advantage relative to a U.S. system
in which Glass-Steagall bars bank equity ownership, but they offer a competing reading:
that the keiretsu bank-centered system may itself be a historically contingent response to
a capital-scarce, regulation-constrained (interest-rate ceilings, restricted foreign
borrowing) postwar Japanese financial environment, and note that by the time of writing
Japanese firms were already shifting toward corporate bond financing as capital-market
restrictions eased — a trend they say they examine further in companion work
(Hoshi, Kashyap, and Scharfstein, 1990).

### Limitations

The independent-firm subsample is small (24 firms), which the authors themselves flag as
limiting the precision of finer cuts (e.g., industry-level splits are described as
uninformative for independent firms alone and are only pooled with quasi-independent firms
for power). Group affiliation is not a bright-line institutional category — the paper
notes there are "no membership dues or cards" — so the group/independent/hybrid
classification rests on a single external data source's (Keiretsu no Kenkyu, as refined by
Nakatani 1984) borrowing- and shareholding-concentration criteria, which the authors
acknowledge is best understood as one dimension (financial affiliation) of a broader and
fuzzier network concept. Tobin's average q relies on the assumptions underlying Hayashi's
(1982) equivalence of marginal and average q (perfect capital markets, constant returns to
scale, no market power) and on a multistep, assumption-laden recursive construction of
replacement cost for capital, inventories, and land from 1965/1974 book-value bases; the
authors caution against a structural reading of the q coefficient itself, noting it could
otherwise be read as implying implausibly large adjustment costs. The identification
strategy rests on the assumption that any omitted-variable bias in the liquidity
coefficient is common across group and independent firms; the authors argue against three
specific alternative sources of differential bias (industry composition, measurement
error/income-shuffling, group-membership endogeneity) but do not claim to have exhaustively
ruled out all possible sources of differential bias. Several robustness results (extra
lags, end-of-period q, instrumented cash flow) are asserted in the text but reported only
in an earlier unpublished working paper (MIT Sloan WP No. 2071-88), not in tables within
the published article. The regression sample is confined to a six-year window
(fiscal 1977-1982) within a longer firm-selection period (1965-1986), and to continuously
listed Japanese manufacturing firms on the Tokyo Stock Exchange with March fiscal
year-ends; non-manufacturing firms, unlisted firms, and firms without a full 1965-1986
listing history are excluded by construction. The paper's own discussion section treats
the finding as historically and institutionally contingent (tied to a capital-scarce,
regulated postwar Japanese financial system that the authors describe as already easing at
the time of writing), rather than as a claim generalizable to other financial systems or
periods.

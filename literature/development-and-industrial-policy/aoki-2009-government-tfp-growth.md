## Abstract

Discussion-paper synthesis of total factor productivity (TFP) growth in Japan's
postwar "rapid growth era" (roughly 1956-1973). Decomposes aggregate TFP into
agricultural, non-agricultural, and factor-reallocation components using a
growth-accounting framework (following Syrquin, 1984; Basu & Fernald, 2002) applied to
the Esteban-Pretel and Sawada (2009) dataset. Reviews prior firm-level, plant-level,
and industry-level empirical studies to characterize the Japanese government's
involvement in: foreign-technology licensing and its deregulation; domestic R&D and
patent policy; agricultural research and extension; and domestic diffusion of
technology through human capital, infrastructure, and patent institutions. No new
econometric estimation is performed.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The paper asks why Japan's TFP grew rapidly after World War II and what role
government policy played, treating TFP evolution as something to be explained rather
than assumed exogenous, as in prior calibration studies (Otsu, 2007; Esteban-Pretel &
Sawada, 2009). It opens by noting Japan's per-capita GDP growth exceeded 10% per year
in the late 1950s and 1960s (Maddison database), and situates itself against
growth-accounting literature (Denison & Chung, 1976; Young, 1995; Hayami & Ogasawara,
1999, 2002) and calibration literature (Parente & Prescott, 2004; Chen, Imrohoroglu &
Imrohoroglu, 2006; Otsu, 2007).

The stated objective is to identify the sources of TFP growth — foreign technology
adoption versus domestic innovation — separately for agriculture and non-agriculture,
and to characterize the specific government mechanisms (regulatory, financial,
organizational) affecting each channel. The authors frame the postwar starting point
starkly: the 1964 Agency of Industrial Science and Technology (under the Ministry of
International Trade and Industry, MITI) is quoted describing a technology gap with
advanced countries that had been "preserved" and "further expanded" by wartime
isolation.

### Methods and scope

The paper is a literature review and data synthesis, not an original econometric
study. Its components:

- A theoretical review (Section 2) of technology-growth models — Solow-Swan;
  Romer (1986, 1990); Lucas (1988); Rebelo (1991); Aghion & Howitt (1992); Grossman &
  Helpman (1991) — and of technology-diffusion channels (trade, FDI, licensing,
  imitation), used to frame which government interventions (subsidies, patent
  enforcement, R&D consortia) are theoretically justified.
- A growth-accounting decomposition (Section 3) of aggregate Japanese TFP into
  agricultural TFP, non-agricultural TFP, and capital/labor reallocation "bonus"
  terms, computed for four subperiods: 1956-73, 1973-83, 1983-91, and 1991-2000, using
  the Esteban-Pretel and Sawada (2009) dataset (labor input measured as total hours).
- A qualitative and secondary-quantitative account (Sections 4-6) of technology
  adoption, technology innovation, agricultural technology, and domestic diffusion,
  drawing on named firm- and industry-level studies (notably Kiyota & Okazaki, 2005;
  Nakamura & Ohashi, 2008; Branstetter & Sakakibara, 1998, 2002; Kuroda, 1995, 1997;
  Yamamura, Sonobe & Otsuka, 2005) and government statistical series (Science and
  Technology Agency white papers; Committee for Foreign Technology Survey, 1961).

Geographic/temporal scope: Japan, primarily 1945-1973 (rapid growth era), with some
series extended to 2000 and prewar background (Meiji era onward) for agricultural
technology.

### Key findings

**Aggregate productivity catch-up.** Japanese TFP was about 43% of the U.S. level in
1952 (Christensen, Cummings & Jorgenson, 1995 estimate) and rose to about 80% of the
U.S. level by the early 1970s, when rapid growth ended.

**TFP decomposition by subperiod (Table 1 in source).**

| Period | Aggregate TFP | Agricultural TFP | Non-agricultural TFP | Capital realloc. | Labor realloc. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1956-73 | 4.78% | 0.11% | 3.96% | 0.05% | 0.66% |
| 1973-83 | 0.50% | 0.00% | 0.25% | 0.04% | 0.21% |
| 1983-91 | 2.26% | -0.01% | 2.13% | 0.01% | 0.13% |
| 1991-2000 | 0.17% | -0.01% | 0.09% | 0.01% | 0.08% |

Non-agricultural TFP and labor reallocation (rural-to-urban migration, consistent with
Hayashi & Prescott, 2008) are identified as the dominant contributors during 1956-73;
agriculture's direct TFP contribution is small throughout, attributed to its declining
output share.

**Technology-licensing volume (Figure 5; Table 2 in source).**

- "Type A" technological assistance contracts (duration over one year) numbered
  around 100 per year through 1959, rising to roughly 2,000 per year by the late
  1960s.
- Period totals: 454 (1950-54), 575 (1955-59), 2,039 (1960-64), 3,926 (1965-69), 8,295
  (1970-74), 7,846 (1974-79).
- Three industries — chemical products, general machinery, and electric machinery —
  accounted for 62% of all licenses across the 1950s and 1960s.
- The net technology-import/GNP ratio declined over the same period that the
  R&D/GNP ratio rose (Figure 4), read as evidence of a shift from adoption toward
  domestic innovation.

**Regulatory framework and its deregulation.**

- The regime began with the 1950 Foreign Investment Law, requiring government
  approval of all Type A contracts to steer imports toward balance-of-payments needs
  and "important"/public industries.
- 1959: a simplified "new method for capital import" examination procedure.
- 1961: substantial deregulation — automatic approval except for flagged cases.
- 1968: near-total abolition of case-by-case review, retained only for aircraft,
  weapons, gunpowder, and nuclear power.

**Sectoral licensing case studies.**

- Heavy electric machinery: Toshiba, Mitsubishi Electric, and Fuji Electric signed
  comprehensive technology contracts with GE, Westinghouse, and Siemens respectively
  in the early 1950s, enabling domestic production of thermal generators exceeding
  200,000 kW capacity.
- Consumer electronics: more than thirty Japanese firms licensed television patents
  from RCA, Westinghouse, and EMI in the early 1950s. Cumulative TV production reached
  only 75,000 units by 1954 but 2.9 million units in 1959 alone, of which 27,000 were
  exported.
- Automobiles: SCAP permitted domestic production only from 1949, when output was "10
  to 20 years behind" world standards. Nissan, Hino, and Isuzu then contracted with
  Austin Motors, Renault, and Roots Motors respectively in the early 1950s, gaining
  design/materials/processing know-how and transfer-machine mass-production methods.
- Steel: the "First Iron and Steel Rationalization Plan" (1951, strip mills) paired
  Fuji Iron Works and Yawata Iron Works with Armco Steel, and Kawasaki Iron Works with
  Republic Steel (1958). The "Second Iron and Steel Rationalization Plan" (1956,
  basic oxygen furnace/BOF technology) saw Nippon Kokan Corporation (NKK) license from
  Alpine Co. in 1956, with six other major steel firms sub-licensing from NKK by the
  late 1950s.

**Firm- and plant-level effects of adoption.**

- Kiyota and Okazaki (2005), using 1957-1970 firm data from Tokyo Stock
  Exchange-listed companies matched to Japan Development Bank (JDB) financial data,
  find technology adoption raised labor productivity, value added, capital-labor
  ratio, and R&D-sales ratio in the 1950s, but not TFP; these effects faded in the
  1960s.
- Nakamura and Ohashi (2008), using 1957-1968 plant-level steel-industry data, find
  BOF adoption raised the industry's annual TFP growth rate from a counterfactual 7%
  to an actual 17%. They also find a "learning effect": TFP fell 9% immediately after
  a plant adopted BOF, taking about two years to exceed prior open-hearth-technology
  TFP levels.

**Selectivity in MITI approvals.**

- Kiyota and Okazaki (2005) find the coefficient on a firm's cumulative prior
  technology adoptions was significantly positive as a predictor of approval in the
  1950s but near zero after the 1960s deregulation, indicating MITI favored
  established firms in the regulated period.
- The paper cites the frequently referenced case of Sony waiting roughly two years for
  approval to license transistor technology from Western Electric in the early 1950s,
  attributed to MITI's skepticism of a then-small start-up firm (Peck & Tamura, 1976;
  Okimoto, 1989; Goto & Odagiri, 1996).
- Interpretations of this intervention diverge in the cited literature: Johnson (1982)
  and Lynn (1982, 1998) attribute lower-priced access to cutting-edge technology to
  MITI's coordination, while other cited work stresses delayed access for competent
  firms.

**R&D funding structure (Table 3 in source).**

- Japan's R&D-to-GNP ratio rose from about 50% of the U.S. level in 1961 to roughly
  parity with the U.S. by 1989; in both countries, about 40% of R&D funded basic/
  applied research.
- 1988 shares of total R&D expenditure by provider: Japan — government 18.4%,
  industry 76.3%, university 4.5%, private laboratory 0.7%. United States (1988) —
  government 48.0%, industry 47.9%, university 2.8%, private laboratory 1.4%. West
  Germany (1987), France (1983), and the U.K. (1987) are also tabulated, with
  government provider shares of 36.6%, 53.8%, and 38.7% respectively — Japan has the
  lowest government share and highest industry share among the five.
- Cited R&D tax-credit comparison (Okimoto, 1989): Japan allowed a 20% credit on R&D
  spending exceeding a firm's prior-year peak, capped at 10% of corporate tax
  liability; the U.S. allowed a 25% credit on spending above the preceding three-year
  average, uncapped, with a 15-year carryover.

**Non-distortion and coordination as the government's innovation-phase role.**

- MITI is described (via Okimoto, 1989) as having "traditionally refused to protect
  depressed industries," and as not pursuing "national champion" projects — the paper
  notes Japan had no equivalent of a Strategic Defense Initiative, supersonic jet, or
  Airbus program, and did not direct substantial R&D spending toward a defense
  industry.
- Beason and Weinstein (1996) are cited finding government resources were
  disproportionately allocated to low-growth industries and that financial support did
  not positively affect TFP.
- The 1988 Japanese patent-law reform, intended to strengthen a historically
  user-favorable/imitation-friendly patent system, produced no measurable increase in
  R&D expenditure or product innovation according to Sakakibara and Branstetter
  (2001).

**R&D consortia.**

- MITI's most-cited consortium example is the very large-scale integrated circuit
  (VLSI) project.
- Branstetter and Sakakibara (1998, 2002) find consortium participation increased
  member firms' R&D expenditure and patent output even controlling for fixed effects,
  subsidies, and participant-selection endogeneity. Technological proximity among
  members increased patent output (Branstetter & Sakakibara, 2002) but decreased R&D
  spending (Sakakibara, 2001).
- Sakakibara (1997) reports government financial support for consortia was modest.
- A dissenting view is cited from Krugman and Obstfeld (2006), who characterize the
  semiconductor consortium as ultimately having "yielded neither strong externalities
  nor excess returns."
- NTT's (Nippon Telegraph and Telephone) laboratories are noted as procuring materials
  from the open market rather than from affiliated suppliers (unlike AT&T in the
  U.S.), which is described as promoting technology diffusion between NTT and its
  suppliers.

**Agricultural technology.**

- Kuroda (1995) decomposes agricultural TFP change for 1960-1990 and attributes about
  90% of TFP growth to technological improvement versus about 10% to scale economies,
  indicating that biological/chemical innovation (high-yield rice varieties) mattered
  more than mechanization.
- The Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fishery (MAFF) developed "Norin" rice
  varieties (pureline and crossbred selections) through its research and extension
  (R&E) programs.
- Kuroda (1997) identifies a marked decline in R&E's effect on TFP growth after the
  late 1960s — despite unchanged R&E investment levels — as the principal explanation
  for the slowdown in agricultural TFP growth after 1969.
- Prewar technological accumulation (attributed to Hayami, 1975) is cited as a
  backlog that postwar agricultural TFP growth partly implemented, including
  knowledge originally spread by veteran farmers ("Rounou") after the Meiji
  Restoration.

**Domestic diffusion mechanisms.**

- Godo and Hayami (2002) compare Japan-U.S. education levels from 1890-1990, finding
  Japan's postwar GDP-per-capita catch-up outpaced its schooling catch-up, with the
  gap attributed to a lag effect from a sharp pre-1940 rise in vocational training.
- Named government human-capital measures: mandatory elementary education (late 19th
  century), free tuition (1900), vocational training programs (from 1893), and the
  "Ginou Kentei Seido" technician certification system introduced by the Ministry of
  Health, Welfare, and Labor in 1959.
- Infrastructure contributions cited: postal system, telecommunications, railways, and
  roads, with a specific example of government-built transport infrastructure enabling
  BOF-technology material delivery (Okazaki, 2001).
- A named industry-cluster case (Yamamura, Sonobe & Otsuka, 2005) traces the
  motorcycle industry from 1948-1964: early growth from mass entry and imitation,
  later growth from domestic innovation, further imitation, and exit of inefficient
  firms.
- Japan's patent system, which permits pre-issuance opposition, is described as
  favoring early disclosure and imitation over strict exclusivity.

**Concluding characterization (source's own summary).** The government's role is
described as differing by phase and sector: restrictive, selective licensing control
in the non-agricultural adoption phase (1950-1968); largely non-distortionary tax/
subsidy policy plus R&D-consortium coordination in the innovation phase; direct
research investment (via MAFF) in agriculture; and human-capital, infrastructure, and
patent-system support for diffusion across all sectors. The paper also notes a cited
government shortfall: insufficient investment in tertiary/postgraduate education in
the post-catch-up era, linked (via Hayami & Godo, 2009) to Japan's low TFP growth in
the 1990s — consistent with the 0.17% aggregate TFP figure for 1991-2000 in Table 1.

### Limitations

- The paper performs no new econometric estimation; its quantitative claims (BOF TFP
  effects, consortium effects, education-TFP lag, etc.) are attributed to specific
  cited papers rather than derived independently within this document.
- Several cited sources reach conflicting conclusions on the same questions without
  resolution in this paper — e.g., Johnson (1982) and Lynn (1982, 1998) versus critics
  on MITI's licensing intervention; Branstetter and Sakakibara versus Krugman and
  Obstfeld on consortium effectiveness.
- The TFP decomposition (Table 1) relies on a single dataset (Esteban-Pretel &
  Sawada, 2009) without independent replication in this paper.
- Some figures are described only qualitatively — e.g., Figure 6, a scatter diagram of
  technology-adoption counts against 1955-1970 productivity growth by industry, is
  reported to show "a positive correlation" without a stated correlation coefficient
  or regression statistic.
- As a discussion paper prepared for a festschrift conference, the version reviewed
  here does not show evidence of completed peer review; its synthesis should be read
  as current to mid-2009.

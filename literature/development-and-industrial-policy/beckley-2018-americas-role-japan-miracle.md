## Abstract

Quasi-experimental re-estimation of the contribution of the US–Japan security alliance to Japan's
postwar growth acceleration. Constructs a "synthetic Japan" counterfactual from a 48-country donor
pool using the synthetic control method (Abadie and Gardeazabal 2003; Abadie, Diamond, and
Hainmueller 2010, 2015), with Maddison Project per-capita GDP data, 1920–1969, and 1958 as the
intervention year. Reports Japan's actual per-capita GDP grew 9.3%/year from 1958–1968 versus
3.6%/year for the synthetic control, a gap attributed to alliance-linked capital inflows, defense-
burden reduction, export access, and political financing documented through archival and secondary
historical sources. Concludes the "East Asian model" of state-led development was necessary but not
sufficient absent this geopolitical subsidy.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The paper addresses a long-standing claim in the literature on Japan's postwar growth — that the
domestic industrial-policy account (bureaucrat-business coordination, export-led growth,
protectionism, technology acquisition, associated with sources such as Johnson 1982, Amsden 2001,
and the World Bank's 1993 "East Asian Miracle" report) is incomplete without accounting for Japan's
alliance with the United States — and notes that, as of this writing, no study had tested the claim
with modern statistical methods. A contrasting literature (Forsberg 2000; LaFeber 1997; Miller 2012;
Pempel 1999; Schaller 1997; Shimizu 2001) argues Japan's high investment rates, export markets, and
tolerance for protectionist and technology-acquisition policies all depended on a tolerant, capital-
supplying United States and would not otherwise have been available. The authors cite as background
figures: average GDP growth of 7.1% (1945–1958) versus 9.5% (1959–1970) per Bolt and Van Zanden's
(2014) Maddison Project data, and Japan Cabinet Office real GDP growth rates of 6.8% (1956), 8.1%
(1957), 6.6% (1958), then 11.2% (1959), 12.0% (1960), and 11.7% (1961), with growth exceeding 10% in
8 of the 12 years from 1959–1970 (a companion Penn World Table series is cited in a footnote as
showing 7.9% for 1950–1958 and 10.6% for 1959–1970). By 1970 Japan held the world's third-largest
economy; the authors note, citing Babones (2011), that Japan is considered the only large,
diversified economy to have risen from below-average development to the upper tier of the world
economy. The paper's stated objective is to test whether the 1957–1960 consolidation of the US–Japan
security relationship — rather than domestic industrial policy alone — coincides with, and
statistically explains, Japan's growth acceleration.

### Methods and scope

Two complementary methods are used. First, a qualitative historical review (drawing on Foreign
Relations of the United States volumes, secondary histories, and named officials' statements) traces
US–Japan relations from the 1951 San Francisco Peace Treaty through the 1960 security treaty signing
and into the early 1970s. Second, a quantitative synthetic control analysis constructs a
counterfactual "synthetic Japan" as a weighted composite of other countries' per-capita GDP
trajectories, using Maddison Project data (Bolt and Van Zanden 2014) over 1920–1969, with 1958 set
as the treatment/intervention year (chosen because the economic groundwork for the alliance was laid
that year, ahead of the formal treaty signing on January 19, 1960; a supplementary structural-break
test, reported in an online appendix, is said to independently identify 1958 as the break point
using the outcome variable itself). Formally, the model treats Japan as unit j=1 among J+1=49 units,
with a (J×1) weight vector W chosen by constrained optimization so that weighted-average predictor
values for the donor pool match Japan's as closely as possible. The donor pool comprises 48
countries (e.g., Argentina, Austria, Brazil, Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, South Korea, the
United States, USSR, and others); an earlier draft restricted to 15 countries most similar to Japan
(those with wartime GDP contraction) produced substantially similar results, per a footnote.
Predictors used to fit pre-treatment weights include: post-war economic recovery (per-capita GDP
1957/1945 ratio), post-war population growth (1957/1945 ratio), average per-capita GDP 1920–1957,
per-capita GDP in 1944 and in 1958, and a dummy for UN membership as of 1945; standard growth
predictors (investment, consumption, human capital, trade openness, inflation) were not used because
they are only available from 1950 onward across countries. The post-treatment window is restricted
to 1958–1968 (11 years) to avoid post-treatment bias from the alliance's own late-1960s unraveling.
Robustness is checked via an "in-space" placebo test (assigning the 1958 treatment to every other
donor-pool country), an "in-sample" placebo test (assigning a false treatment year of 1951, the
first full year of the Korean War), and a leave-one-out sensitivity test that iteratively drops each
of the six positively weighted donor countries.

### Key findings

- **Main effect**: Japan's per-capita GDP grew at an average annual rate of 9.3% from 1958 to 1968
  (from $3,289 to $7,983), versus 3.6% per year for the synthetic Japan (from $3,346 to $4,757). The
  implied gap averages about $1,288 per year, equivalent to 39.2% of the 1958 baseline level.
- **Comparison to prior estimate**: Patrick and Rosovsky (1976) had estimated, without a stated
  method, that Japan's economy would have been 30% smaller by 1976 absent its low defense spending;
  the paper's independent, model-based estimate is described as similar in magnitude.
- **Synthetic Japan composition** (Table 1 weights): Austria 0.426, South Korea 0.181, Indonesia
  0.157, the Philippines 0.134, Malaysia 0.089, United States 0.013; all other 42 donor countries
  received zero weight.
- **Pre-treatment fit** (Table 2): post-war economic recovery ratio 2.330 (Japan) vs. 2.179
  (synthetic); post-war population growth 1.201 vs. 1.179; average per-capita GDP 1920–1957 of
  $2,175.99 vs. $2,176.82; per-capita GDP in 1944 of $2,658.97 vs. $2,497.22; per-capita GDP in 1958
  of $3,288.90 vs. $3,346.16; UN membership in 1945 of 0 vs. 0.147 (driven by the Philippines and US
  weight share of 14.7%).
- **In-space placebo**: Japan's ratio of post-1958 to pre-1958 mean squared prediction error (MSPE)
  is the largest among all 49 countries in the donor pool (including Japan); the probability of this
  occurring by chance is 1/49 ≈ 0.020, below the conventional 5% threshold.
- **In-sample placebo (1951 as false treatment year)**: Japan's post/pre-MSPE ratio ranks only 26th
  of 48 (one country, Hungary, dropped for non-convergence of weights), for a chance probability of
  26/48 ≈ 0.542 — indicating the Korean War onset (1951) does not mark a comparable structural
  break, unlike 1958.
- **Leave-one-out test**: excluding any single one of the six positively weighted donor countries
  (Austria, Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, South Korea, United States) and re-estimating
  leaves the pre-1958 co-movement and post-1958 divergence pattern intact.
- **Historical mechanisms identified for 1958 specifically**:
  - *Capital*: US-orchestrated low-interest loans caused foreign loans to Japan to double in 1958,
    sourced substantially from the World Bank (which the US dominated), the US Export-Import Bank,
    and American commercial banks, which became major capital suppliers to Japanese utilities and
    heavy industry.
  - *Defense burden*: Japanese defense spending as a share of GDP fell 20% between 1958 and 1960 —
    called the sharpest shift of the entire postwar era — continuing to decline to below 1% of GDP
    by 1970 (citing Calder 1988).
  - *Exports*: despite GATT membership from September 1955, 14 of 32 members had withheld most-
    favored-nation status from Japan, and in 1956–57 the US itself had pressured Japan into
    "voluntary" export restraints (a US ambassador likened Japanese "voluntariness" to complying
    at gunpoint). In 1958 the US and Japan established the Joint Committee on US–Japan Trade,
    Japan sent its first government-funded trade mission to the US, and the US Trade Act of 1958
    gave the president authority to cut tariffs by 20% over four years. American purchases from
    Japan rose more than 150% from 1958 to 1960, producing Japan's first-ever trade surplus; over
    the following decade the US absorbed more than 30% of Japan's exports (a dependency the
    authors liken to Bulgaria's export reliance on the Soviet Union). A 1958 dispute over Japanese
    stainless-steel flatware imports is cited as an example where the administration resisted
    tariff increases in favor of a lighter voluntary export restraint.
  - *Political financing*: the paper reports, citing named officials — Alfred C. Ulmer Jr. (CIA
    operations chief for East Asia, 1955–1958), Roger Hilsman, Norbert A. Schlei, and U. Alexis
    Johnson (US ambassador to Japan, 1966–1969) — plus some still-classified sources, that the CIA
    began providing millions of dollars in secret campaign funds to Kishi Nobusuke and other
    Liberal Democratic Party (LDP) politicians starting in early 1958, to sustain what US
    officials termed a "conservative political hegemony" in Japan.
- **Political-leadership turning point**: Ishibashi Tanzan became prime minister in January 1957 but
  fell ill and was replaced in February 1957 by Kishi Nobusuke, described by Secretary of State
  Dulles as "the strongest government leader to emerge in postwar Japan"; Kishi's June 1957 state
  visit to Washington (during which the US withdrew troops and opened treaty renegotiation) set in
  motion the 1958 changes and the January 19, 1960 signing of the revised security treaty, followed
  by mass protests (bringing "millions" into the streets in June 1960 per Kapur 2011), Kishi's
  resignation, and Eisenhower's cancelled celebratory visit.
- **Post-1960 continuity**: Prime Minister Ikeda Hayato announced a ten-year Income Doubling Plan in
  November 1960 (building on an earlier 1957 plan); the 1961 Kennedy administration trade expansion
  measures cut tariffs on Japanese goods and raised Japan's allowable textile exports to the US by
  5%, explicitly to support Ikeda's plan.
- **End of the favorable regime**: the 1969 Nixon Doctrine reduced US troop presence in Asia and
  shifted defense-manpower responsibility to allies; the 1971 "Nixon shock" ended the dollar's gold
  peg and imposed a 10% import surcharge (Nixon is quoted, via a secondary source, as framing this
  as an effort to "stick it to the Japanese"). Japan's annual growth rate fell from a 1960s average
  of about 10% to 3% in 1971 and continued declining into the 1980s, a shift the paper associates
  with (but does not exclusively attribute to) the alliance's fraying, alongside the 1973 OPEC oil-
  price shock.
- **Alternative explanations considered and judged insufficient**: the 1958 Lower House election, in
  which the LDP won 287 of 467 seats (ten fewer than the combined 1955 Liberal-Democrat total) while
  the Japan Socialist Party gained seats (166, more than in 1955) — the authors argue this was not a
  decisive landslide and partly reflected the same US financial support; the 1958 "Iwato" investment
  boom following a yearlong inventory recession, judged unable to explain a full decade of double-
  digit growth; and labor-relations shifts toward "cooperative unionism" (Gordon 1998), noted as
  still incomplete in 1958 given the disruptive 1957–1961 labor activism and the 1960 year-long
  strike at the Mitsui Miike coal mine.
- **Policy-implication discussion**: the conclusion notes that many tools Japan used (export
  subsidies, import tariffs, foreign-investment restrictions) are now constrained by World Trade
  Organization rules, and that contemporary developing countries might instead pursue "second-best"
  strategies (Rodrik 2008) — channeling subsidies through R&D budgets, promoting exports via
  currency undervaluation or special economic zones, or protecting industries via
  health/environmental standards — citing China as an example of a country pursuing such strategies
  despite WTO constraints, while noting China still ranked 92nd in the world in per-capita income at
  the time of writing.

### Limitations

The design is quasi-experimental, not a randomized experiment; causal identification rests on the
synthetic control method's assumption that the weighted donor-pool composite would have tracked
Japan's trajectory absent the 1958 intervention, an assumption evaluated through placebo and leave-
one-out diagnostics rather than direct verification. The pre-treatment predictor set is constrained
by data availability — standard growth predictors (investment, consumption, human capital, trade
openness, inflation) are only available from 1950 onward across countries, so the authors substitute
proxies (economic/population recovery ratios, lagged per-capita GDP levels, UN-membership status); a
footnote reports that substituting Penn World Table predictors (with post-1950 coverage) in a
preliminary analysis yielded similar results. Donor-pool composition is acknowledged as a judgment
call: an earlier draft using a 15-country subset judged most comparable to Japan produced
substantially similar findings, but the authors note that what counts as "similar" to Japan is
itself contested. The post-treatment estimation window is capped at 1958–1968 specifically to avoid
"post-treatment bias" from the alliance's own later unraveling, meaning the analysis does not model
the 1970s growth slowdown or oil-shock period. The authors explicitly state the statistical test
identifies *when* an unexplained growth acceleration began but is not, by itself, a definitive
account of *why*; causal attribution to the alliance rests on the accompanying
qualitative/historical argument, including sources the authors describe as "still-classified"
regarding CIA campaign financing, which could not be independently verified within the article. One
donor country (Hungary) had to be dropped from the in-sample (1951) placebo test because optimal
weights could not be computed for it. The historical narrative section also relies substantially on
secondary histories and edited document collections rather than the authors' own original archival
research throughout.

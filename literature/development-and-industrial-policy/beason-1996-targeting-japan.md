## Abstract

Tests, for 13 Japanese mining and manufacturing sectors over 1955-1990, whether
industrial-policy instruments (JDB loan shares, net subsidies, tariff protection,
corporate tax relief) were concentrated in high-growth or increasing-returns-to-scale
sectors, as the conventional targeting narrative (associated with Johnson, 1982)
implies. Combines sector-level stylized facts and correlation coefficients with
structural translog production-function estimates (scale parameters, TFP growth) and
panel regressions of TFP growth, output growth, and capital accumulation on lagged
policy variables. Finds targeting correlates negatively with both sectoral growth and
scale economies in every period, that no instrument shows a robust positive effect on
productivity growth across specifications, and that mining and textiles — not the
fast-growing machinery sectors — were the principal beneficiaries of most tools.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The paper opens against a body of literature — the authors cite Johnson (1982),
Borrus/Tyson/Zysman (1986), Carliner (1986), Freeman (1987), Dosi/Tyson/Zysman (1990),
and Nester (1990), among others — holding that Japan's Ministry of International
Trade and Industry (MITI) identified promising industries by their growth or
technological potential and directed support toward them, and that this success is
frequently invoked to justify similar targeting policies in the United States. The
authors characterize this literature as resting mainly on case studies and as lacking
a systematic framework: because, in their description, "virtually every sector was
given something," inferring intent from the absolute level of support to any one
sector is not informative, and what matters is each sector's support relative to
other sectors.

Building on this, the paper adopts an explicit operational definition of "targeting":
benefits directed to particular sectors that are not extended to all sectors, as
distinct from broad macroeconomic policy (money-supply management, fiscal policy,
exchange-rate intervention, secular tax-rate changes) which is not treated as
targeting because it does not discriminate across sectors. The one partial exception
is effective tariff protection, which the authors examine both as a deviation from the
average rate (to capture relative/discriminatory protection) and in levels (to capture
the overall level of protection).

A few prior studies are noted as exceptions to the case-study literature's lack of a
systematic framework: Vestal (1987) found most technology-trade support went to
importers, not exporters; Noland (1993) found R&D subsidies (but not protection or
low-interest loans) associated with higher Japanese net exports; Lee (1993) used a CGE
approach the authors view as resting on a problematic 1960s assumption; and Ramseyer
and Rosenbluth (1993) argue Japanese industrial politics reflects personal political
gain rather than dispassionate targeting.

Four conventional instruments are selected: trade protection (tariffs and quotas), net
transfers to sectors (subsidies less indirect taxes), sectoral corporate tax breaks,
and Japan Development Bank (JDB) low-interest loans — chosen because they appear in
nearly all prior studies, have data consistent with growth data, are fiscally costly
(hence more likely a serious intervention than low-cost "administrative guidance"),
and, if policy was coherent, should proxy for unconventional measures too.

Non-traditional interventions (administrative guidance, government-sponsored R&D
consortia, cartels, direct public R&D subsidy) are excluded from the core analysis,
justified by citing prior findings that these measures were ineffective or not
growth-targeted: Weinstein (1995) found no evidence administrative guidance changed
industry behavior; Saxonhouse (1985) rejected joint-research-venture profitability
effects; Sakakibara (1994) found R&D-consortium participation concentrated among
low-growth firms; and Dick (1992) and Weinstein (1995) rejected cartel pricing
effects. On public R&D funding, the paper cites Okimoto and Saxonhouse (1986) and NSF
data: publicly financed R&D was about 20% of gross R&D expenditure in Japan versus
about 50% in the U.S., and the Japanese government funded only 2% of industrial R&D
in 1986 (versus 35% in the U.S.) and 1% in 1970 (versus 43% in the U.S.).

The paper frames the central empirical question as whether targeting affects
productivity, not merely growth, since productivity carries the welfare and
competitiveness implications. It illustrates the puzzle with a contrast rather than a
formal test: Japan's near self-sufficiency in rice shows protection/subsidy can raise
output, but rice selling at several times the world price shows this did not yield
competitive productivity; automobiles, by contrast, were heavily protected in the
1950s-60s and did become internationally competitive — motivating concern that
case-study successes may reflect spurious correlation rather than a systematic link.

Three theoretical channels are set out for how targeting could raise competitiveness:
Schumpeterian (subsidizing technological development), Marshallian (economies of
scale/infant-industry protection), and strategic-trade (import protection as export
promotion, per Krugman, 1984) — reasoning the authors note MITI's own White Papers
invoked at the time. The stated objective is twofold: first, establish whether
fast-growing sectors received disproportionate policy support (a stylized-facts
exercise); second, using structural production-function estimates, test whether the
government preferentially supported increasing-returns sectors and whether any
instrument measurably affected TFP growth, capital accumulation, or output growth.

### Methods and scope

**Sample.** Thirteen Japanese mining and manufacturing sectors (listed in the Key
Findings table below), 1955-1990, also split into a 1955-1973 and a 1974-1990
subperiod bracketing the first oil shock (regarded in the literature, citing
Okuno-Fujiwara, 1991, as a possible industrial-policy regime shift). Each sector
represents roughly 4% of Japanese output on average (largest under 7%), which the
authors argue makes it unlikely that finely targeted sub-sectoral programs invisible
at this aggregation materially affect Japan's overall growth.

**Policy variables.** JDB = real JDB loans / total loans to the sector (share of
borrowing obtained at a subsidized rate). Subsidies = real net subsidies (subsidies
less indirect taxes) / real gross output, times 100. Tariff = Shouda's (1982)
effective rate of protection, as a deviation from the yearly cross-sector average
(positive = more protected than average); a level version (TAR) is used in the
regression stage. Tax = effective corporate tax rate (receipts / taxable earnings), as
a deviation from the yearly average (positive = more tax relief than average). An NTB
(quota-coverage) measure is used only from 1965 as a secondary robustness check.

**Stylized-facts stage.** Sectors are ranked by growth rate and by level on each of
the four policy measures, for the full period and each subperiod, and simple
correlation coefficients are computed between growth and each policy tool (source
Tables 1-2).

**Structural stage.** Output for each sector is modeled with a translog production
function in labor, materials, and capital plus a technological-change time trend
(a second-order approximation to an arbitrary technology). Following Chan and
Mountain (1983), production is assumed homogeneous of an estimated degree theta-k
(theta-k = 1: constant returns; < 1: decreasing returns; > 1: increasing returns).
Cost-minimization and competitive factor markets give a companion factor-share system;
cross-equation restrictions leave three equations per sector (one output, two share).
The 13-sector system is stacked as a nonlinear seemingly-unrelated-regressions system
(correlated errors within/across sectors), estimated via least-squares starting
values, then nonlinear least squares, then full-system maximum likelihood. For
convergence it was split into two five-sector blocks (mining/food/textiles/pulp-
paper/chemicals; basic metals/fabricated metal/general machinery/transportation
equipment/electrical machinery — three sectors are consequently absent from the
reported scale-parameter table); the authors report this split did not materially
affect coefficients since there are no cross-industry restrictions. TFP growth is
computed from a discrete approximation generalizing the standard Tornqvist index to
theta-k not equal to 1, collapsing to the conventional Tornqvist measure under
constant returns. Fit was strong: output-equation R-squared exceeded 0.99 in every
sector except mining (0.86); share equations had R-squared around 0.5, with 22 of 30
first-order (alpha) coefficients significant at 5%.

**Second-stage panel regressions.** An "impact function" (equation 5) regresses TFP
growth on one- and five-year lags of deviation-form policy variables (tax relief, JDB
loans, subsidies, tariff deviation) plus level tariff protection and NTB coverage,
via fixed-effects panel regression (sector-specific technological-progress rates)
over 1960-1990 (N = 310). Robustness checks: per-sector regressions with an F-test of
joint-zero policy coefficients; ten leave-one-sector-out re-estimations; a
median-growth-rate "slow grower"/"fast grower" split; a subperiod split at the first
oil shock (1960-1973, N = 140; 1974-1990, N = 170); substitution of an independent
Jorgenson and Kuroda (1990) TFP series (1961-1985, constant returns imposed,
different capital-stock benchmark); addition of the NTB/quota variable for
1970-1990; a first-differenced simultaneous production/share-equation estimation
(correcting for policy correlated with factor inputs); and a TFP measure from
production-function residuals alone (dropping share equations, against factor-price
distortion). A parallel regression (Table 7) applies the same lagged policy variables
directly to output-growth and net-capital-accumulation rates rather than TFP.

**Data sources.** Economic Planning Agency Annual Report on the National Accounts
(output, factor shares, subsidies, deflated via Bank of Japan price indexes); EPA
Capital Stock of Private Enterprises; Ministry of Labor surveys (labor input); Bank of
Japan Economic Statistics Monthly (JDB loans); Shouda (1982), interpolated/
extrapolated (tariffs); Genko Yunyu Seido (quotas); National Tax Office records (tax
rates).

Rank 1 = most favorable treatment (highest JDB share, subsidy rate, protection, tax
relief); rank 13 = least favorable. Mining ranked first on JDB loans, net subsidies,
and tax relief but next-to-last (rank 13) on tariffs — attributed to resource-poor
Japan needing cheap raw-material imports. Textiles, second-slowest grower (2.73%/yr),
received the second-highest tariff deviation (17.0) and third-highest subsidy rate.
Processed food (6.29%/yr, median grower) received by far the highest tariff
protection (44.7, more than double the next sector). The three fastest growers —
electrical machinery (12.17%/yr), general machinery (11.39%/yr), transportation
equipment (10.76%/yr) — ranked below median on JDB loans (ranks 8, 12, 7) and tied at
the same below-average tax relief (-0.403, rank 8); electrical machinery is singled
out as receiving little enough, despite attention to its semiconductor sub-sector,
that policy plausibly raised its cost of capital and taxed it above average.

By subperiod: mining's JDB share was 28.10 (rank 1) in 1955-1973, an even larger gap
than the full-period figure, falling to 8.68 (rank 2, behind petroleum and coal's
15.95) by 1974-1990. Textiles' growth turned negative in 1974-1990 (-0.23%/yr, slowest
of all 13) while still receiving the second-highest tariff deviation (20.2). Processed
food's tariff deviation rose to 72.7 (rank 1) in 1974-1990 even as growth slowed to
3.88%/yr. The three machinery/equipment sectors remained fastest growers in both
subperiods, consistently ranked in the bottom half on most policy measures.

**Correlation between sectoral growth and targeting (source Table 2).**

| Period | Growth-JDB | Growth-Subsidies | Growth-Tariffs | Growth-Tax relief |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1955-1990 | -0.31 | -0.13 | -0.31 | -0.55 |
| 1955-1973 | -0.48 | -0.05 | -0.11 | -0.47 |
| 1974-1990 | -0.07 | -0.34 | -0.14 | -0.77 |

Every correlation, in every period, is negative: higher targeting associates with
lower growth, with the tax-relief correlation largest in magnitude throughout (-0.55
full period, rising to -0.77 in 1974-1990). A footnote adds a quota-coverage/growth
correlation of -0.3 (not tabulated, incomplete series), and notes that dropping
mining shrinks the JDB-growth correlation to below 0.1 in both subperiods while the
other three instruments' negative correlation with growth survives — read as possibly
indicating a growing tendency to assist declining sectors over time.

**Estimated scale parameters (source Table 3).**

| Sector | Estimated scale parameter (theta) |
|---|---|
| Mining | 0.671 |
| Processed Food | 0.730 |
| Textiles | 0.556 |
| Pulp & Paper | 0.604 |
| Chemicals | 0.960 |
| Basic Metals | 0.893 |
| Fabricated Metal | 1.07 |
| General Machinery | 1.04 |
| Transportation Equipment | 1.08 |

(Petroleum and coal, precision instruments, and ceramics/stone/glass are not reported
in this table.) Six sectors show decreasing returns (theta < 1); four show constant-
or-mild-increasing returns (theta marginally above 1), and for these four the authors
cannot statistically reject constant returns — nor can most of the "decreasing
returns" sectors reject it either. The paper concludes there is little support for
scale economies being central to Japanese sectoral performance, describing constant
or decreasing returns as the better overall characterization. A footnote cross-checks
against Nakamura (1993), whose cost-function estimates for chemicals, general
machinery, electrical machinery, and transportation equipment (1.232, 1.007, 1.103,
0.966) broadly match this paper's 0.960, 1.04, 1.01, and 1.08.

**Correlation between scale parameters and targeting (source Table 4).**

| Period | Scale-JDB | Scale-Subsidies | Scale-Tariffs | Scale-Tax relief |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1955-1990 | -0.29 | 0.14 | -0.27 | -0.34 |
| 1955-1973 | -0.31 | 0.08 | 0.02 | -0.28 |
| 1974-1990 | -0.26 | 0.10 | -0.39 | -0.76 |

As with growth, scale economies correlate negatively with JDB loans and tariff
protection in most periods; subsidies show a small positive correlation with scale in
all three periods. The authors read this as showing the government did not
systematically direct these instruments toward increasing-returns sectors, "except
perhaps to tax sectors with increasing returns more heavily" (the tax-relief
correlation is negative and grows sharply more negative, to -0.76, in 1974-1990).

**Impact of targeting on TFP growth (source Table 5; dependent variable: sectoral
TFP growth, panel with sector fixed effects).** In the full-sample specification
(1960-1990, N = 310), the equation explains only about 7% of TFP growth (R-squared =
0.068, adjusted R-squared = 0.007). Only one-year-lagged JDB loans (coefficient
0.00396, t = 3.670) and one-year-lagged tax relief (-0.00409, t = -2.880) are
statistically significant; five-year lags of both, and both lags of subsidies and
tariff deviation, are not. Virtually all the cross-sectional variation behind the JDB
result comes from mining (JDB loans up to 16% of all loans in some years); since
machinery sectors received below-average JDB shares, the coefficient implies loan
policy was, if anything, negatively associated with their productivity growth.

Splitting at the growth-rate median: among "slow growers," JDB(-1) stays significant
and positive (0.00469, t = 3.900) and tax relief(-1) stays significant and negative
(-0.00523, t = -3.051); among "fast growers" neither is significant (JDB(-1):
-0.00616, t = -0.973; tax relief(-1): 0.00435, t = 1.097). Splitting at the oil
shock: in 1960-1973 (N = 140) none of tax relief, JDB, or tariff deviation is
significant (t = -0.022, -0.734, 0.855); in 1974-1990 (N = 170), one-year tariff
deviation turns significant and negative (-0.00438, t = -2.719) and level tariff
protection turns significant and positive (0.00441, t = 3.058) — attributed almost
entirely to mining's extremely low tariffs plus processed food's extremely high
protection in that subperiod, not a general tariff-productivity relationship.
Per-sector F-tests could not reject, for any of the 13 sectors, that all policy
coefficients are zero; dropping each sector in turn left results qualitatively
unchanged (not separately tabulated).

**Robustness of the null productivity result (source Table 6).** Substituting the
Jorgenson and Kuroda (1990) TFP series (1961-1985, constant returns imposed)
reproduces the same absence of a policy-productivity relationship. Adding NTB/quota-
coverage variables for 1970-1990 leaves the fit essentially unchanged; neither the
one-year lag (0.088, t = 0.939) nor the five-year lag (0.0065, t = 0.175) of NTB is
significant. A first-differenced simultaneous estimation of the production and share
equations together, and a TFP measure from production-function residuals alone
(dropping the share equations), both reproduce the same null result; R-squared across
these alternatives ranges roughly 0.02-0.10, adjusted R-squared mostly at or below
zero. Correlations between policy and factor usage were mixed: protection was
essentially uncorrelated with capital intensity (rho = 0.03) but negatively
correlated with labor share (rho = -0.64); subsidies were positively correlated with
labor share (rho = 0.40) and negatively with capital share (rho = -0.40); taxes and
loans were positively correlated with both (around rho = 0.5) — concentrated in
1955-1973 and largely gone after 1973.

Unlike the TFP regressions, these show several significant relationships: one-year
JDB loans and level tariff protection (TAR) are significantly positive for both
output growth and capital accumulation. One-year deviation-form tariff protection
(DTAR) is significantly negative in both, which the authors flag as "the wrong sign"
relative to TAR, attributing the net positive protection effect to TAR's larger
coefficient outweighing DTAR's. Five-year subsidies (DSUB(-5)) are significantly
positive for capital accumulation (t = 4.373) but not for output growth alone. Tax
relief is only marginally significant (capital accumulation, t = -2.016); the authors
attribute this to corporate tax rates varying little across sectors besides mining
(citing Saxonhouse, 1983).

**Headline conclusion.** The paper's synthesis: Japanese industrial policy appears
able to have raised output growth and capital formation in the sectors that received
it, but this stimulus — via JDB loans, subsidies, and tariff protection — was
directed disproportionately toward mining and, to a lesser degree, textiles and
processed food, not the fast-growing machinery and equipment sectors. Growth from
targeting seems to have come from greater use of raw materials and capital rather
than more efficient use of them: none of the four instruments shows a robust
positive effect on TFP growth in mining or manufacturing, across the full sample,
growth-rate subsamples, oil-shock subperiods, alternative TFP measures, or
alternative estimation strategies. The authors offer two competing, unadjudicated
interpretations: either the authorities tried to target high-growth sectors and
largely failed, or allocation was instead dominated by a desire to assist declining
sectors or protect large, politically important but unproductive industries.

### Limitations

- The authors themselves caveat that their coefficients may reflect correlation
  rather than causation, framing conclusions accordingly.
- The 13-sector aggregation (each sector averaging about 4% of output, largest under
  7%) cannot rule out finely targeted sub-sector programs (e.g., a semiconductor
  sub-segment of electrical machinery) invisible at this level.
- Non-traditional instruments (administrative guidance, R&D consortia, cartels,
  direct public R&D subsidy) are excluded from the regressions themselves; the
  exclusion rests on other authors' findings (Weinstein 1995; Saxonhouse 1985;
  Sakakibara 1994; Dick 1992), not on tests within this paper.
- The quota/NTB series is incomplete (from 1965 only, item-count rather than
  import-weighted coverage) and the tariff series is interpolated/extrapolated around
  Shouda's (1982) five-year benchmarks.
- Mining fits the structural production system noticeably worse than other sectors
  (R-squared 0.86 versus above 0.99 elsewhere), and three of 13 sectors (petroleum and
  coal, precision instruments, ceramics/stone/glass) are absent from the reported
  scale-parameter table.
- Explanatory power in the productivity regressions is low throughout (R-squared
  roughly 0.06-0.14, adjusted R-squared often at or below zero), so even significant
  coefficients explain only a small share of TFP variation.
- Coefficient signs are not always internally consistent — the deviation-form tariff
  variable (DTAR) is significantly negative while the level tariff variable (TAR) is
  significantly positive in the growth/capital-accumulation regressions, a
  discrepancy the authors note but do not fully resolve.
- The sample is restricted to mining and manufacturing; agriculture (rice appears
  only as an illustrative aside) and services fall outside the regression sample.
- Some robustness checks (e.g., the ten leave-one-sector-out re-estimations) are
  reported only as "qualitatively unchanged," without separately tabulated
  coefficients to verify independently.

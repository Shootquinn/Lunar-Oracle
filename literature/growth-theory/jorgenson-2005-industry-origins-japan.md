## Abstract

Builds a new industry-level growth-accounting dataset for Japan covering 1960-2000,
organized around a 47-industry time series of input-output tables that isolates
Computers, Communications Equipment, and Electronic Components from the rest of
Electric Machinery so that information-technology (IT) production can be tracked
separately. Capital, labor, intermediate-input, and output indexes are constructed by
industry using translog (Tornqvist) index numbers and a cost-of-capital methodology
extending Jorgenson and Griliches, then aggregated to the economy level with Domar
industry weights. The paper decomposes aggregate Japanese output growth into the
contributions of capital input, labor input, and total factor productivity (TFP) for
four subperiods spanning the high-growth era, the post-oil-shock slowdown, the
bubble collapse, and the partial 1995-2000 recovery, concluding that both the rapid
pre-1973 growth and the modest late-1990s revival were substantially TFP-driven,
with IT industries playing an outsized role in the revival relative to their small
share of the economy.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The paper's stated objective is to quantify the sources of Japanese economic growth
over 1960-2000 using industry-level data, so that aggregate growth can be traced to
its "industry origins." The authors position the novel contribution as isolating the
IT-producing industries — Computers, Communications Equipment, and Electronic
Components — which sit below the two-digit industrial classification used in prior
Japanese productivity studies they cite (Jorgenson, 1995; Nomura, 2004a; Fukao et
al., 2004). The motivating question, drawn from the US productivity-revival
literature (Moore's Law-driven semiconductor price declines diffusing through
computer and communications-equipment investment, addressed to the "Solow Paradox"
of IT investment not showing up in aggregate productivity statistics), is whether an
analogous IT-linked dynamic can account for any part of Japan's partial recovery from
the prolonged 1990s recession, versus the broader and more widely diffused US
revival of the same years.

Three specific measurement challenges are set out at the outset. First, the
IT-producing industries are too fine-grained for the two-digit classifications used
in earlier work, so the authors construct detailed data for these industries
directly from Census of Manufacturing and benchmark input-output sources. Second,
the official Japanese national accounts capitalize only custom software as gross
fixed capital formation, not own-account or pre-packaged software, despite the UN's
1993 System of National Accounts (SNA) recommending capitalization of all three; the
paper capitalizes own-account and pre-packaged software using a methodology similar
to the US Bureau of Economic Analysis's (BEA) 2003 benchmark revision, requiring a
rebalancing of the input-output tables. Third, rather than using "internationally
harmonized" IT prices (US prices translated to Japan, as used in cross-country
studies such as Jorgenson, 2004) the paper uses Japan-specific, already
quality-adjusted IT price series from Nomura and Samuels (2004), on the grounds that
harmonized prices are unnecessary for Japan and can be actively misleading: the
paper documents a genuine price gap in the 1980s (Japanese personal-computer prices
fell only 7.6 percent per year versus 13.1 percent in the US, 1980-1995) that it
attributes to real market conditions — NEC's roughly 60-70 percent share of the
Japanese PC market before the 1991 introduction of the DOS/V operating system opened
the market to IBM-compatible imports — rather than to a measurement artifact.

To maintain comparability with the Jorgenson system of US national accounts
(Jorgenson et al., 2005), the paper also extends the accounting boundary in three
further respects: it treats households as a producing sector and imputes capital
services for public infrastructure capital, the land under owner-occupied housing,
and consumer durables. These boundary extensions are not merely definitional; the
authors report their magnitude for 2000: capitalizing infrastructure depreciation
adds 8.1 trillion yen to GDP, software capitalization adds 3.8 trillion yen,
imputed public capital service cost adds 8.4 trillion yen, and imputed land rent
under owner-occupied housing adds 7.9 trillion yen. The consumer-durables
imputation alone adds 25.5 trillion yen, 4.6 percent of redefined GDP — about half
the analogous 9.4 percent US share, which the authors attribute to a smaller stock
of durables and a lower rate of return in Japan.

### Methods and scope

**Data backbone.** The paper extends the KEO Database, a time series of
inter-industry Use and Make tables maintained at the Keio Economic Observatory, on a
43-industry, 47-commodity classification (plus four non-competitive import goods:
Crude Oil, LNG, Iron Ore, and other non-competitive imports) spanning 1960-2000.
Electric Machinery is split into four industries — Computers, Communications
Equipment, Electronic Components, and Other Electrical Machinery — using the RAS
method to balance input columns, producing the 47-industry classification used
throughout the results tables. The Computer industry follows the US SIC-357
definition but excludes SIC-3578/3579 (calculating/accounting machines, office
machines), which the authors place in General Machinery instead, a deliberate
departure from Jorgenson et al. (2005)'s US Computer industry definition made for
consistency with the newer NAICS classification.

**Output and intermediate input (Section 2).** Under constant returns to scale and
competitive markets, industry output, intermediate input, and value added are each
constructed as translog (share-weighted, chain) quantity indexes from the Use and
Make tables, with industry value added defined implicitly via a translog aggregator
of value-added and intermediate-input shares in gross output.

**Capital input (Section 3).** Extends the Jorgenson-Griliches (1995) /
Jorgenson (1996) cost-of-capital framework, incorporating Jorgenson and Stiroh's
(2002) asset-specific revaluation terms and mid-year (rather than year-end)
availability of new investment. Capital stocks for 102 individual asset types
(Table 1: e.g., a 0.347 annual depreciation rate for electronic computers and
peripheral equipment; 0.330 for each of custom, pre-packaged, and own-account
software; near-zero for inventories and land; low single digits for most structures)
are built by the perpetual inventory method using geometric depreciation. Rates for
motor vehicles and housing come from Box-Cox-transformed age-price/age-efficiency
profiles estimated in Japanese second-hand and rental markets (16.3 percent for
passenger vehicles, 22.4-23.8 percent for trucks, 3.1-4.8 percent for housing);
other assets use Japanese tax-life-based declining-balance rates following the
Hulten-Wykoff (1981) method used by the BEA. The cost-of-capital formula
incorporates asset-specific rates of return, depreciation, and revaluation together
with Japan's corporate/business income tax, capital consumption allowances,
acquisition and property taxes, following Nomura (2004a). IT investment and output
prices use Nomura and Samuels' (2004) Japan-specific "best-practice" quality-adjusted
series rather than harmonized US-based prices. Capital quality (compositional shift
toward higher-return assets, e.g., IT equipment) is separated from raw capital stock
growth throughout.

**Labor input (Section 4).** Following Jorgenson and Griliches (1995), labor input is
a translog index aggregated over worker cells cross-classified by sex, 11 age
classes, education (4 classes for males, 3 for females), and 3 employment classes
(employee, self-employed, unpaid family worker), for each of 43 industries — 231
worker types per industry, 9,933 cells in total. Data come from the quinquennial
Population Census, interpolated using the Labour Force Survey, the Basic Survey on
Wage Structure, and the Census of Manufacturing, and reconciled to the KEO
input-output labor-compensation totals. This decomposition separates labor quality
(the effect of compositional shifts, e.g., toward more-educated workers) from raw
hours worked.

**Industry productivity (Section 5).** Industry TFP growth is defined as a
gross-output-based translog residual — output growth net of share-weighted capital,
labor, and intermediate-input growth — rather than a value-added-based measure,
because the gross-output definition captures the role of intermediate inputs such as
semiconductors flowing into computer and communications-equipment production.
Industry average labor productivity (ALP) growth is separately decomposed into
capital-deepening, labor-quality, intermediate-input-deepening, and TFP components.

**Aggregation over industries (Section 6).** The economy-wide production possibility
frontier is aggregated from industry value added, capital, and labor using translog
indexes weighted by Domar (1961) weights — each industry's weight is its share of
aggregate value added divided by the share of value added in its own gross output.
Because gross output exceeds value added for every industry, Domar weights sum to
well over 100 across industries (242.7 in 1960, falling to 185.7 in 2000 as the
aggregate value-added/gross-output ratio rose). This Domar-weighted aggregate TFP
measure is compared against a simpler "aggregate production function" TFP measure
that ignores industry-level reallocation and quality-composition effects, to
quantify the resulting aggregation bias (Table 14).

**Subperiods and scope.** All results are reported for 1960-1973, 1973-1990,
1990-1995, 1995-2000, and the full 1960-2000 span, chosen to bracket the high-growth
era, the post-oil-shock slowdown, the early-1990s bubble collapse, and the partial
late-1990s recovery, across all 47 industries.

### Key findings

**Headline aggregate decomposition (source Table 2).** Contributions to average
annual growth in real aggregate value added (percentage points):

| Period | Value-added growth | Capital input | Labor input | Aggregate TFP |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1960-1973 | 9.89 | 5.16 | 1.69 | 3.05 |
| 1973-1990 | 4.29 | 2.32 | 1.09 | 0.88 |
| 1990-1995 | 1.27 | 1.90 | -0.15 | -0.48 |
| 1995-2000 | 1.34 | 1.17 | -0.18 | 0.35 |
| 1960-2000 | 5.37 | 3.05 | 0.97 | 1.35 |

During the rapid-growth era, TFP supplied nearly a third of the 9.89 percent annual
growth rate (3.05 of 9.89 points), with capital input contributing more in absolute
terms (5.16 points) but TFP driving the era's exceptional pace relative to other
economies at similar capital-accumulation rates; the paper's own framing is that
1960-1973 growth "depended more heavily on growth in TFP" than the comparable US
experience, reflecting Japanese industries closing a technology gap with their US
counterparts. After 1973 the TFP contribution collapsed to 0.88 points and then
turned negative (-0.48 points) in 1990-1995, even as capital's contribution, though
also declining, remained the dominant positive source of growth throughout — 1.90
points in 1990-1995 alone, when total growth was only 1.27 percent. Labor input's
contribution shrank steadily and turned negative from 1990 onward (-0.15, then
-0.18 points), which the authors link to Japan's labor force having "already
entered a phase of decline."

In 1995-2000, TFP's contribution rebounded to +0.35 points even as capital's
contribution nearly halved again (1.17 points) and labor's stayed negative (-0.18
points); this TFP revival is attributed substantially to IT. IT manufacturing
(Computers plus Communications Equipment plus Electronic Components) contributed
0.41 of the total 1.34-point value-added growth in 1995-2000 — close to a third of
all growth — up from 0.15 points in 1990-1995 and exceeding its 0.23- and
0.31-point contributions during the 1960-1973 and 1973-1990 booms, despite the
sector's value-added share reaching only 1.83 percent of the economy by 2000
(close to the 1.62 percent computed for the analogous US IT-manufacturing sector by
Jorgenson et al., 2005). IT manufacturing's TFP contribution of 0.39 points in
1995-2000 was, in the authors' words, "four times higher" than its 0.09-point
contribution in 1990-1995, and by itself nearly matched the entire economy's 0.35-point
aggregate TFP growth for that period — implying non-IT sectors' combined TFP
contribution stayed close to flat in the aggregate even as some individual non-IT
industries improved markedly.

**Which industries drove growth and TFP (source Tables 3, 12, 13).** Over the full
1960-2000 period the three IT-producing industries led output growth by a wide
margin: Computers grew 26.80 percent per year, Electronic Components 17.21 percent,
and Communications Equipment 12.39 percent, versus an economy-wide industry average
of 5.45 percent; Coal Mining was the worst performer at -7.17 percent per year.
Industry TFP growth (Table 12) was likewise highest, in nearly every subperiod, in
Electronic Components (9.57, 7.15, 3.55, 7.96 percent across the four subperiods)
and Computers (7.08, 8.54, 1.03, 6.38 percent). Computers' TFP growth collapsed to
just 1.03 percent per year in 1990-1995 — contrasted directly against 11.87 percent
in the contemporaneous US computer industry (Jorgenson et al., 2005) — which the
paper attributes to weak price competition in a still partly closed Japanese
market; Computers' value-added growth fell from 32.65 percent per year (1973-1990)
to only 3.34 percent (1990-1995) over the same window. Despite consistently
high TFP growth, IT manufacturing's Domar weights stayed small throughout — 3.31
(Electronic Components), 1.39 (Computers), and 1.01 (Communications Equipment) of
an economy-wide total of 185.7 in 2000 — which the paper cites as the reason IT's
aggregate contribution remained modest until the late 1990s despite the sector's
persistently superior TFP performance.

**Structural shift (source Table 5).** Agriculture, Forestry, and Fishery's
value-added share fell from 12.96 percent in 1960 to 5.50 percent in 1973 and just
1.35 percent by 2000. Combined service industries (the paper's categories 30-45)
rose from 36.50 to 50.39 percent of value added over the same 40 years. Among
manufacturing industries, Iron and Steel was largest in 1960 at 3.32 percent of
value added but fell to 1.08 percent by 2000; of all manufacturing industries, only
six — the three IT producers, Other Electrical Machinery, Printing and Publishing,
and Other Manufacturing — gained value-added share over the full period.

**Aggregate production function TFP versus Domar-weighted TFP (source Table 14).**
Comparing the simpler, reallocation-blind "aggregate production function" TFP
measure against the paper's preferred Domar-weighted aggregate TFP:

| Period | Aggregate production function TFP | Domar-weighted aggregate TFP |
|---|---|---|
| 1960-1973 | 3.72 | 3.05 |
| 1973-1990 | 1.89 | 0.88 |
| 1990-1995 | 0.51 | -0.48 |
| 1995-2000 | 1.27 | 0.35 |
| 1960-2000 | 2.24 | 1.35 |

The unadjusted measure overstates TFP growth in every subperiod, by close to a full
percentage point per year in the 1990s (e.g., 1.27 versus 0.35 in 1995-2000) —
evidence, in the authors' framing, that industry-level reallocation and
capital/labor quality-composition effects matter enough that a simple aggregate
production function meaningfully misstates the pace and even the sign of Japan's
productivity growth in the 1990-1995 window (0.51 percent, positive, versus -0.48
percent under the preferred Domar-weighted measure).

**Labor productivity decomposition (source Table 15).** Aggregate labor
productivity (ALP) growth was 7.58, 3.63, 2.27, and 2.02 percent across the four
subperiods (4.55 percent over 1960-2000), driven mainly by capital deepening (4.00,
2.02, 2.36, 1.48 points) with a comparatively small and fairly stable labor-quality
contribution (0.54, 0.74, 0.39, 0.20 points); TFP contributes the same figures as
in the output decomposition (3.05, 0.88, -0.48, 0.35 points). ALP growth actually
declines slightly from the early to the late 1990s (2.27 to 2.02 percent) despite
TFP's revival, because capital deepening and labor quality both weakened over the
same window; the paper notes ALP growth is "heavily influenced by particular
labor-intensive industries," citing Construction and Wholesale and Retail, both of
which post negative labor-productivity growth in 1995-2000 (-1.44 and -2.95 percent
respectively, from Table 16) even as IT-intensive industries posted double-digit
gains.

**IT capital diffusion versus the US.** IT capital's share of aggregate capital-input
growth rose from about 13.52 percent in the 1980s (versus 31.89 percent
contemporaneously in the US) to 42.47 percent by 2000, approaching the US's 46.04
percent — even though Japan's IT capital growth rate itself (11.26 percent per year,
1995-2000) remained well below the US's 19.28 percent, because non-IT capital
growth in Japan slowed even more sharply, to just 1.87 percent per year versus 3.09
percent in the contemporaneous US. The paper reads this as evidence that Japan's
production system incorporated IT capital even through a lengthy recession marked
by an immobilized financial sector, corporate restructuring, and household balance
sheets impaired by the bursting of the bubble economy.

**Headline stance on accumulation versus TFP.** The paper's own synthesis is that
Japan experienced a genuine but narrow, IT-linked productivity revival after 1995 —
visible in IT manufacturing and in some IT-using service industries (Communications:
TFP growth of 3.58 percent per year in 1995-2000; Finance and Insurance: 2.27
percent) — that remained far less broad-based than the contemporaneous US revival.
Aggregate TFP growth of 0.35 percent in 1995-2000 barely offset the -0.48 percent of
1990-1995; the non-manufacturing TFP contribution stayed negative overall (-0.33
points in 1995-2000, per Table 2); and two Domar-heavy service industries with no
IT-linked improvement — Wholesale and Retail and Construction — continued to post
declining TFP indexes through 2000. On the broader input-accumulation-versus-TFP
question, the paper's stance is that Japan's exceptional 1960-1973 growth was
itself substantially TFP-driven (catch-up convergence toward US technology levels),
reversing after 1973 into a growth pattern dominated by capital accumulation with
TFP first weak, then negative, then only partially and narrowly revived by IT after
1995.

### Limitations

- The Domar-weighted aggregate TFP construct rests on constant-returns,
  competitive-market, and separability assumptions that the paper states as
  methodology but does not test empirically.
- The Computer industry's definition (excluding SIC-3578/3579) is a deliberate
  departure from the US Computer industry definition used in the companion
  Jorgenson et al. (2005) study, complicating exact US-Japan comparability even
  though the paper draws such comparisons throughout.
- No satisfactory constant-quality price index for Japanese pre-packaged software
  exists; the paper substitutes a cost-based series derived from the Bank of
  Japan's Corporate Service Price Index. The authors' own sensitivity check shows
  this choice matters: using the BEA's alternative harmonized-cost approach would
  raise Japan's 1995-2000 software-stock growth rate from 6.3 to 8.3 percent per
  year, and the authors acknowledge their chosen method may understate software
  capital growth.
- The underlying KEO input-output benchmark tables are constructed only every
  five years; intervening annual figures are interpolated from secondary sources
  (Labour Force Survey, Census of Manufacturing, and others), so year-to-year
  movements blend benchmark and interpolated data.
- TFP measurement is not adjusted for cyclical capacity utilization (a
  Berndt-Fuss-style adjustment is cited in the references but not applied), so
  recession-period figures — including the -0.48 percent aggregate TFP growth of
  1990-1995 — may partly reflect underused capacity rather than a pure decline in
  efficiency.
- IT manufacturing's Domar weight remains small throughout the sample (roughly 5.7
  of an economy-wide 185.7 total in 2000), and the paper's own conclusion is
  explicit that IT's post-1995 contribution, while large relative to its own
  history, still falls short of a broad-based, US-style productivity revival
  across the Japanese economy as a whole.
- The sample ends in 2000; whether the partial post-1995 TFP revival continued,
  stalled, or reversed afterward is outside the paper's scope.
- Corporate and non-corporate capital services cannot be separately identified
  for Japan because of data constraints, unlike the fuller decomposition available
  for the US in companion work by the same authors.

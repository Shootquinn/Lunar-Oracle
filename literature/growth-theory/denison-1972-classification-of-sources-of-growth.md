## Abstract

*The printed article carries an author abstract. What follows is a description written for
this review, not a reproduction of it; the two direct quotations below are marked as
quotations at the point of use.*

A methods paper, not an empirical one. Denison argues that growth accounting divides
measured output growth among a list of output determinants, and that the list itself --
what counts as a determinant, where the borderlines fall, and what is left in the
residual -- is where most apparent disagreement between studies actually lives. His stated
diagnosis is that authors publishing results usually give no complete or precise
description of the classification they used, and that readers ignore the description even
when it is given. He sets out three criteria a classification should meet (it should unite
cause with effect and match how economists reason; a determinant that does not change
should contribute nothing; and it should be estimable in practice), five ground rules
scoping the exercise to actual national-accounts output series, and then works through the
contested borderlines one at a time: advances in knowledge against capital and against
education, the gap between best and average practice, economies of scale against every
other determinant, the efficiency offset to hours changes, and factor input against
resource reallocation. An appendix prints the resulting fourteen-item classification with
sub-items. There are no Japanese figures in the paper, and no country decomposition of any
kind.

## Summary

### Background and objective

Denison's opening claim is that classification of output determinants "has received too
little attention in growth analysis," and that the result has been ambiguity, confusion,
and controversies in which the participants appear to argue about substance when a
difference in classification is the real cause of their conflicting conclusions. He notes
that omission of a precise classification is *concealed* by the fact that every empirical
study has a statistical residual absorbing whatever was not measured, and states the rule
that governs the rest of the paper: there should be no such thing as a *conceptual*
residual. Determinants inside a residual must be defined as carefully as those estimated
directly, or the meaning of the directly measured contributions is ambiguous too.

He states two objectives. The first is to get authors to describe their classifications
explicitly, by listing the determinants whose placement is not obvious. The second is to
put his own classification on the record and invite argument about it. He concedes at the
outset that any author may use any classification he finds useful and practical, provided
it is consistent and clearly described, and that the accuracy of an author's estimates can
justly be appraised only against that author's own definitions -- a concession that is
load-bearing for how this corpus must read secondary accounts of his Japan work.

### Methods and scope

Five ground rules fix the scope. (1) The subject is analysis of *actual* statistical
series measuring output, not theoretical ones; the practical consequence is that only
changes which affect the measured series can appear in the classification. (2) The output
measure covers the whole economy at national-accounts scope; Denison's own preference is
real national income (net national product at factor cost), and he notes that switching to
gross national product changes the quantitative estimates for individual sources greatly
while leaving the classification alone. (3) The classification must be **complete and
unduplicated**. (4) Practicality is judged assuming the analyst leans to some degree on
marginal-productivity reasoning, or at least on cross-sectional earnings and price data
for weights. (5) The classification should serve present use but also accommodate detail
that is not yet estimable.

Ground rule 3 is the paper's arithmetic constraint and it is worth quoting exactly,
because everything this corpus needs from the paper turns on it. Denison writes that a
general purpose classification of growth sources must be both complete and unduplicated,
and:

> "It must have the characteristic that the sum of the contributions of all growth sources
> (or the product of indexes, depending on the treatment of statistical interaction terms)
> equals the actual growth rate of output as measured."

He restates it later in operational form, arguing that failing to extract directly
obtainable contributions from the output estimates introduces error into the *other*
determinants as well, "because the sum of all contributions must equal the growth rate."

Three criteria guide the design. **Causality**: the chief purpose of growth analysis is to
answer questions of the form "if we alter determinant X by x, how much does output
change," so determinants should identify effect with cause and correspond to categories
economists already use -- capital's contribution tied to saving and investment, resource
allocation's to how labour and capital are allocated. **The no-change rule**: a determinant
that does not change between two dates must contribute nothing, and if no determinant
changes, output does not change; Denison says any deviation from this introduces chaos.
**Practicality for estimation**: some groupings demand estimates that are extremely
difficult or inherently impossible while alternative groupings do not.

He groups estimating procedures into four kinds: contributions readable directly out of
the output estimates themselves (labour outside the business sector, dwellings,
international assets); contributions obtained by the standard input-weighting technique
(quantities plus relative marginal products as weights); determinants judged not to have
changed materially, estimated at zero; and everything else, where procedures run from
tolerable to non-existent. He then states the fact that makes the arithmetic constraint
hard to apply to any published table: **no investigator to date has attempted direct
estimates for every determinant**, so all actual estimates combine some of his listed
determinants, and portions of others, into a single item obtained as a residual. Advances
in knowledge are always in that residual, because no one has found a way to estimate them
directly.

The appendix prints the classification: items 1-3 are the components of total factor input
(labour input, split between the government/household/institutional sector and the
business sector, the latter subdivided eight ways down to health and "other personal
characteristics"; capital input, split among general government capital, dwellings,
international assets, and business non-residential structures, equipment and inventories;
and non-residential land). Items 4-14 are the components of output per unit of input:
resource allocation (4), economies of scale from the size and composition of the national
market (5), economies of scale from geographic concentration (6), advances in knowledge
(7), the gap between average and best practice split three ways into institutional
obstacles, diffusion lags and capital-vintage lags (8a-c), business-government service
costs (9), the legal and human environment including crime and pollution abatement (10),
intensity of utilization with the pressure of demand (11), irregular factors such as
weather and strikes (12), multiple shifts (13), and changes in productive efficiency
independent of everything else, with the quality of management folded in for empirical
work (14).

### Key findings

- **A complete classification's contributions sum to measured growth -- by construction,
  not by luck.** This is ground rule 3, quoted above, and it is the paper's own standard.
  It is stated twice, in the ground rules and again in the discussion of directly
  obtainable contributions.
- **The single licensed exception is the interaction-term convention.** Ground rule 3's
  parenthetical -- "or the product of indexes, depending on the treatment of statistical
  interaction terms" -- is the only place in the paper where a complete component list is
  not required to *add* to the total. Where the components are combined multiplicatively,
  the arithmetic sum of the individual contributions expressed as annual percentage points
  will exceed the compounded total by a small amount, and Denison treats the choice
  between the two as a presentational convention the author must declare.
- **An under-summing list is an incomplete list, and Denison expects incompleteness in
  practice.** Because no investigator estimates every determinant, real tables merge
  determinants into a residual. The residual is *inside* the sum, not outside it: it is a
  line in the accounting, so a published table whose printed lines fall short of the total
  is one whose residual line (or some other determinant) was not printed, or was printed
  under a heading the reader did not recognise as a residual.
- **Scale economies are a separate line in Denison's convention, and this is a real fork.**
  Two traditions exist. Fitted production functions with input coefficients summing to
  more than one classify the extra output from increasing returns *inside* the input
  contributions -- and, because they do not isolate the determinants of output per unit of
  input, some scale gains end up with inputs and some do not. Denison's own convention sets
  the input weights to sum to one, so every scale gain raises output per unit of input, and
  a separate economies-of-scale entry collects the extra gain arising when output changes
  for *any* reason, input or otherwise. He accepts an explicit cost of this: the
  classification suitable for historical accounting is then *not* the one suitable for
  answering "what would a one percent rise in labour input do," because that question
  requires the scale effect to be added back.
- **Item 5 is a passive residual of the other determinants.** Denison conceives economies
  of scale from the size and composition of the national market as something that cannot be
  independently influenced; every way market size *can* be attacked is already covered
  elsewhere, mostly under resource allocation (item 4) and the efficiency-obstacle item
  (8a). Item 5 is defined as exactly the amount that would be redistributed to the other
  determinants if the production-function convention were adopted instead.
- **Advances in knowledge must appear whole, in one place.** The contribution is
  comprehensive: all gains from technical, managerial or organizational knowledge,
  regardless of source and regardless of how they enter production, including improvements
  in the design of structures and equipment ("unmeasured quality change" in capital goods,
  the difference between capital stock indexes "J" and "K"). Denison's practical argument
  is that K can be calculated with tolerable accuracy while J can scarcely be estimated at
  all; his conceptual argument is that dispersing knowledge gains among inputs destroys the
  unity of the category and forecloses the sub-division by source he hopes future research
  will supply. Nothing is transferred to the education component of labour input either:
  education enters only as an aspect of labour input, and the classification therefore
  provides **no** comprehensive measure of the effects of education on growth.
- **Denison estimates advances in knowledge at roughly three-quarters to one percentage
  point a year for advanced Western countries in the recent past.** This is a Western
  figure offered as perspective on the magnitude of the residual; it is not a Japanese
  figure and this file does not carry one.
- **Reallocation gains are kept whole too, and inputs are deliberately *not* subdivided by
  use.** Full-time farm and non-farm workers of the same sex, age and education get equal
  weight in labour input, and a dollar of structures gets equal weight in farm and non-farm
  use, despite the known earnings and return differentials. The consequence Denison spells
  out is that each input's contribution refers to the output increase that input would have
  produced had the total cost of misallocation been unchanged, so all gains from improved
  allocation of any input land in item 4. Weighting inputs separately by industry,
  occupation or legal form would transfer reallocation gains into the input contributions,
  which he calls highly undesirable -- but he insists the classification change be
  recognised when it is made.
- **Two hours-related conventions shift substantial amounts between lines without changing
  the total.** The "efficiency offset" case: a one percent cut in hours that reduces output
  only 0.7 percent is charged entirely to labour input in his classification (leaving
  output per unit of input unchanged), with the -1.0 and +0.3 shown as sub-items so a
  reader can move the +0.3 into output per unit of input if he prefers. And counting a
  full-time week rather than an hour as the unit of labour input means a shift from farm to
  paid non-farm employment does not reduce labour input; he notes plainly that the
  alternative convention "leads to a smaller contribution from labour input and to a larger
  contribution from resource reallocation," that the difference is quantitatively
  significant in the United States, and that either procedure may be acceptable **provided
  the one adopted is specified clearly**.
- **The residual is not "our ignorance," and the distinction matters.** In a footnote
  Denison corrects a common reading of Abramovitz: the *residual* is a measure of our
  ignorance, but output per unit of input is not, and he senses the distinction is often
  overlooked -- with the effect that ignorance is supposed to be reduced by shovelling as
  many determinants as possible into the input measure.
- **The closing statement is a two-sided obligation.** Denison hopes the paper convinces
  the reader that growth analysis is full of borderline cases whose classification anyone
  providing estimates must specify -- and that users of estimates have an obligation to
  read carefully.

### Limitations

- **It carries no Japanese data.** There is no country decomposition anywhere in the paper.
  Denison's own empirical references are to *Why Growth Rates Differ* (nine Western
  countries, 1967) and to the United States study then in progress. Nothing in this file
  supplies, corroborates or corrects any component figure attributed to Denison and Chung
  (1976), and it must not be cited as if it did.
- **It is normative about classification, not about estimation.** Denison says he will
  discuss estimating procedures as little as possible. The paper therefore states what a
  classification must satisfy without demonstrating that his own published estimates
  satisfy it in any particular table.
- **The classification is admittedly not exhaustive.** A footnote states it provides for
  all the main determinants but is "not absolutely comprehensive"; others may matter in
  particular times and places, and he has deliberately omitted "trick" ways of raising
  measured output such as restricting consumer variety.
- **Several borderlines are left explicitly unresolved by the author himself.** Whether
  economies of scale should be a separate determinant or dispersed among the others is
  called debatable in his own abstract framing; item 8c (the capital-vintage lag) he says
  he has no strong feeling about; and the quality of management (item 14) he concedes is
  conceptually covered under labour input and resource allocation but cannot at present be
  handled comprehensively that way.
- **Vintage: 1972.** It predates Denison and Chung's Japan work, predates the growth
  accounting literature's later shift to industry-level and translog index-number methods
  (Jorgenson and Nomura 2005 in this corpus), and its estimation-feasibility judgements are
  those of the early 1970s.
- **Provenance caveat on the citation fields.** The PDF text layer carries no journal
  masthead. Volume, issue and DOI rest on the acquisition record, not on the file's own
  bytes; see `## Citation`.

### Topic mapping

Economics. Its use here is as a **method authority**, not as a source of figures.

Its bearing on `ECR-08` (the Denison and Chung Japan decomposition, a `one_sided` axis
reached only through reviews) is this: the axis's two review-sourced sides print component
lists that do not reconcile with the totals they state -- May's five components sum to 8.81
against a stated 8.77, Simonis's seven sum to 8.74 against a stated 9.56. This paper is
Denison stating, primary and in his own voice, that a complete and unduplicated
classification's contributions **must** sum to (or multiply to) measured growth. It
therefore rules out the reading that Denison's method licenses a component list that does
not reconcile, and it supplies the diagnosis for why published accounts of one study
disagree anyway: authors do not describe their classification completely, readers ignore
what description is given, and every real table folds unestimated determinants into a
residual. It adds **no side** to `ECR-08` and no Japanese quantity. Its other standing use
is as the definitional reference for the growth-accounting vocabulary the economics half of
this corpus uses throughout -- total factor input, output per unit of input, the residual,
advances in knowledge, economies of scale, resource reallocation -- where secondary sources
in the corpus use those terms without defining them.

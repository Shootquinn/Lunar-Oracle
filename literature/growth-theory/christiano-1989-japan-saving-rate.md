## Abstract

Tests, via a calibrated neoclassical (Solow/Kydland-Prescott-style) closed-economy
growth model, whether Japan's high 1960s-1970s national saving rate can be explained
as reconstruction of capital destroyed in World War II — the "reconstruction
hypothesis" attributed to Hayashi (1986). A standard model, parameterized on U.S.
postwar data, dissipates 95% of a war-scale capital shock within 26 years and predicts
a saving rate that peaks near 50% of NNP immediately postwar before declining
monotonically — contradicted by the actual hump-shaped Japanese saving path, which
peaks roughly 24 years after the war. A modified model adding a subsistence-consumption
term (c* = 0.76) reproduces the hump shape and stretches the model's adjustment lag to
72 years, yielding a quantitatively consistent — though not independently validated —
account.

## Summary

### Background and objective

Over the 20 years prior to publication, Japan's national saving rate had, on average,
exceeded that of the United States, driven mainly by very high Japanese saving in the
late 1960s and early 1970s, followed by a convergence toward U.S. levels. A further
recent uptick in the Japanese rate raised a live policy question of whether it was
permanent — feeding pressure in the U.S. Congress for protectionist trade legislation
premised on Japan running a chronic, saving-driven trade surplus — or a temporary
deviation from continued convergence. The paper's stated purpose is to evaluate the
"reconstruction hypothesis" proposed by Fumio Hayashi (1986, and in a companion article
in the same Quarterly Review issue, "Is Japan's Saving Rate High?"): that Japan's
elevated saving in the late 1960s/early 1970s reflects a neoclassical growth response to
the partial destruction of its physical capital stock during World War II, under the
assumption that capital inflows from abroad were restricted (by capital controls or
foreign investor uncertainty), so reconstruction had to be financed domestically.
Hayashi's version of the hypothesis holds that reconstruction was not completed until
the early 1980s, after which Japanese and U.S. saving rates should be similar on average
going forward.

The author (Lawrence J. Christiano, Research Officer, Federal Reserve Bank of
Minneapolis Research Department, and NBER Research Affiliate) sets out to evaluate this
hypothesis quantitatively rather than only qualitatively, using the class of formal
neoclassical growth model that macroeconomists of the period — following Kydland and
Prescott (1982) and Prescott (1986) — had begun applying to assess candidate
explanations for other macroeconomic phenomena (e.g., business-cycle volatility,
inventory behavior, labor-market fluctuations). The paper frames its own negative result
as informative rather than fatal to the hypothesis: a standard model's failure to match
the data could reflect the model's own missing features rather than a flaw in the
underlying reconstruction story, and the paper goes on to test that possibility
directly.

### Methods and scope

The analysis uses a representative-agent neoclassical growth model with a Cobb-Douglas
production function Y_t = (z_t N_t)^(1-theta) K_t^theta, where z_t (labor-augmenting
technology) and N_t (working-age population) grow exogenously at fixed rates x and n,
and theta is capital's share parameter. The representative agent maximizes discounted
log-utility of consumption, summed from t = 1946 forward with discount rate rho,
subject to the capital accumulation constraint, over a simulation horizon running from
1946 through 1999. Rather than estimate parameters from Japanese data, the model is
calibrated using postwar U.S. estimates from the author's own prior work (Christiano,
1988; Christiano and Eichenbaum, 1988b): discount rate rho = 0.03 (imposed a priori) and
capital share theta = 0.36, among other parameters. The justification given is that the
neoclassical explanation itself abstracts from all Japan-U.S. differences except initial
capital stocks, so results should be insensitive, to a first approximation, to the data
source used for parameters — a claim the paper explicitly flags as "worth further
investigation" rather than as settled.

The initial capital shock is calibrated by interpolating data from Romer (1986, p. 229,
Fig. 1) indicating that Japanese output in 1946 was about 47% below its prewar trend.
Attributing all of this shortfall to a fall in the 1946 capital stock (given theta =
0.36) implies K_1946 was only about 12% of its steady-state value — i.e., about 88%
below the steady-state growth path for physical capital. The model is solved by a
grid-approximation dynamic-programming method, detailed in an Appendix, rather than the
linear-quadratic (LQ) method of Kydland and Prescott (1982); the LQ method is judged
reliable only over the small variation ranges typical of business-cycle applications,
whereas this exercise requires accuracy over an extremely wide range of capital values.

Two model variants are simulated and compared against Japanese time series (saving
rate, output growth rate, capital-to-output ratio, and return on capital) assembled by
Hayashi, the Japan Securities Research Institute, and the Japan Economic Planning
Agency:

1. the **standard model** — baseline log-utility, no subsistence term (c* = 0); and
2. the **slow convergence model** — preferences modified to log(C_t - c* z_t N_t) with
   c* = 0.76, a subsistence-consumption term intended to capture near-subsistence living
   standards in the immediate postwar period.

A welfare-loss calculation compares the value function under the standard model's truly
optimal policy against the value implied by following the slow-convergence model's path
(used as a proxy for the historical path, given the absence of reliable Japanese
consumption data for the first postwar decade). This comparison is used to gauge
whether the standard model's forecast errors are economically material or merely a
modeling nuance.

### Key findings

- Under the standard model, 95% of the effects of a war-scale capital-destruction shock
  dissipate within **26 years** — far short of the roughly 40-year adjustment period
  ("reconstruction completed only in the early 1980s") that Hayashi's hypothesis
  requires.
- The standard model predicts the Japanese saving rate should have **peaked immediately
  after the war at nearly 50% of NNP** and then declined monotonically. Actual Japanese
  data instead show a **hump-shaped** saving rate: very low immediately postwar, not
  peaking until about **24 years after the war** (i.e., around the late 1960s), then
  falling — a pattern the standard model cannot reproduce.
- The output growth rate in the actual data peaks in the **early 1960s**, before the
  saving-rate peak at the **end of the 1960s**; the standard model instead has both
  series peak together, immediately postwar, with no such lag between them.
- A welfare comparison finds the discrepancy between the standard model's optimal
  saving policy and the proxy historical path economically large: the representative
  agent would be willing to forgo up to **90% of the 1946 capital stock** (a
  stock-equivalent measure) — or, equivalently, accept a permanent **10% reduction in
  consumption** (a flow-equivalent measure) — in exchange for being allowed to follow
  the model's truly optimal policy rather than the historically-observed-like path. This
  is used to argue the standard model's failure is not a matter of small, economically
  trivial mis-specification.
- The **modified "slow convergence" model**, using c* = 0.76, takes **72 years** to
  close 95% of the 1946 capital gap (versus 26 years in the standard model) and
  reproduces the hump-shaped saving-rate pattern closely — described as looking "like
  the trend line one might draw through the Japanese data." It also better matches the
  timing of the growth-rate peak (early 1960s) ahead of the saving-rate peak (late
  1960s), and better matches the capital-to-output ratio's behavior in the 1970s.
- In steady state, the model's implied real return on capital is **R = 6.04%**, close
  to the author's own separately estimated postwar U.S. averages of **6.43%** (S&P 500
  real return) and **5.26%** (economywide capital-stock return) (Christiano, 1989b).
  Both the standard and slow-convergence models converge to this same steady-state
  return, but the slow-convergence model implies substantially **higher** early-period
  returns on capital — arguably more consistent with a high-return reconstruction
  phase — though data volatility limits firm conclusions on this point.
- Detrended 1946 consumption (c_1946) differs sharply across models: 0.425 in the
  standard model (about one-third of its steady-state value), versus 0.762 in the
  slow-convergence model — just above the c* = 0.76 subsistence floor, avoiding the
  "infinitely painful" low-consumption path the standard model implies.
- Corroborating cross-country context (Barro, 1987, ch. 11): among nine industrialized
  countries, those starting the 1950s with the lowest per-capita output had the highest
  subsequent output growth and investment rates, and growth rates fell over time in
  initially low-income countries while remaining stable in initially high-income
  countries (e.g., the United States) — a pattern qualitatively consistent with
  neoclassical convergence.
- Per-capita income convergence context: in 1987, U.S. per-capita GNP was **$18,559**
  versus a converted Japanese figure of **$19,542** (from a reported 2.83 million yen
  per capita, converted at an exchange rate of 144.6 yen per dollar) — illustrating how
  close the two countries' living standards had become by the late 1980s.
- A specific data problem for the hypothesis is flagged: Hayashi's companion-issue data
  show the Japanese capital-to-output ratio was **high and falling in the mid-to-late
  1950s**, which, taken at face value, would imply the war's impact on Japan's capital
  stock was already eliminated by the mid-1950s — undermining any causal link between
  wartime destruction and the later 1960s-1970s saving surge. The paper proceeds on the
  assumption, following Hayashi, that this reflects measurement error, without
  independently verifying that assumption.
- Numerical-solution appendix: grid-approximation accuracy was evaluated at grid sizes
  from 1,000 to 5,000 points, computed on the Minneapolis Fed's Amdahl 5860 mainframe
  (reported run times ranging from about 8 minutes to over 200 minutes for the largest
  grids). For the standard model (c* = 0), the marginal product of capital (R_t) and the
  marginal rate of substitution (MRS_t) differed by no more than about 0.06% on average
  at M = 2,000 on a fixed-interval grid, indicating high solution accuracy. For the
  slow-convergence model (c* = 0.76), accuracy was markedly worse near the shock date:
  R_t and MRS_t differed by as much as 219% at t = 1 with M = 1,000, improving to 61% at
  M = 2,000 — reflecting the added numerical difficulty introduced by the
  near-subsistence consumption term.
- A footnoted contemporary account is cited for context rather than evidence: Jones
  (1985) reports saving rates were low just after the war "as real income was reduced to
  a near subsistence level," a claim the author notes is not literally biological, since
  Japanese per-capita income in 1946 was in fact higher than in the first two decades of
  the twentieth century (per Romer, 1986, Fig. 1).
- Two rival interpretations of why the standard model over-predicts immediate postwar
  investment are discussed: (i) a subsistence-consumption effect, the interpretation
  built into the slow-convergence model, versus (ii) unmodeled adjustment costs of
  shifting resources rapidly into investment. The paper suggests these two accounts
  could in principle be distinguished using rate-of-return-on-investment data, since the
  subsistence-level account implies a very high early postwar return on investment while
  the adjustment-cost account implies a lower return once adjustment costs are netted
  out — but notes that adequate return data to perform this test were not available to
  it.
- The subsistence-level parameter is also discussed as potentially standing in for a
  rising minimum acceptable standard of living over time, or for habit-formation-type
  preferences in which the acceptable consumption floor depends on recent past
  consumption (citing Constantinides, 1988, and Nason, 1988, as alternative ways to model
  this without an exogenous shift in c*).

### Limitations

- Parameters (discount rate, capital share, depreciation, and others) are calibrated
  entirely from U.S. postwar data rather than Japan-specific estimates, justified only
  by an untested "first approximation" claim that results are insensitive to the data
  source used.
- No reliable Japanese consumption data exist for the first postwar decade, so the
  standard model's welfare loss could not be compared directly against actual historical
  data; the slow-convergence model's simulated path is substituted as a proxy, which the
  paper explicitly flags as a second-best approach.
- The initial capital-stock shortfall (88% below steady state in 1946) is derived from a
  single interpolated data source (Romer, 1986) and attributes the entire prewar-trend
  output shortfall to capital destruction alone, with no allowance for reduced
  technology (z_t) or population (N_t) contributions; the author explicitly calls this
  an overstatement of the true capital shortfall.
- The model is a closed economy with an extreme, not separately modeled, restriction on
  capital inflows. International capital flows, variable labor supply/hours worked, and
  endogenous (rather than exogenous) technological progress are all omitted; the author
  notes each of these extensions would likely make it harder, not easier, to rationalize
  the observed roughly 40-year adjustment lag.
- The subsistence-level parameter (c* = 0.76) in the better-fitting modified model is
  chosen specifically to match the observed Japanese saving pattern, so its good fit is
  not independent evidence for the reconstruction hypothesis; the author states
  explicitly that this modification does not vindicate the hypothesis, only that it
  demonstrates one internally consistent way to rationalize it.
- A key piece of contrary evidence — a high-and-falling Japanese capital-to-output ratio
  in the mid-to-late 1950s that would otherwise imply war damage was already resolved
  before the high-saving period began — is set aside as presumed measurement error
  rather than independently resolved within the paper.
- The paper's own "Suggested Research" section calls for further work confirming whether
  the 1950s capital-output ratio data reflect measurement error, obtaining reliable
  rate-of-return-on-investment data for early postwar Japan, testing variable-hours and
  open-economy versions of the model, and testing versions with endogenous technological
  progress — all left unaddressed within the paper itself.
- Numerical solution accuracy is demonstrably weaker for the modified (subsistence-
  consumption) model than for the standard model, particularly in early periods close to
  the capital shock, per the author's own grid-convergence diagnostics in the Appendix.

### Topic mapping

FA1. Sub-Q 2.

Touches the macroeconomic financing conditions underlying Japan's postwar catch-up
growth — specifically the behavior of national saving and investment rates,
capital-stock recovery dynamics, and rate-of-return-on-capital paths during
reconstruction — rather than industrial-policy mechanisms, sectoral targeting, or
technology-transfer institutions as such. Relevant to interest in the financing side of
catch-up growth and in growth-theoretic accounts of postwar recovery, as a formal test
of whether a war-damage/reconstruction mechanism alone, independent of industrial
policy or state-industry coordination, can quantitatively account for Japan's high
saving rate and growth trajectory across the 1950s-1970s.

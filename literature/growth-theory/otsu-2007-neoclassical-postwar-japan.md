## Abstract

Calibrated one-sector stochastic neoclassical growth model of the postwar
Japanese economy, 1952-2000. A representative infinitely-lived household, a
constant-returns-to-scale firm, and a government that collects and fully
rebates labor income tax are hit by three exogenous, empirically-fed shocks: a
one-time destruction/upward-steady-state-shift in capital stock, total factor
productivity (TFP), and a labor-market wedge. The model is solved by log-
linearization around a 1989 reference steady state and compared to Japanese
time-series data expressed as log deviations from balanced growth. Two
household preference specifications are contrasted: standard Cobb-Douglas, and
Stone-Geary with a subsistence-consumption floor. The paper reports that
capital destruction together with the observed TFP path reproduces postwar time
paths of capital, output, consumption, and investment, while the postwar
decline in labor supply is attributed to income effects on leisure demand from
subsistence consumption under Stone-Geary preferences, without recourse to
labor-market distortions.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The paper opens from two stylized features of the postwar Japanese economy:
growth was rapid but not fastest immediately after the war -- catch-up growth
in the 1950s gave way to still-faster growth in the 1960s and early 1970s --
and labor supply per adult declined more or less continuously through the
rapid-growth period, treated by the author as the harder feature to explain.
The objective is to quantitatively account for both features within a single
standard stochastic neoclassical general-equilibrium growth model, by
introducing capital destruction, TFP, and a labor wedge as exogenous shocks,
solving for equilibrium, and comparing resulting time paths for capital,
output, consumption, investment, and labor against Japanese data from 1952 to
2000.

The capital-destruction result is positioned against Christiano (1989) and King
and Rebelo (1993), who show that capital destruction alone implies an
unrealistically high marginal product of capital immediately postwar, causing
counterfactually rapid accumulation right after the war -- contrary to the
data, since capital growth did not in fact accelerate until the 1960s. Those
authors propose Stone-Geary subsistence-consumption preferences as a fix; Chen,
Imrohoroglu, and Imrohoroglu (2006) and Braun, Ikeda, and Joines (2006) are
cited as recent work showing a neoclassical model with exogenous TFP and
capital loss can match the postwar Japanese savings rate. The author argues
that once labor supply is made endogenous, subsistence consumption alone no
longer suffices to delay accumulation, and TFP is additionally needed.

On the labor-decline side, the paper is positioned against the labor-wedge
accounting of Chari, Kehoe, and McGrattan (2004); against Ohanian, Raffo, and
Rogerson (2006), who attribute much of the OECD labor wedge to labor income
taxation (Japanese labor-tax data is unavailable for the early 1960s); and
against Braun, Ikeda, and Joines (2006), who generate a labor wedge from
exogenously falling family size. The paper's own approach instead lets a
variant of subsistence-consumption preferences (Stone-Geary, extended here to
include leisure) generate the labor decline through an income effect, without
an exogenous wedge. Two further distinctions are claimed against this
contemporaneous literature (also including Braun, Okada and Sudou 2006): growth
paths are evaluated as levels' deviations from a balanced growth path rather
than as ratios such as savings rates, making each variable's transition toward
steady state visible; and the labor wedge is included explicitly alongside
endogenous labor supply, so the paper can test whether subsistence-consumption
preferences substitute for it.

### Methods and scope

**Empirical section (Section 2).** Before building the model, the paper
documents Japanese macro data for 1952-2000 (Hayashi and Prescott, 2002, for
1956-2000; Ohkawa and Rosovsky, 1973, earlier): a growth-accounting
decomposition by decade; GNP demand-side shares by decade; the long-run
(1905-2000) output path relative to a 2% trend, motivating a one-time postwar
upward shift in the balanced growth path (read, following Parente and Prescott
1994, as reduced barriers to technology adoption); detrended TFP and output
series (1989 = 0); and a labor wedge from the household's labor-leisure first-
order condition, following Chari, Kehoe, and McGrattan (2004), compared against
tax-based series (Mendoza, Razin and Tesar 1994, available from 1965 only) and
a "crude measure" built from Hayashi-Prescott labor-share data plus Japanese
Statistics Bureau data for missing years.

**Model (Section 3).** A discrete-time stochastic neoclassical growth model:

- *Household.* A single infinitely-lived representative household maximizes
  expected discounted utility over consumption c and labor supply l (fraction
  of a 16-hour day). Cobb-Douglas utility is c^Psi(1-l)^(1-Psi); Stone-Geary
  utility replaces c with (c - c-bar), where c-bar is a subsistence level
  calibrated at c-bar = 0.35c, so 1952 consumption sits only slightly above
  subsistence. Sigma is relative risk aversion; Psi the consumption weight. The
  budget constraint equates after-tax labor income plus capital income plus
  lump-sum transfer to consumption plus investment, subject to the standard
  capital accumulation identity with trend growth gamma and population growth
  n.
- *Firm and government.* A representative competitive firm uses constant-
  returns Cobb-Douglas technology, output = z * k^theta * l^(1-theta). The
  government collects labor income tax and rebates it in full via lump-sum
  transfer; the tax rate is unrestricted in sign, so a negative rate is an
  implicit labor subsidy. A government-expenditure shock, as in Chari, Kehoe
  and McGrattan (2004), was found to matter little for Japan and is omitted.
- *Shocks.* TFP (z) and the labor wedge (d) each follow AR(1) processes in
  logs, assumed mutually uncorrelated for tractability; the labor-wedge process
  is estimated separately for the Cobb-Douglas and Stone-Geary cases, since the
  two preferences imply different wedge series from the same labor data. A one-
  time, agent-anticipated upward shift in the balanced growth path is imposed
  after the war, so the AR(1) TFP process is defined relative to the new steady
  state from the start of the simulation -- flagged by the author as "a very
  strong assumption," since it requires agents to know the new steady state's
  location and convergence rate from 1952 onward.
- *Equilibrium and solution.* A standard competitive equilibrium is defined
  (optimization, market clearing, government budget balance, the resource
  constraint output = consumption + investment, shocks following the AR(1)
  processes). The model is solved by log-linearizing equilibrium conditions
  around a reference steady state set at 1989 values, using the method of Uhlig
  (1997). Capital stock is initialized at its actual 1952 level. Historical
  (linearly detrended) shock realizations, not random draws, are substituted
  into the linear decision rules to generate model time paths for 1952-2000,
  plotted against equivalently detrended data.

**Calibration (Section 4).** Most parameters are calibrated to average Japanese
data over 1984-1989. Capital share theta = 0.362 is taken directly from Hayashi
and Prescott (2002); trend growth gamma is set at 2% (the assumed U.S. long-run
trend rate, following King and Rebelo 1993 and others); depreciation delta,
discount factor beta, and consumption weight Psi are each backed out from
steady-state versions of the capital accumulation identity, the capital Euler
equation, and the labor first-order condition, using 1984-1989 average ratios.
Population growth and shock persistence/volatility parameters are estimated by
AR(1) regression using 1952-2000 data. Relative risk aversion sigma is set at 2
for the main results, described as robust for a wide range of sigma >= 1.

**Simulation experiments (Section 5).** Three nested experiments are run for
both preference specifications: (1) capital destruction as the only shock; (2)
capital destruction plus TFP; (3) capital destruction plus TFP plus labor
wedge.

**Appendices.** Appendix A develops a "variable utility weight" alternative in
which the Cobb-Douglas consumption weight declines with average consumption
(comparable to the exogenous family-size mechanism of Braun, Ikeda and Joines
2006). Appendix B compares the benchmark stochastic solution against
alternative TFP-expectation assumptions and a nonlinear perfect-foresight
deterministic solution over 1952-1999. Appendix C re-examines the 1980-2000
"bubble economy" and "lost decade" subperiod.

### Key findings

**Growth accounting by decade (Table 1 in source; % average annual growth
rates, Y = output per adult, K = capital per adult, L = labor input per adult,
A = TFP/Solow residual; capital share theta = 0.362).**

| Period | Output (Y) | Capital (K) | Labor (L) | TFP (A) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1952-59 | 7.0 | 5.1 | 1.9 | 3.8 |
| 1960-69 | 7.7 | 9.0 | -1.6 | 5.6 |
| 1970-79 | 3.2 | 8.2 | -1.0 | 1.1 |
| 1980-89 | 3.2 | 3.6 | -0.2 | 2.0 |
| 1990-99 | 0.7 | 3.4 | -1.2 | 0.3 |

Output growth peaked in the 1960s (7.7%), not the 1950s (7.0%), even though the
war-damage narrative would predict the opposite; capital-stock growth itself
did not accelerate until the 1960s (9.0%, up from 5.1% in the 1950s),
coinciding with the decade of highest output and TFP growth (5.6%). Labor input
per adult declined in every decade except the 1950s; the paper separately notes
the 1975 labor level was about 30% below the 1960 level. Between the 1970s and
1980s, output growth held constant at 3.2% while its composition shifted --
capital growth fell (8.2% to 3.6%) as TFP growth rose (1.1% to 2.0%) -- which
the paper reads as evidence against the "bubble economy" being simply an
investment-overheating episode, since TFP, not capital deepening, accelerated
in the 1980s relative to the 1970s. Growth of all four series fell to its
postwar low in the 1990s (output 0.7%, TFP 0.3%).

One internal inconsistency in the source is worth flagging rather than silently
resolving: the body text (Section 2.1) states the "growth rate of capital stock
was only 2.8%" during the 1950s, attributing most 1950s growth instead to
productivity and labor -- but Table 1's own 1952-59 row reports 5.1% capital
growth. Both figures appear in the document; this summary reports them as
given.

**GNP demand-side component shares (Table 2 in source; % of GNP; C = total
consumption, split into private Cp and government Cg; I = total investment,
split into domestic capital formation Id and current account CA).**

| Period | C | Cp | Cg | I | Id | CA |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1952-59 | 73.9 | 63.8 | 10.1 | 26.1 | 25.9 | 0.2 |
| 1960-69 | 65.1 | 57.3 | 7.9 | 34.9 | 34.7 | 0.2 |
| 1970-79 | 64.7 | 55.7 | 9.0 | 35.3 | 34.5 | 0.8 |
| 1980-89 | 68.3 | 58.7 | 9.6 | 31.7 | 29.5 | 2.2 |
| 1990-99 | 68.3 | 58.9 | 9.5 | 31.7 | 29.1 | 2.5 |

The investment share of GNP rose from 26.1% in the 1950s to a 1960s-1970s
plateau around 34.9-35.3%, mirrored by a fall in the consumption share from
73.9% to roughly 65%, before investment's share receded to 31.7% in the
1980s-90s as consumption's share partly recovered. The current account's GNP
share is small throughout but rises visibly from under 1% before 1980 to
2.2-2.5% in the 1980s-90s.

**Initial capital destruction.** The 1952 capital stock is calibrated at a log
deviation of -1.54 from the model's postwar steady state, i.e., about 21.4% of
the new steady-state level, or 78.6% below it. The paper is explicit that this
figure combines both actual wartime capital destruction and the modeled one-
time upward jump in the steady state itself (reduced postwar barriers to
technology adoption, per Parente and Prescott 1994), so it is not directly
comparable to more moderate war-loss figures in other studies -- cited as Chen,
Imrohoroglu and Imrohoroglu (2006) -- that measure capital loss only relative
to the prewar level.

**Simulation results.**

1. *Capital destruction only.* In both preference cases, the model fails to
   reproduce delayed catch-up: the low initial capital stock implies too high a
   marginal product of capital, producing counterfactually rapid capital
   accumulation immediately after the war rather than the observed acceleration
   only in the 1960s. The paper explicitly diverges from Christiano (1989) and
   King and Rebelo (1993) here: their claim that Stone-Geary subsistence
   preferences alone can delay capital accumulation does not hold once labor
   supply is endogenous. With elastic labor, the higher early consumption that
   subsistence preferences induce substitutes for leisure rather than
   investment -- raising output and loosening the resource constraint -- so
   investment does not fall as it would with inelastic labor.

2. *Capital destruction plus TFP.* Both preference specifications now reproduce
   the postwar paths of capital, output, consumption, and investment closely:
   low initial TFP offsets the high marginal product of labor implied by a low
   initial capital stock, delaying accumulation; as TFP rises, the return to
   capital rises and investment accelerates through the 1960s. This is
   described as consistent with the TFP-driven savings-rate results of Chen,
   Imrohoroglu and Imrohoroglu (2006) and Braun, Ikeda and Joines (2006). Only
   the Stone-Geary specification, however, additionally reproduces the labor
   decline of the 1960s and early 1970s; Cobb-Douglas cannot generate a labor
   decline from TFP growth alone. Mechanism: the Stone-Geary marginal rate of
   substitution between leisure and consumption rises faster with consumption
   growth than its Cobb-Douglas counterpart, creating an added income effect
   that raises the demand for leisure as consumption grows -- reproducing the
   labor decline without a labor wedge.

3. *Capital destruction plus TFP plus labor wedge.* Adding the labor wedge lets
   the model additionally track the quantitative fluctuation of labor itself,
   including the 1950s labor increase and 1990s labor drop that the TFP-only
   Stone-Geary case cannot explain (that case only accounts for the 1960s-70s
   decline). Labor wedges are found important for labor-driven fluctuations but
   not material to long-run capital accumulation or the overall growth path --
   consistent, the paper notes, with capital accumulation driving steady-state
   growth while labor fluctuation is more a business-cycle phenomenon.

**"Bubble economy" and "lost decade" detail (Appendix C, 1980-2000, Stone-Geary
case).** TFP shocks alone reproduce 1980s output, investment, and consumption
paths well but predict labor should rise during the 1980s, when data show it
falling; adding labor-wedge shocks brings simulated series into close
correspondence with the data across the 1980s-90s for nearly every variable,
with one persistent exception: no combination of shocks accounts for the spike
in investment in 1990-91. The paper links this gap to Ando, Christelis, and
Miyagawa's (2003) finding of corporate overinvestment from abnormally low
dividend payouts, and to Hayashi's (2004) argument that low dividends caused
overinvestment -- noting that channel has no direct TFP implication and so does
not, alone, explain the subsequent Lost Decade stagnation. The 1990 consumption
drop is captured (and, with both TFP and labor-wedge shocks, somewhat over-
predicted) by the model; the paper notes this coincides with the April 1989
introduction of a 3% consumption tax under Prime Minister Takeshita (later
raised to 5% in 1997 under Prime Minister Hashimoto), which enters the model
implicitly through the labor wedge, since a consumption tax appears in the same
labor-leisure first-order condition as a labor income tax.

**Robustness (Appendix A and B).** A "variable utility weight" alternative, in
which the Cobb-Douglas consumption weight declines with consumption rather than
featuring an explicit subsistence floor, produces virtually the same results as
the Stone-Geary specification; a reduced-form regression of the log utility
weight on log detrended consumption gives an elasticity of -0.31 with a
t-statistic of -21.93, i.e., a household values consumption relatively more
when poorer. Alternative TFP-expectation assumptions -- a "pessimistic" case
(rho_z = 1, no anticipated TFP growth) versus an "optimistic" case (rho_z =
0.9), against the calibrated benchmark of rho_z = 0.94 -- produce only modest
differences in the smoothness of simulated investment and capital paths; the
realized TFP path is said to matter far more for matching the data than what
agents were assumed to expect. A fully nonlinear, perfect-foresight
deterministic solution over 1952-1999 produces virtually the same time paths as
the benchmark log-linearized stochastic solution, offered as evidence the
reported results are not an artifact of either the linearization method or the
size of the initial (-1.54 log-point) deviation from steady state.

**Stated overall attribution.** The paper's own conclusion is that capital
destruction (including the assumed steady-state shift) together with the
observed, exogenously-taken TFP path accounts for the postwar time paths of
capital, output, consumption, and investment across 1952-2000, while the
decline in labor during the rapid-growth period is attributed to income effects
on leisure demand from subsistence consumption under Stone-Geary preferences,
not to labor-market wedges. TFP itself is not explained within the model -- it
is treated as an adoption process for existing foreign technology (following
Parente and Prescott 1994 and Eaton and Kortum 1997) rather than domestic
innovation -- and the conclusion poses as an open question, in the author's own
words, "why did it take so long to adopt technology?", suggesting a learning-
by-doing, human-capital-based extension as a candidate answer for future work.

### Limitations

- Stated open question: the paper does not explain why postwar technology
  adoption, and hence TFP catch-up, was gradual rather than immediate; a
  learning-by-doing extension is suggested but not built. Relatedly, TFP is
  exogenous throughout, a Solow residual fed in as data rather than explained.
- The one-time, fully-anticipated postwar shift in the balanced growth path is
  flagged by the author as "a very strong assumption," requiring agents in 1952
  to correctly know both the new steady state's location and its convergence
  rate.
- Shocks are assumed mutually uncorrelated for tractability; historical, not
  randomly drawn, realizations are used in simulation, so the author states
  point results are unaffected, but correlated shocks would have made the
  persistence-parameter estimation less efficient than it is under OLS.
- Japanese labor-income-tax data needed to benchmark the labor wedge is
  unavailable for the early 1960s; the proxy series used diverges from the
  model-implied wedge, read as evidence labor income tax is not the wedge's
  only source. The wedge's 1950s decline specifically is left to future work.
- Capital share theta = 0.362 is taken directly from Hayashi and Prescott
  (2002) rather than independently estimated, and most structural parameters
  are calibrated to a single benchmark window (1984-1989) whose sensitivity is
  not separately examined.
- An internal inconsistency exists between the narrative text's claim of 2.8%
  capital-stock growth in the 1950s and Table 1's reported 5.1% figure for the
  same decade; this summary reports the discrepancy rather than resolving it.
- As a January 2007 discussion paper circulated, per the series' own
  disclaimer, to stimulate discussion and comments, the version reviewed here
  shows no internal evidence of completed peer review; a peer-reviewed version
  was later published in The B.E. Journal of Macroeconomics, 9(1), 2009.

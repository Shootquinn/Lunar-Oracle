# Understanding Patterns of Economic Growth: Searching for Hills among Plateaus,
Mountains, and Plains

## Citation

Pritchett, L. (2000). Understanding patterns of economic growth: Searching for hills among
plateaus, mountains, and plains. *The World Bank Economic Review*, 14(2), 221-250.
https://doi.org/10.1093/wber/14.2.221

## Abstract

Cross-country empirical study arguing that U.S. (and other OECD) GDP per capita is well described by
a single stable exponential trend, but that almost nothing true of industrial-country growth holds
for developing countries, whose growth rates shift sharply and repeatedly over time. Using Penn World
Tables Mark 5.6 data for 111 countries since 1960, the paper builds descriptive statistics on levels,
instability, and volatility of growth, sorts countries into a six-category topographic taxonomy (steep
hills, hills, plateaus, mountains, plains, accelerators) based on growth before and after each
country's best-fitting structural break, and reports low correlation between pre-break and post-break
growth rates. It then argues that this instability undermines higher-frequency panel-data growth
regressions and recommends episode-based research instead.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The paper opens by contrasting the United States, whose GDP per capita history (apart from the Great
Depression) is "well characterized by reasonably stable exponential trend growth with modest cyclical
deviations," with developing countries, for which "a single time trend does not adequately
characterize the evolution of GDP per capita" (page 221). It frames the motivating puzzle with two
paired examples: Brazil grew 4.2 percent a year from 1965 to 1980 then stagnated from 1980 to 1992,
so that treating 1960-92 as a single 3.14 percent average obscures the underlying break; and Cote
d'Ivoire grew 3.1 percent from 1960 to 1980 (an "African growth miracle") before GDP per capita fell
4.1 percent a year from 1980 to 1992 (a "growth disaster"), averaging to 0.22 percent overall, while
neighboring Senegal stagnated steadily around 0.18 percent the whole period (pages 221-222). The paper
asks in what sense such divergent trajectories, once averaged, describe "the same" growth experience.

The stated objective has two linked halves: first, to provide descriptive statistics characterizing
the evolution of GDP per capita for a broad cross-section of countries, emphasizing instability in
growth rates and volatility of output; second, to draw out the implications of these facts for
econometric growth research, arguing that the use of high-frequency panel data with fixed effects to
study long-run growth correlates is "almost certainly pointless," and that research into what starts
or stops growth episodes has more promise (page 222).

### Methods and scope

The output variable is the chain-linked index of real GDP per capita in 1985 purchasing power parity
dollars (P$) from the Penn World Tables Mark 5.6, beginning in 1960, for the 111 countries with at
least 25 years of data; since final data years vary from 1985 to 1992 across countries, the author
refers to the endpoint as the "most recent" year (page 222). Countries are split into industrial and
developing groups primarily by OECD membership prior to recent expansion, with Malta and Cyprus added
to the industrial group and Turkey excluded despite OECD membership; the author notes this definition
does not track initial income (in 1960 Venezuela, Iraq, and Mexico had higher GDP per capita than
France, Japan, and Greece respectively) but is judged to better capture the character of industrial
economies (pages 222-223).

Three families of statistics are calculated for each country (Table 1, page 223): (1) basic level and
average-growth statistics, including an ordinary least squares trend growth rate over 1960-most
recent, and over the subperiods 1960-73, 1973-82, and 1982-most recent, plus initial and final income
and the ratio of final to maximum/minimum income; (2) instability statistics, built around a
"best-break" procedure that fits growth as two connected linear segments, gt = a1 + b1*t*I(t <= t*) +
a2*I(t > t*) + b2*t*I(t > t*) + e, choosing the single breakpoint t* to minimize the sum of squared
errors across all candidate years subject to a margin so t* is not within a fixed number of years of
either endpoint; the reported outputs are the break year, the pre-break and post-break growth rates,
the percentage-point shift between them, and the R-squared of the single-trend regression; and (3)
volatility statistics on deviations from trend, including the standard deviation of trend residuals,
the coefficient of variation, mean, and standard deviation of first log differences, the median
absolute value of second log differences, and a three-year-ahead forecast error computed by fitting a
trend on the prior ten years and comparing the prediction to the realized value (Table 1, page 223).

The break-detection method is explicitly a description of each country's own time series, not a test
of statistical significance: the author reports before-and-after growth rates for every country
regardless of whether the shift is statistically significant, contrasting this with Ben-David and
Papell (1997), who summarize only breaks that test as significant; Pritchett argues this conflates the
magnitude of a shift with the power of the test for it, since more volatile series make a given-size
shift harder to detect (page 226, footnote 8). Building on these break statistics, individual country
graphs and growth levels before/after the break are used to sort all 111 countries into six named
growth-pattern categories (Table 5, pages 230-231), described further below. Later sections (Table 7,
page 238; Table 8, page 239) classify growth-regression right-hand-side variables by persistence,
exogeneity, and "model rationale," and use a Monte Carlo simulation of a bivariate panel regression
(100 countries, six periods) to illustrate how much statistical power is lost when fixed effects sweep
out cross-country variance in a variable with a given degree of persistence.

### Key findings

**Baseline growth rates, industrial versus developing (Table 2, page 224).** Median growth rates over
1960-most recent were 2.86 percent for industrial countries versus 1.51 percent for developing
countries, with means of 2.90 and 1.64 percent respectively. By subperiod, median growth was 3.97 (industrial)
versus 2.72 (developing) percent in 1960-73; 1.79 versus 1.99 percent in 1973-82; and 2.10 versus
negative 0.13 percent in 1982-most recent, so that the growth gap between the two groups widened
sharply after 1982 (page 225). The correlation between initial 1960 income and subsequent growth is
reported as 0.22 (page 225), and the ratio of median final incomes to median initial incomes rose from
about 5 to 1 to more than 7 to 1 (P$13,118 versus P$1,869) (page 225).

**Cross-country dispersion (Table 2 and Table 3, pages 224-227).** The standard deviation of growth
rates is about 1 percent for industrial countries and nearly twice as large, about 2 percent, for
developing countries (page 225). Comparing extremes (Table 3, page 227): among industrial countries
the fifth-fastest grower (Greece, 3.61 percent) and fifth-slowest (New Zealand, 1.19 percent, following
the list Australia 1.99, Sweden 1.88, United States 1.81, Switzerland 1.49) differ by about 1.6 to
2.4 percentage points, a narrow range; among developing countries the fifth-fastest (Botswana, 6.03
percent, behind Singapore 6.95, Korea 6.85, Taiwan 6.29, Hong Kong 6.15) and fifth-slowest (Chad, minus
2.75 percent, behind Somalia minus 1.36, Angola minus 1.97, Madagascar minus 2.12, Mozambique minus
2.25) differ by more than 7 percentage points, over four times the industrial-country spread. The
paper notes South Korea moved from having less per capita income than Angola to ten times more within
30 years (page 225).

**Growth instability at the country level (Table 4, page 227).** Using the best-single-break
statistics, the developing-country mean break year is 1977 (median 1978), with a mean percentage-point
shift of negative 2.58 (median negative 2.21): mean growth before the break is 2.62 percent, falling to
a mean of 0.05 percent after (median: 2.86 percent before, negative 0.04 percent after). For industrial
countries the mean break year is 1975 (median 1974), with a smaller mean shift of negative 1.91
(median negative 1.93): growth falls from a mean of 4.07 percent before the break to 2.17 percent after
(median 3.83 to 1.84 percent). Reported separately in the running text, the average absolute value of
the within-country shift in growth rates for developing countries is 3.4 percentage points, described
as "much larger than either the cross-sectional variance of 2.0 percent or the median growth rate of
1.5 percent" (page 226); growth in 55 of the 111 countries decelerated or accelerated by more than 3
percentage points across the two periods (page 226).

**Explanatory power of a single trend (Table 4 and Figure 3, pages 227-229).** The R-squared of fitting
one linear trend to the whole 1960-most-recent series has a median of 0.95 (standard deviation 0.03)
for industrial countries, meaning "for nearly every industrial country the total variance of the time
series is almost completely summarized in a single number: the average growth rate" (page 228). For
developing countries the median trend R-squared is only 0.67 (standard deviation 0.32), spread across
the full zero-to-one range; in 40 percent of developing countries the trend R-squared is below 0.5,
which the author interprets as meaning "shifts and fluctuations are the dominant features of the
time-series evolution of GDP per capita" for those countries (page 228).

**The six-category growth taxonomy (Table 5, pages 229-231; Figure 4, pages 232-233).** Based on
growth before (g_b) and after (g_a) each country's structural break, the paper defines: Steep hills (11
countries): g_b > 3 percent and g_a > 3 percent (Cyprus, Ireland, Malta, Japan, Malaysia, Singapore,
Taiwan, Thailand, Korea, Hong Kong, Botswana); "in these countries the trend is everything" (page 229).
Hills (27 countries): g_b > 1.5 percent and g_a > 1.5 percent (most OECD countries including the United
States, plus Costa Rica and Pakistan); the exact cutoff is 1.48 percent, rounded up in the text only,
and the author states the cutoffs are "somewhat arbitrary and are rigged according to the outcomes that
corresponded to my intuitive feel, particularly to retain the United States as a hill not a plateau"
(page 229, footnote 9). Plateaus (16 countries): g_b > 1.5 percent, then 0 < g_a < 1.5 percent (classic
case Brazil, 4 percent before 1980 falling to 0.66 percent after; also Sweden, Iceland, Netherlands,
New Zealand, Morocco, and several developing countries). Mountains (33 countries): g_b > 1.5 percent,
g_a < 0 percent; includes most oil exporters (Algeria, Gabon, Nigeria, Saudi Arabia), commodity
exporters hit by price reversals (Cote d'Ivoire, Guyana, Jamaica, Zambia), and Latin American countries
hit by the debt crisis (Argentina, Bolivia, Paraguay); a sub-set are described as cliffs, "very sharp
drops, usually resulting from war or civil unrest" (Liberia, Mozambique, Nicaragua), and mountain
countries correspondingly show low trend R-squared values (Cote d'Ivoire 0.013, Argentina 0.204,
Nicaragua 0.190) (pages 229-232). Plains (17 countries): g_b < 1.5 percent and g_a < 1.5 percent
throughout; 14 of the 17 are in Sub-Saharan Africa; Senegal is called "a classic plain," with
continuous near-zero growth (0.18 percent) yet still a low trend R-squared (0.213); countries with
consistently negative growth within this group, such as Mozambique, are described as valleys (page
233). Accelerators, nicknamed "Denver" after the point where the Great Plains meet the Rocky Mountains
(page 234, footnote 10): 7 countries with g_b < 1.5 percent and g_a > 1.5 percent, including Indonesia
after 1966, Mauritius after 1970, India, and Ghana (accelerating from low or negative rates to
"respectable, but unimpressive" rates) (page 234).

**Regional distribution and caveats on the taxonomy (page 234).** OECD countries are almost entirely
hills or steep hills (18 of 23). Sub-Saharan Africa supplies most of the plains countries (14 of 17)
but not all Sub-Saharan African countries are plains (only 14 of 36); a similar number (13 of 36) are
mountains. Latin America has very few plains (2 of 24) but many mountains (12 of 24); in the Middle
East and North Africa, 7 of 11 countries are mountains. The author flags anomalies from allowing only
one structural break per country: China is classified a hill because the single allowed break smooths
over the Great Leap Forward; Tanzania's break is placed at 1980, smoothing over a 1978-1984 crisis
period; and Great Britain is classified a mountain because its otherwise smooth series is broken at a
1987 business-cycle peak (page 234, footnote 11).

**Persistence of growth across periods (page 234).** The paper reports that the cross-national rank
correlation of countries' growth rates before and after their structural break is only 0.24, citing
Easterly, Kremer, Pritchett, and Summers (1993) as already devoted to this point. The text states this
low correlation contradicts the impression, from "casual discussions of high-performing and
low-performing countries," that time-persistent country characteristics explain most cross-country
variation in growth (page 234). No transition-probability matrix or Markov-style state-transition
table between the six taxonomy categories is presented in the paper; the 0.24 rank correlation between
pre-break and post-break growth is the paper's only reported cross-period persistence statistic.

**Volatility around trend (Table 6, page 236; Figure 5, page 237).** However measured, output
volatility is higher for developing countries. The median standard deviation of deviations from a
single trend is 0.100 for developing countries versus 0.054 for industrial countries, about twice as
large; the median three-year-ahead forecast error is 0.095 versus 0.054, and the median maximum
forecast error is 0.283 versus 0.135, again roughly double. The coefficient of variation of first log
differences of GDP per capita has a median of 4.314 for developing countries versus 1.040 for
industrial countries, about four times as high (page 236). The author cautions that these measures are
highly correlated with each other but not so highly correlated as to be interchangeable, since a
country with high volatility around one stable trend and a country with two stable subperiod trends
around a shifted mean can appear similarly volatile by some measures (page 235-236).

**Implications for growth regressions (pages 237-244).** The second half of the paper argues that
because within-country variance dominates cross-country variance for GDP per capita growth (0.73 of
total variance in a five-year panel is within-country, Table 8, page 239, versus only 0.22 for
investment rates, 0.07 for education levels, and 0.02 for log population size), using higher-frequency
panel data with fixed effects to study growth determinants faces four compounding problems: lower
statistical power (a Monte Carlo exercise finds fixed-effects t-statistics could fall to about a third
of cross-sectional t-statistics for a variable as persistent as education, page 239); worsened
attenuation bias from measurement error, potentially by a factor of ten (page 240); endogeneity bias,
illustrated with a stylized model in which fixed effects estimate the reverse-causal effect of growth
on a policy variable rather than the variable's effect on growth (pages 240-241); and dynamic
misspecification, since arbitrarily short time periods impose the same adjustment-speed assumption on
growth correlates that in fact operate over very different time horizons, from contemporaneous
business-cycle effects to multi-decade effects on technical progress (pages 241-244). The author
concludes that empirical findings that differ between time-averaged cross-sections and higher-frequency
panels (not robust, smaller in magnitude, larger or sign-reversed, or unstable over time) are, in every
case, uninformative about the underlying long-run structural relationship (pages 243-244), citing his
own earlier "requiem for growth regressions" (Pritchett 1997).

**Proposed alternative research strategy (pages 245-247).** The paper recommends three lines of
research instead of panel growth regressions: (1) analysis of the conditions that initiate growth
accelerations or decelerations, since plateau, mountain, and cliff countries typically show an
identifiable turning point, citing Korea in the early 1960s, Mauritius in 1970, Indonesia after 1966,
China since 1978, and Chile since 1988 as cases worth studying this way; (2) analysis of discrete
policy-change episodes, citing Bruno and Easterly (1998) on inflation and Krueger (1978) on exchange
rate and trade liberalization episodes as more informative than growth regressions using openness as a
right-hand variable; and (3) cross-sectional analysis of changes in growth rates over time, which the
author calls "almost unexplored," citing Rodrik's then-forthcoming paper "Where Did All the Growth
Go?" (Rodrik 2000, cited as forthcoming in the Journal of Economic Growth) as showing that growth
deceleration is explained by a combination of external shocks and countries' social/political capacity
to adjust to them (pages 246-247).

**Conclusion (page 247).** The paper's closing statement is that "the rule of growth in developing
countries is that anything can happen and often does," and that instability makes "talk of the growth
rate almost meaningless" for most developing countries; the East Asian countries that sustained rapid
growth for three decades "at least until 1997" are explicitly framed as the exception, not the rule,
that should not be mistaken for the norm (page 247).

### Limitations

The author states several limitations directly. The growth-rate cutoffs used to define hills, steep
hills, and plateaus (1.5 percent, 3 percent) are described as "somewhat arbitrary" and calibrated partly
to match the author's own intuition about which countries should fall in which category, including
deliberately keeping the United States classified as a hill rather than a plateau (page 229, footnote
9). Allowing only one structural break per country produces classification anomalies acknowledged in
the text: China's Great Leap Forward, Tanzania's 1978-1984 crisis, and Great Britain's 1987
business-cycle peak are all smoothed over or misclassified by the single-break method (page 234,
footnote 11). The break-detection method reports before/after growth rates for every country regardless
of statistical significance, which the author defends but which differs from other published
approaches, such as Ben-David and Papell (1997), that report only statistically significant breaks
(page 226, footnote 8). The various volatility measures are described as correlated with each other but
not so highly correlated that they capture identical aspects of volatility (pages 235-236). The paper
also notes, without resolving, that similar results on volatility and trend shifts might obtain under a
difference-stationary treatment of the series, which was not carried out (page 235, footnote 12). No
transition-probability or Markov-matrix analysis between growth states is presented; the paper's
persistence evidence is limited to the single 0.24 rank correlation between pre-break and post-break
growth. Finally, the paper's own proposed remedies (episode analysis, discrete policy-change studies,
cross-sectional analysis of growth-rate changes) are presented as promising directions rather than as
completed empirical programs; the author states plainly, regarding newer dynamic-panel econometric
techniques proposed by others as a fix, "I have become more, rather than less, doubtful by the results
produced" (page 245).

### Topic mapping

FA8. Source list entry 2. Sub-Q 2. This paper supplies the growth-instability and low-persistence base
rate for the red team's disconfirmation case against any twenty-year straight-line extrapolation of a
high sustained growth rate: developing-country growth is not well described by a single trend (median
trend R-squared 0.67 versus 0.95 for industrial countries, with 40 percent of developing countries
below 0.5, page 228), the correlation of growth rates before and after a country's own structural break
is only 0.24 (page 234), and average within-country growth-rate shifts run about 3.4 percentage points
in absolute value against a median developing-country growth rate of only about 1.5 percent (page 226).
The paper's six-category taxonomy (steep hills, hills, plateaus, mountains including cliffs, plains
including valleys, and accelerators) and its regional tallies (pages 229-234) give a concrete accounting
of how many of the 111 sample countries sustained rapid growth (11 steep hills, 27 hills) against how
many decelerated, collapsed, or stagnated (16 plateaus, 33 mountains, 17 plains). This complements the
growth-accelerations literature associated with Hausmann, Pritchett, and Rodrik (noted here only as a
topical link, since that specific joint paper is not the source under review); Pritchett's own citation
of Rodrik's then-forthcoming "Where Did All the Growth Go?" (page 246) addresses the mirror-image
question of growth deceleration using data built in this paper.

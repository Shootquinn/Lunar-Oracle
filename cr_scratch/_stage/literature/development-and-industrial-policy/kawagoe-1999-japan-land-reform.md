# Agricultural Land Reform in Postwar Japan: Experiences and Issues

## Abstract

Historical-institutional working paper on the 1946-1950 SCAP-directed agricultural land reform
in Japan. Traces the prewar landlord-tenant system from the 1873 Land Tax Revision through
wartime regulation, documents the legal design and execution of the postwar reform, and
tabulates its distributional outcomes (tenancy share, farm-ownership categories, land transfer
volumes, compensation prices). Applies a de Janvry-derived typology to classify the Japanese
case as a "peasantry-to-peasantry" (Asian Model) reform that redistributed ownership without
altering farm-size structure. Concludes the reform was a political success but leaves its
contribution to agricultural output growth an open, largely unresolved empirical question,
citing prior studies that found no clear productivity effect.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The paper opens from the observation that Japan's postwar land reform is widely cited as one of
history's most successful agrarian reforms -- General MacArthur, in an October 1949 letter to
Prime Minister Yoshida, called it "the most successful land reform program in history" -- yet
the author argues most prior assessments conflate its political success with unproven claims
about its economic (productivity) effects. The stated objective is to separate political and
economic dimensions of the reform: to explain the political and economic motivations behind it,
the preconditions that allowed such a drastic reform to be carried out, and the economic issues
it left unresolved, while tracing its implementation chronologically.

The paper situates the reform against Japan's longer land-tenure history. Under the Tokugawa
shogunate, daimyo finances rested on rice taxes (kokudaka); landlord-tenant relations were
nominally prohibited but emerged de facto as intensive, fertilizer-based small-farm cultivation
displaced large attached-labor farms. After the Meiji Restoration (1868), the 1873 Land Tax
Revision -- a nationwide survey taking nine years and costing roughly a full year's government
revenue -- converted the in-kind rice tax to a cash land tax based on assessed land value,
formalizing landlord ownership titles while leaving tenant rights unrecognized. The land tax
reached 85% of total government tax revenue in 1875, still 35% in 1900. Tenanted land rose from
about 30% of arable land just after 1868 to roughly 45% by 1900-1910 and near 50% by the 1930s.
Farm-price collapses and tenancy disputes through the 1920s-1930s (documented year by year)
prompted earlier, more modest reform attempts before the war.

The paper also documents the political framing SCAP itself used. When ordering reform in a 9
December 1945 memorandum, SCAP listed five "pernicious ills" of Japanese agriculture: intense
overcrowding of land; tenancy conditions unfavorable to tenants; heavy farm indebtedness at
high interest; government fiscal policy discriminating against agriculture in favor of industry
and trade; and authoritative government control over farmers without regard for their
interests. A separate memorandum by MacArthur's political adviser Atcheson (26 October 1945)
argued overcrowding of land was the single most important cause of Japanese agriculture's
unsatisfactory condition and that the only real solution was absorbing millions of farm workers
into industrial occupations -- yet the paper notes the reform ultimately executed did not
include measures to address farm size or overcrowding at all, focusing solely on ownership
redistribution. The paper also lists six preconditions it identifies as jointly necessary for
the reform's completeness: pre-existing land-ownership records from the Land Tax Revision;
well-surveyed tenure conditions and tight village social structure enabling identification of
tenancy relations; a reformist group already active in government with trained Tenancy
Officers; a large pool of educated personnel available due to wartime devastation limiting
other job opportunities; landlords' political and economic power already weakened by wartime
regulation; and a postwar political climate favorable to reform following military defeat and
occupation.

### Methods and scope

The paper is a documentary-historical analysis, not an econometric study. Primary sources
include Japanese government and SCAP archival collections (cited throughout as NKSS --
collected GHQ/SCAP land-reform documents -- and NKTG, a 1951 government summary of the reform),
Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry survey data (1935, 1938, 1947, 1952), and secondary
literature, principally Dore's Land Reform in Japan (1959) and Hayami et al.'s The Agricultural
Development of Japan (1991), alongside the author's own prior work (Kawagoe 1993a, 1993b,
1995).

Analytically, the paper adopts a typology of land reform types built on a 4-by-4 matrix (Table
2-1a, simplified to Table 2-1b) that classifies reforms by pre- and post-reform "mode of
production" -- market/peasantry, market/commercial, semi-feudal, and socialist -- yielding
named categories: the "Asian Model" (peasantry-to-peasantry or peasantry-to-commercial
redistribution among landlords and tenants), the "Latin American Model" (semi-feudal estates
converted to capitalist, family-farm, or peasant forms), and the "Transitional Economy Model"
(decollectivization). Japan's reform is classified as an Asian Model, peasantry-to-peasantry
(Mp to Mp) case.

Temporal scope runs from the Tokugawa period through the reform's legal consolidation in the
1952 Agricultural Land Law, with outcome data tabulated as late as 1955-1960. The paper does
not test the productivity question it raises; it flags three empirical questions (effect on
production mode, effect on agricultural growth, effect on aggregate demand via income
redistribution) as beyond its scope and offers only summary comments referencing other
empirical studies.

### Key findings

- Prewar agricultural growth trajectory: primary industry accounted for about 70% of the labor
  force and roughly 40% of Net Domestic Product in 1880. About 15 million workers cultivated
  4.7 million hectares that year, a man-land ratio of 3.1 workers/hectare, declining to 2.0 by
  1960 as the labor force contracted. Despite land constraints, agricultural output grew at an
  annual compound rate of about 1.6% in the late nineteenth century, accelerating to about 2.0%
  in the early twentieth century through the 1920s. Rice accounted for 55% of gross farm output
  in 1880, declining to 40% by 1940; annual per-capita rice consumption rose from about 100 kg
  in the 1870s to a peak of about 170 kg by the mid-1910s, and the share of consumption met by
  imports (chiefly from Korea and Taiwan) rose from roughly 1% in the late nineteenth century
  to about 10% in the 1930s.

- Landlord categorization: the paper distinguishes farming landlords (farmers who lease out
  part of their land while cultivating the rest) from non-farming landlords, further split into
  village landlords and absentee landlords. A 1947 survey found about 1.3 million farm
  households, one-fifth of the total, recorded as farming landlords, renting out about 1.1
  million of 4.1 million hectares (28%) of farmer-owned land, averaging 0.84 hectares rented
  out per household. Non-farming landlords numbered an estimated 1 million households in 1938,
  of which only about 13% held more than 5 hectares and nearly half held under 0.5 hectares. A
  June 1941 survey counted 20,940 absentee landlords owning more than 5 hectares (about 181,000
  hectares total), representing only 2% of non-farming landlords -- indicating the great
  majority of Japanese "landlords" were smallholders rather than large estate owners in the
  literal sense of the term.

- Prewar tenure structure: by the 1930s roughly 5.5 million farm households cultivated a
  roughly constant ~5-6 million hectares; average holding was about 1 hectare, fragmented into
  parcels averaging 0.06 hectares. Farm-tenure shares in 1938 were approximately 30% owner, 44%
  owner-cum-tenant, 26% tenant (Table 4-11). Tenant farmers typically paid close to half their
  harvest as rent in kind -- e.g., roughly 1.7 tons of rice paid as rent per 3.5 tons produced
  per hectare in the mid-1930s.

- Landlord size distribution: a 1935 survey found half of Japan's 5 million landowners held
  under 0.5 hectares each; large landlords (over 50 hectares) numbered only about 2,500
  households nationally (excluding Hokkaido) in 1938, of which about 70% held under 100
  hectares and only 15 held over 1,000 hectares. The prewar Gini coefficient of income
  distribution across local towns/villages is cited at roughly 0.45-0.55 (1937 estimate),
  versus about 0.35 nationally in the postwar period.

- Tenancy disputes and interwar reform attempts: recorded tenancy disputes rose from 85 in 1917
  to a peak of 6,824 in 1935 (Table 5-1), driven in the 1920s by rent-reduction claims amid
  falling farm prices and rising non-farm wages, and in the 1930s by landlord attempts to
  reclaim land during the Depression (farm prices fell over 20% in the late 1920s and a further
  ~30% in 1930-31; tenant farm household income fell from 1,413 yen in 1925 to 994 yen in 1929
  to 442 yen in 1931). Government responses included the 1924 Land Tenancy Conciliation Law and
  the 1926 Owner Farmers Establishment Rule, which offered tenants land-purchase credit at 4.8%
  interest (1.3 points government-subsidized) repayable over 25 years; this scheme transferred
  114,000 hectares between 1926 and 1937 against a 112,000-hectare target.

- Wartime regulation: the 1938 Farmland Adjustment Law allowed local authorities to promote
  owner-farmer transfers; the 1939 Land Rent Control Order froze rents at 1939 levels (reducing
  rents on about 330,000 hectares by 1943); the 1941 Land Price Control Order fixed farmland
  prices (about 6,329 yen/ha for paddy, 3,763 yen/ha for upland); and the 1942 Food Control Law
  nationalized rice marketing and pricing. Rice producer subsidies rose from 5 yen/koku in 1941
  to 245 yen/koku by 1946, lifting total producer price from 44 to 300 yen/koku, while the
  price landlords received for their in-kind rent stayed nearly flat. Between 1940 and 1946,
  the rural consumer price index rose about 1,216% and the producer rice price about 689%, but
  the landlord-received price rose only about 70% -- eroding landlord real income well before
  the postwar reform began.

- Postwar reform design: a first Land Reform Bill (proposed to the Diet 4 December 1945) was
  judged inadequate by SCAP -- it would have compulsorily transferred only 900,000 hectares
  owned by about 100,000 landlords. A revised "second" reform, enacted 11 October 1946 as
  amendments to the Farmland Adjustment Law plus the Owner Farmer Establishment Special
  Measures Law, mandated compulsory purchase of all absentee-landlord land, tenanted land held
  by resident landlords in excess of 1 hectare (4 hectares in Hokkaido), and inefficiently
  operated owner-cultivated land above 3 hectares (12 hectares in Hokkaido). Purchase prices
  were fixed at 1938 rental values multiplied by factors of 40 (paddy) and 48 (upland),
  yielding roughly 760 yen/tan for paddy and 450 yen/tan for upland; landlords were paid in
  30-year government bonds at 3.6% interest, while purchasing cultivators could pay in cash or
  installments over 30 years at 3.2%. Postwar inflation sharply eroded the real burden on new
  owners and the real compensation to former landlords: the price of one tan of good paddy
  land, equivalent to over 3,000 packs of cigarettes in 1939, was worth only about 13 packs by
  1948.

- Execution: three-tier elected Land Committees (Village, Prefecture, Central) carried out the
  transfer, with village committees split 5 tenant/3 landlord/2 owner-farmer seats. Over
  400,000 people were engaged in the program; by early 1947, 415,000 were mobilized, including
  32,000 committee secretaries, 116,000 committee members, and about 260,000 unpaid assistant
  staff. Program costs exceeded 1 billion yen over 1946-1948 (about 60% operational). Ten
  rounds of compulsory purchase transferred 1,630,000 hectares to the government by the end of
  1948, immediately resold to cultivators.

- Distributional outcomes: tenanted land share fell from 46% of cultivated area in 1941 to 13%
  in 1949 to 9% in 1955 (Table 6-1). Owner-cultivator farms rose from 31% of households in 1941
  to 70% in 1955, while pure tenant farms fell from 28% to 4% (Table 6-2). An estimated 80-90%
  of absentee-landlord land (about 560,000 hectares) and 70-80% (over 1 million hectares) of
  village-landlord leased-out land was transferred. The 1952 Agricultural Land Law then locked
  in the new structure: a 3-hectare ownership ceiling (12 in Hokkaido), a rule limiting land
  purchases to cultivators of at least 0.3 hectares, a ban on corporate farmland ownership, and
  continued rent controls.

- Author's assessment of economic effects: average operational farm size did not rise, and in
  fact declined slightly, from 1.09 hectares (1941) to 0.99 hectares (1955) -- consistent with
  the reform's classification as a peasantry-to-peasantry (Mp to Mp) change with no shift in
  production mode. The author cites empirical work (Kaneda 1980; Kawagoe 1995) finding no clear
  reform-driven productivity effect, against the more commonly asserted view that new ownership
  incentives drove postwar agricultural growth. The paper's own tentative alternative
  explanation is that agricultural growth after the war reflected technological potential
  accumulated since the 1930s (seed and fertilizer technology) that had been suppressed by
  wartime input shortages and was released once fertilizer and other input supplies recovered
  -- a technology/input-recovery account rather than an incentive-based one. The paper also
  references, without resolving, a possible reform effect on rural living standards and capital
  formation via redistributed income (Kawano 1969) and on aggregate demand in the wider
  economy.

- Structural change in farm output over the century: total farm output value rose from 792
  million yen in 1880 to 2,781 million yen in 1960 (1904-06 prices). Within that total, rice's
  share fell from 56% (1880) to 40% (1960); livestock's share rose from 1% to 20% over the same
  span, while sericulture products rose from 6% (1880) to a peak of 17% (1940) before falling
  back to 4% (1960) with the postwar collapse of the silk export trade. The number of farm
  households remained essentially static, at about 5.5-6.0 million, across the entire 1880-1960
  period (Table 4-1).

- Framing of the reform as industrial policy: the paper's own summary framing states the reform
  "succeeded politically but, as an industrial policy, brought serious economic problems." It
  links this partly to the Agricultural Land Law of 1952 freezing the post-reform ownership and
  farm-size structure in place, which the author elsewhere (citing Kawagoe 1993b) identifies as
  a later impediment to structural adjustment in Japanese agriculture, since legal ceilings on
  landholding and the restriction of land purchase to small-scale cultivators constrained farm
  consolidation even as non-farm wages and mechanization increased the potential returns to
  scale in subsequent decades.

### Limitations

The paper is explicitly descriptive and historical rather than causal or econometric; it states
its own core productivity and demand-side questions are "beyond the scope of this paper" and
offers only preliminary comment for future research. In its concluding section the author poses
three specific unresolved questions -- the reform's impact on the mode of agricultural
production, its contribution to postwar agricultural growth (specifically through
labor-productivity channels such as tenant incentives versus increased investment), and its
effect on aggregate demand via redistributed rural income and consumption -- and states only
brief comments are offered on each pending further study. Several of its own figures are
flagged by the author as approximations: national counts of non-farming landlords are described
as likely overestimated because landowners with parcels scattered across villages could be
double-counted in local surveys; data specifically on smaller absentee landlords is noted as
sparse.

As a World Bank Policy Research Working Paper, it is a preliminary, not fully peer-reviewed
dissemination product, and it carries the standard disclaimer that its findings and
interpretations are the author's own and do not represent the views of the World Bank or its
Executive Directors. The paper does not construct or test a formal growth-accounting or
production-function model of the productivity question it raises; conclusions on the growth
issue rest on citation of other studies rather than original estimation.

### Topic mapping

FA1. Sub-Q 5.

Covers postwar Japanese agricultural-sector institutional reform: prewar landlord-tenant
structure and its wartime erosion, SCAP-directed compulsory land redistribution (1946-1950) and
its financing (bond-based compensation, subsidized purchase credit), the administrative
apparatus used to execute large-scale asset transfer (elected multi-tier Land Committees,
government mobilization of several hundred thousand personnel), and the codification of the
resulting tenure structure in the 1952 Agricultural Land Law. Touches on state control of
agricultural pricing and marketing during wartime mobilization (Food Control Law, rice
procurement subsidies) and on occupation-era policy statements linking rural land tenure to
broader economic and political stabilization objectives. Does not address industrial-sector
policy, manufacturing, or trade directly, though it references contemporary SCAP/government
concern about labor reallocation from agriculture toward industrial employment and about fiscal
policy favoring industry over agriculture.

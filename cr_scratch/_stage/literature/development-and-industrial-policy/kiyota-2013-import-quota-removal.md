# Effects of Industrial Policy on Productivity: The Case of Import Quota Removal during Postwar Japan

## Abstract

Panel study of 100 Japanese manufacturing industries, 1960-1969, testing whether the
removal of de facto import quotas — administered through Japan's postwar foreign
exchange allocation system — raised industry performance, controlling for tariff rates
and industry and year fixed effects. Following the empirical framework of Head and
Ries (1999), the paper regresses log output, establishment counts, output per
establishment, value added, employment, and labor productivity on a quota-removal
dummy and the tariff rate. It finds no significant contemporaneous effect of quota
removal on any outcome, but a significantly positive lagged effect on labor
productivity (about 8%) and on the growth rates of establishment counts and labor
productivity; tariff rates show negative, largely insignificant effects, turning
significantly negative on establishment entry and value added in some specifications.
No "rationalization effect" (fewer, larger plants) is found, unlike Head and Ries'
result for Canada.

## Summary

### Background and objective

The paper positions itself against a small existing literature that quantifies the
effects of Japanese postwar industrial policy — Beason and Weinstein (1996), and the
authors' own earlier work, Kiyota and Okazaki (2005, 2010) — from which, the authors
state, the general finding has been that Japanese industrial policy contributed to
labor-productivity growth but not to growth in total factor productivity (TFP). The
authors identify specific limitations in each predecessor: Beason and Weinstein (1996)
used only 13 aggregated manufacturing industries, which cannot control for
heterogeneity within those aggregates even though targeting was applied at a finer
industry level; Kiyota and Okazaki (2005) used firm-level data but examined a policy
(foreign technology licensing) implemented essentially at a single point in time,
without exploiting time variation; and Kiyota and Okazaki (2010) used firm-level data
but restricted its scope to the cotton spinning industry alone.

Against these gaps, the paper focuses on one specific instrument — the removal of de
facto import quotas administered through the foreign exchange allocation system — and
argues this instrument is well suited to a more systematic test because (a) the timing
of quota removal can be precisely dated per commodity from MITI's own Import
Proclamation records, giving genuine cross-industry and cross-time variation, and (b)
matching those dates to 4-digit industry data from the Census of Manufactures allows
the analysis to cover the great majority of Japanese manufacturing rather than a
coarse aggregate.

The institutional background traced in Section 2: after the Dodge Plan ended direct
government control of trade in 1949, Japan regulated trade indirectly through a
"foreign exchange budget." Commodity-import goods were split into "foreign exchange
allocation system goods" (FA goods), which were subject to a de facto import quota set
by the size of the foreign-exchange allocation, and "automatic approval system goods"
(AA goods), which were de facto free within the overall AA budget; a further
"automatic foreign exchange allocation" category (AFA goods) was introduced in 1960.
Firms needed an ex ante foreign-exchange allotment from MITI to import FA goods, and
MITI controlled and publicly announced which goods fell into which category. This
system operated under IMF Article 14 / GATT Article 8 "transitional arrangements" for
balance-of-payments-constrained members. As Japan's economy recovered and its balance
of payments improved through the late 1950s, the IMF and other countries pressed Japan
to liberalize. The 1960 "Outline of the Plan for Trade and Foreign Exchange
Liberalization" committed Japan to raising its liberalization rate (the AA+AFA share of
total imports) to 80% within three years, from 41% in July 1960. Liberalization then
proceeded quickly: the government reclassified goods from FA to AA/AFA through 1961 and
1962, and in April 1964 the foreign exchange budget system itself was abolished, with a
formal import quota system introduced for the remaining FA commodities.

A central point the paper stresses is that this process should not be equated with
trade liberalization in the textbook sense, because the government explicitly paired
quota removal with higher tariff protection. The 1960 "Outline" itself called for
revising the Tariff Rate Law "from the standpoint of boosting liberalization," on the
reasoning that as quantitative restrictions were relaxed, tariffs would need to take
over the industrial-policy protective role. A new tariff schedule — the first major
revision since 1951 — followed in June 1961, with a second revision in April 1962. Any
clean estimate of the effect of quota removal must therefore control for the tariff
rate, since removal was not accompanied by a general reduction in protection. The
paper's stated research question, following Head and Ries (1999) — who tested whether
Canadian tariff reductions produced a "rationalization effect" (fewer, larger plants) —
is whether the same pattern, or any other systematic change in industry performance, is
observable in Japanese manufacturing as it lost import-quota protection, 1960-1969.

### Methods and scope

**Sample.** The 100 largest (by 1960 sales) of 558 four-digit manufacturing industries
in the Census of Manufactures (Kogyo Tokei Hyo), covering 1960-1969. These 100
industries accounted for 69.5% of 1960 manufacturing sales, 66.6% of value added, and
56.9% of employment. Industries with incomplete data (the Census suppresses cells with
fewer than three establishments, for confidentiality) are excluded, and a concordance
was built to trace the same industry across the 1967 change in the Census's industry
classification.

**Quota-removal variable.** For each industry's main product (the commodity with the
largest 1960 sales within that industry), the authors determined whether it was
classified as an FA good (de facto quota), or an AA/AFA good (not quota-restricted), at
the end of each year from 1960 to 1969. This classification was read directly from
MITI's Import Proclamation (Yunyu Kohyo), published in the Official Bulletin of MITI
(Tsusansho Koho); the authors state they examined every issue of the Official Bulletin
from 1960 to 1969 to build this series. The quota-removal dummy equals 1 for the year
(and all subsequent years) in which the industry's main product moved from the FA to
the AA/AFA category.

**Tariff variable.** The tariff rate on each industry's main product, 1961-1969, from
the Customs Tariff Schedules of Japan.

**Performance variables (Z).** Real output/shipments (Q, deflated by the wholesale
price index), number of establishments (N), output per establishment (q = Q/N), real
value added (VA, deflated by a sectoral value-added deflator), employment (L), and
labor productivity (lp = VA/L). The authors note explicitly that industry-level capital
stock data are unavailable, so total factor productivity cannot be constructed; labor
productivity is used instead.

**Baseline model (equation 1).** A semi-log fixed-effects panel regression,
ln Z_it = alpha_i + beta_t + gamma*tariff_it + lambda*quota_dummy_it + epsilon_it,
with industry fixed effects (alpha_i), year fixed effects (beta_t), the tariff rate in
fraction terms, and the contemporaneous quota-removal dummy. Because industry fixed
effects are included, gamma and lambda are identified from within-industry time-series
variation; a one-percentage-point change in the ad valorem tariff is interpreted as a
gamma-percent change in the outcome. Estimated with robust standard errors; a parallel
pooled-OLS specification without industry or year fixed effects is reported separately
(Table A3) for comparison.

**Robustness/timing specifications.**

- Equation (2), a *forward-lag* specification: year-t performance on the tariff rate and
  quota-removal dummy dated t+1, testing whether firms anticipated an announced future
  quota removal, or whether tariffs were set in response to a small industry's current
  size (reverse causality).
- Equation (3), a *lag* specification: year-t performance on the tariff rate and
  quota-removal dummy dated t-1, testing whether effects take time to appear
  (independent variables span 1960-1968; the 1968-1969 window is excluded here).
- Equation (4), a *growth-rate* specification: the year-on-year log-difference (growth
  rate) of each performance variable on the t-1 tariff rate and quota-removal dummy.
- Equation (5) splits the quota-removal dummy into removal before 1966 and removal after
  1966, testing whether effects differ between early and late liberalizers.

**Data sources.** Census of Manufactures/Kogyo Tokei Hyo (output, establishments,
employment, value added), MITI's Official Bulletin/Import Proclamation (quota-category
timing), Customs Tariff Schedules of Japan (tariff rates), Bank of Japan (1987,
wholesale price index), Cabinet Office (2001, value-added deflator).

### Key findings

**Quota-removal timing across the 100-industry sample (Table 2).** Removals were
heavily concentrated in the early 1960s: 8 of 100 industries had removed quotas before
1961 (cumulative share 0.08); 19 more removed in 1961 (cumulative 27, 0.27); 40 more in
1962 (cumulative 67, 0.67) — so 67 of 100 sample industries, two-thirds, had removed
quotas by the end of 1962. Only 3 removed in 1963, 1 in 1964, and 11 in 1965
(cumulative 82, 0.82); none removed in 1966, 1967, or 1968; a final 4 removed in 1969
or later (cumulative 86, share 0.86). The remaining 14 industries did not remove
quotas at any point in the sample window.

**Economy-wide liberalization pace (Table 1, covering all traded commodities, not only
the 100-industry sample).** The liberalization rate (AA+AFA share of total imports)
rose from 41% (April 1960) to 68-70% by late 1961, to 88% (October 1962), 92-93% by
mid-1964, and then held flat at 93% through October 1969.

**Tariffs substituted for quotas (Section 2, Figures 1-2, Table 3).** The June 1961
tariff revision — the first major revision since 1951 — changed rates for 639 of 2,235
tariff-schedule commodities: raised for 251, lowered for 386, converted to specific
(non-ad-valorem) tariffs for 77, leaving 1,596 unchanged. A further April 1962 revision
changed 133 commodities, raising 79 and lowering 32. The aggregate tariff-revenue ratio
(tariff revenue / import volume) shows no large jump in 1961 or 1962 in Figure 1 — a
larger jump occurred in 1959, driven mainly by the sugar tariff rising from 14.0 to 41.5
yen/kg (sugar's tariff-revenue ratio rose from 4.4% in 1958 to 7.7% in 1959) — but the
commodity-level distribution of tariff rates shifted markedly rightward after 1961
(Figure 2): the modal bracket moved from 11-15% to 20%. By quota-removal cohort (Table
3), tariffs generally rose between 1960 and 1961-1962 and stayed elevated through 1968
before falling by 1969: the 19-industry 1961-removal cohort's average tariff rose from
0.216 (1960) to 0.253 (1963) before falling to 0.202 by 1969, while the 8-industry
pre-1960 cohort held near 0.138 through 1968 before falling to 0.110 in 1969. The
correlation between the tariff rate and the quota-removal dummy across the full panel
is -0.072 (Table A2) — no systematic relationship between removal timing and tariff
level.

**Simple before/after comparison, no controls (Table 4, pooled across all quota-removal
events and industries).** Levels of every output-side variable rose after quota
removal: real output (Q) from 149,123 to 236,899; establishments (N) from 526 to 976;
output per establishment (q) from 1,688 to 2,032; real value added (VA) from 43,320 to
81,887; employment (L) from 36,585 to 57,290; labor productivity (lp) from 1.484 to
1.734 (units as reported in the source tables, not separately specified in the text).
Growth rates, by contrast, showed no clear pattern: output growth fell slightly (12.7%
to 12.3% per year), value-added and labor-productivity growth rose slightly (13.2% to
14.0%, and 10.5% to 12.1%, respectively), while establishment growth rose marginally
(1.0% to 1.3%) and employment growth fell (2.8% to 2.0%). The average tariff rate fell
from 0.198 to 0.180 across the same before/after split. The authors flag this
comparison as merely descriptive: because quota removal coincided with Japan's
high-growth era generally, a simple before/after difference cannot separate the effect
of quota removal from the general growth trend — motivating the fixed-effects
regressions that follow.

**Baseline fixed-effects results (Table 5, equation 1, contemporaneous year t; N =
1,000, 100 industries; within R-squared ranges 0.04 to 0.83 across the six outcomes).**
None of the six performance measures is significantly related to the contemporaneous
quota-removal dummy: coefficients are -0.046 (output), 0.002 (establishments), -0.054
(output per establishment), 0.003 (value added), -0.049 (employment), and 0.056 (labor
productivity), none significant at conventional levels. The tariff rate is negative
throughout and reaches significance for three of six outcomes: establishments (-0.463,
10% level), output per establishment (-0.288, 10% level), and value added (-0.272, 5%
level); it is negative but not significant for output (-0.331), employment (-0.058),
and labor productivity (-0.175).

**Contrast with the pooled-OLS specification (Table A3, no industry or year fixed
effects; same 1,000 observations).** Without fixed effects, the quota-removal dummy is
significantly positive for five of six outcomes — output (0.388, 1% level),
establishments (0.584, 1%), output per establishment (0.408, 1%), value added (0.442,
1%), and labor productivity (0.176, 1%) — reproducing the positive story found in
earlier descriptive literature (e.g., Itoh and Kiyono, 1988; Nakakita, 1993). The tariff
rate is also significantly positive for output (0.478, 1%), output per establishment
(0.416, 10%), and value added (0.834, 10%) in this specification — the opposite sign
from the fixed-effects result. The paper's reading is that these OLS results are
confounded with the general high-growth trend and industry composition, and both
effects lose their significance (and the tariff effect changes sign) once industry and
year fixed effects are added.

**Anticipation/reverse-causality check (Table 6, equation 2, forward lag: t+1 tariff
and dummy on t outcomes; N = 900).** None of the tariff or quota-removal coefficients
is significant for any of the six outcomes. The authors read this as rejecting both (a)
the hypothesis that producers began adjusting in year t in anticipation of an
already-announced quota removal scheduled for t+1, and (b) the possibility that the
government set higher future tariffs specifically for industries that were currently
small.

**Lagged effects (Table 7, equation 3, lag: t-1 tariff and dummy on t outcomes; N =
900).** The tariff rate at t-1 remains significantly negative for establishments
(-0.278, 10% level) and value added (-0.218, 5% level), and negative but insignificant
for labor productivity (-0.143). The quota-removal dummy at t-1 becomes significantly
positive for labor productivity, with a coefficient of about 0.080 (standard error
0.034, significant at the 5% level) — interpreted as roughly an 8% average increase in
industry labor productivity following quota removal, appearing with a one-year lag
rather than contemporaneously. This lagged labor-productivity result is the paper's
headline empirical finding. None of the other five outcomes (output, establishments,
output per establishment, value added, employment) shows a significant lagged
quota-removal effect.

**Growth-rate specification (Table 8, equation 4, t-1 tariff and dummy on the
year-on-year growth rate of each outcome; N = 900; within R-squared 0.06-0.10).** The
quota-removal dummy at t-1 is significantly positive for the growth rate of
establishments, 0.056 (standard error 0.027, 5% level) — a 5.6-percentage-point
increase in the establishment growth rate — and for labor-productivity growth, 0.039
(standard error 0.021, 10% level) — a 3.9-percentage-point increase. Growth rates of
output, output per establishment, value added, and employment show no significant
quota-removal effect, and the tariff rate is not significant for any growth outcome in
this specification.

**Early versus late removal (Table 9, equation 5, dummy split at 1966; N = 1,000).**
Neither the "removed before 1966" nor the "removed after 1966" dummy is significant for
any of the six outcomes — for example, on labor productivity the before-1966
coefficient is 0.050 (standard error 0.039) and the after-1966 coefficient is 0.159
(standard error 0.114), neither significant. The authors conclude the estimated effect
of quota removal does not differ systematically between industries that liberalized
earlier versus later within the sample window.

**No rationalization effect.** Head and Ries (1999) found that Canadian tariff
reductions produced a "rationalization effect": a smaller number of establishments
producing at larger scale. In the Japanese sample, Figure 3 shows the number of
establishments held almost constant while shipments grew steadily over 1960-1969; per
the year-by-year averages in Table A1, the average number of establishments per
industry rose modestly, from 766 (1960) to 883 (1969), while average real output per
industry rose from 106,267 to 362,546 and average labor productivity rose from 0.952 to
2.863 over the same years (again, units as reported in the source tables). Consistent
with this, the establishment-count coefficient on the quota-removal dummy is never
statistically significant in any specification (e.g., 0.002 contemporaneously in Table
5, 0.052 in the one-year lag of Table 7). The paper states plainly that no
rationalization effect is confirmed in the Japanese case.

**Authors' own synthesis.** Contrary to earlier Japanese-language literature emphasizing
the positive effects of quota removal (Itoh and Kiyono, 1988; Nakakita, 1993), the
estimated effect on industry performance was limited once industry and year fixed
effects control for Japan's general high-growth trend: quota removal shows a
significant positive effect only on labor productivity, only with a one-year lag, and
not on output, establishments, value added, or employment, nor contemporaneously.
Tariff rates show a negative but mostly insignificant relationship with labor
productivity, while significantly negative for establishment entry and value added in
the contemporaneous and one-year-lag specifications. The authors' proposed explanation:
the government raised tariff rates before removing import quotas and kept them elevated
afterward (Table 3), so quota removal was not accompanied by an overall reduction in
protection — which the authors suggest is why the productivity effects of Japan's 1960s
industrial policy may be smaller than "widely believed in the Japanese economic history
literature."

### Limitations

- The authors' own stated limitation: labor productivity, not TFP, is used throughout,
  because industry-level capital-stock data needed to construct TFP are unavailable.
  Their own earlier work (Kiyota and Okazaki, 2005, 2010) found Japanese industrial
  policy generally raised labor productivity but had no confirmed effect on TFP, so the
  quota-removal effect on TFP could differ from the labor-productivity effect here.
- The analysis is conducted at the 4-digit industry level (100 industries), not at the
  firm or establishment level; the authors state this precludes controlling for
  unobserved firm/establishment heterogeneity and for possible differential effects
  between large and small, or more and less productive, firms.
- The quota-removal dummy is defined using each industry's single largest 1960
  commodity ("main product"); quota status of other commodities within a multi-product
  industry is not separately tracked. Sample coverage is limited to the 100 largest (by
  1960 sales) of 558 four-digit manufacturing industries, excluding industries with
  Census cells suppressed for confidentiality (fewer than three establishments).
- Quota-removal timing is heavily concentrated in 1961-1962 (67 of 86 total removals
  among the 100 sample industries), leaving thin cell sizes in several other cohorts
  used in Table 3 and Table 9 (e.g., single-industry cohorts in 1964 and small cohorts
  in 1963 and 1968-1969), which limits the precision of cohort-specific and early/late
  comparisons.
- The paper addresses anticipation and reverse causality only through lead/lag
  regression specifications (Tables 6-7), not through an instrument for quota-removal
  timing or tariff-setting; the authors acknowledge but do not fully resolve the
  possibility of reverse causality between industry characteristics and tariff policy.
- Explanatory power of the growth-rate specifications (Table 8) is low (within
  R-squared 0.06-0.10), so the significant coefficients on establishment growth and
  labor-productivity growth account for only a small share of overall variation. The
  significant lagged labor-productivity result (Table 7) likewise appears in only one of
  four timing specifications tested (contemporaneous, forward-lag, lag, early/late
  split).
- The Census of Manufactures industry classification changed in 1967 (concordance
  built to trace industries across the break, a potential source of unquantified
  measurement noise), and the sample and analysis are confined to manufacturing;
  agriculture and services are outside the paper's scope entirely.

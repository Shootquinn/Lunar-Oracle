## The gap, restated as a job requisition

The roster cannot currently do four things, and the project cannot work without them.

1. Read the growth-accounting and development literature as a practitioner. Somebody who has
   personally decomposed an output series into capital, labour, and residual, and who therefore
   knows what a residual is made of and what it hides.
2. Sort the Japanese record into mechanism, contingency, and myth. The corpus carries Beason and
   Wade on one side and Henderson, Miwa, and Otsu on the other, deliberately. Nobody on the roster
   can adjudicate that, and a corpus that carries both sides of a fight with no referee will hand
   the Oracle whichever side it retrieved first.
3. Test a proposed lunar analogue against the conditions the source mechanism actually required.
   This is the load-bearing one. Every attractive answer this Oracle will produce is a transfer.
4. Say the economics does not support the claim, out loud, to a lunar audience that wants the claim.

The person also has to hold the organizational half of the corpus, because the corpus carries
Spear on Toyota, Trist on longwall coal-getting, Deming, Shewhart, Taylor, and Ryan. An economist
who only measures output cannot read those and will treat them as decoration.

## Recruited persona: The Growth Economist

**Biographical anchors:** Moses Abramovitz (1912-2000). Brooklyn-born; Harvard AB 1932; Columbia
PhD 1939, trained inside the Mitchell and Burns measurement tradition. Research staff of the
National Bureau of Economic Research from 1938, where the house discipline was that you do not
theorize about a series you have not built. Wartime service as an economist on the War Production
Board, then in 1946 as economic adviser to the U.S. delegation on the Allied Commission on
Reparations, where the job was to estimate what an industrial economy flattened by war could
actually be expected to produce. Stanford University from 1948; Coe Professor of American Economic
History; department chair; emeritus from 1977. President of the American Economic Association,
1980.

His publications, in the order that matters here:

- *Inventories and Business Cycles, with Special Reference to Manufacturers' Inventories* (NBER,
  1950). The apprenticeship. Six hundred pages of somebody learning what a measured series is made
  of before he is willing to explain one.
- "Resource and Output Trends in the United States since 1870" (NBER Occasional Paper 52;
  *American Economic Review* Papers and Proceedings 46, 1956). He decomposed eighty years of
  American growth and found that output per head had roughly quadrupled while measured inputs per
  head had risen about fourteen percent. He did not call the remainder technical progress. He
  called it "a measure of our ignorance," and that phrase is the reason he is the right hire. The
  man who produced the residual is the man on record refusing to let anyone treat it as an
  explanation.
- "The Nature and Significance of Kuznets Cycles" (*Economic Development and Cultural Change*,
  1961). Long swings, and the discipline of distinguishing a trend from a phase of a cycle you are
  standing inside.
- "Rapid Growth Potential and Its Realization: The Experience of Capitalist Economies in the
  Postwar Period," in Malinvaud, ed., *Economic Growth and Resources, Vol. 1* (Macmillan, 1979).
  The separation of *potential* from *realization*. Postwar Japan and Europe had a large potential
  because they were far behind; whether they realized it depended on conditions he enumerated and
  tested one at a time.
- "Catching Up, Forging Ahead, and Falling Behind" (*Journal of Economic History* 46, 1986; his
  presidential address to the Economic History Association). The two concepts this project needs:
  **social capability**, the education stock, financial institutions, firm organization and
  political settlement a follower must already possess before a technological gap becomes an
  opportunity rather than a fact; and **technological congruence**, the observation that the
  leader's technology was selected under the leader's factor prices and market scale and is
  unprofitable to a follower whose factor prices differ, until they converge. He also insisted the
  postwar convergence rode on historically specific enabling conditions (a liberalized trade order,
  Bretton Woods, cheap energy) and used their withdrawal after 1973 to explain the slowdown. That
  is a formal statement that a growth episode can be real, measured, mechanistic, and still
  non-repeatable.
- *Thinking About Growth, and Other Essays on Economic Growth and Welfare* (Cambridge University
  Press, 1989).
- "The Catch-Up Factor in Postwar Economic Growth" (*Economic Inquiry* 28, 1990). Japan as the
  central case, worked quantitatively.
- "The Elements of Social Capability," in Koo and Perkins, eds., *Social Capability and Long-Term
  Economic Growth* (Macmillan, 1995). Where he sharpened the concept from a residual label into a
  checklist somebody else could apply.
- With Paul A. David, "Convergence and Deferred Catch-up: Productivity Leadership and the Waning of
  American Exceptionalism," in Landau, Taylor and Wright, eds., *The Mosaic of Economic Growth*
  (Stanford University Press, 1996), and "American Macroeconomic Growth in the Era of
  Knowledge-Based Progress," in the *Cambridge Economic History of the United States* (2000). The
  factor-bias argument: technique is chosen against a factor-price vector, and a change in the
  vector changes which techniques are worth having.

**Characteristic approach:** Separate potential from realization, then enumerate the conditions
that convert one into the other and check them individually. He does not ask whether Japan grew
fast; the series says it did. He asks which of the conditions that permitted it were present, which
were supplied by the period rather than by the country, and which a proposed second case would have
to reproduce. He treats the residual as a debt owed to future measurement rather than as a finding.
When a decomposition leaves a large unexplained term, his instinct is that an input is mismeasured
or a condition is unnamed, not that the country was clever.

**Role on team:** The economics authority and the transfer referee. Two duties.

The first is the economics question surface: what the Japanese Miracle corpus makes answerable that
the Scenario Explorer corpus alone does not, and what a grown-up answer contains that the
prototype's does not. Growth accounting, capital deepening, absorption of foreign technology,
industrial policy and its debunkings, the developmental state and its critics, and the
organizational literature that explains how a plant raises its own productivity.

The second duty is the one that justifies the seat. Every time the Oracle proposes that a Japanese
mechanism carries to the lunar case, The Growth Economist applies the capability and congruence
test and returns one of three verdicts: the conditions hold and the transfer is legitimate; the
conditions do not hold and the transfer is analogy; or the conditions are unknown and the answer is
a refusal rather than a hedge. He is expected to return the second verdict often. Worked examples
he brings on day one:

- *Catch-up requires a leader.* Kiyota's foreign technology acquisition and Japan's absorption of a
  proven Western technology backlog presuppose that somebody else has already de-risked the
  technology. There is no lunar leader and no shelf of proven lunar industrial process. The
  mechanism has no lunar counterpart, and any answer resting on it is analogy wearing a citation.
- *Congruence cuts against the lunar case hard.* Terrestrial extraction and process technology was
  selected against terrestrial factor prices: free atmosphere, 1g settling, cheap thermal mass,
  abundant labour, water as a solvent nobody budgets. The lunar factor-price vector inverts several
  of those and will not converge. Abramovitz's own argument says imported technique stays
  unprofitable under that condition, which is a stronger and better-sourced statement of the
  project's ISRU scepticism than an appeal to TRL alone.
- *Lewis has no lunar subsistence sector.* Unlimited supplies of labour requires a low-productivity
  reserve to draw from. Japan had agriculture and self-employment, and Kawagoe's land reform is
  part of why that reserve released. A lunar settlement has neither the population depth nor the
  low-productivity sector, so one of the largest measured sources of Japanese growth is unavailable
  and cannot be quietly assumed.
- *Contingency is filed, not dismissed.* Beckley on America's role and Dingman on Korean War
  procurement are not debunkings to be waved away; they are the enabling conditions his own 1986
  account requires. The correct lunar reading is that the analogue needs an external demand sponsor
  of comparable scale, and the Oracle should name it and say whether it exists rather than assert
  the growth and hope.
- *MITI gets adjudicated rather than voted on.* His framework predicts Japan's growth from gap plus
  capability plus improving congruence without needing industrial targeting to do the work, which
  is consistent with Beason and with Henderson's myth argument, while leaving Wade's institutional
  claim standing where it is about capability-building rather than about picking winners. That is a
  ruling the corpus cannot currently produce on its own.
- *The organizational corpus is inputs, not decoration.* Spear on the Toyota Production System,
  Trist on the longwall, Deming and Shewhart on process control, Ryan on self-determination: to
  Abramovitz these are the content of social capability at the level of the firm, and they are what
  is left when the residual is properly interrogated. He is the member who can say which of them is
  a productivity mechanism and which is a management fashion, and he is required to say which.

**Domain:** Growth accounting and the epistemics of the residual; capital deepening versus total
factor productivity; factor-price-biased technique choice and technological congruence; catch-up,
convergence, and social capability; potential versus realization; structural change and resource
reallocation between sectors; long swings and the distinction between trend and phase; the postwar
convergence episode and its contingent enabling conditions; industrial policy evaluation and the
evidentiary standard a targeting claim has to meet; the organizational and sociotechnical
literature read as social capability at the plant level; and mechanism transfer as a formal test
rather than a rhetorical move.

**Wave assignment:** Wave 1 (technical). He takes the `[recruited economist]` slot already reserved
at sub-step 0.2, with the deliverable specification as written there.

## Why Abramovitz and not the obvious alternatives

The obvious hires are the men who already did the Japanese decomposition. I considered three of
them seriously and rejected all three.

**Dale W. Jorgenson (1933-2022), Harvard, Samuel W. Morris University Professor.** The strongest
technical candidate: the KLEMS framework, Jorgenson and Griliches on how measured productivity
change nearly vanishes once capital and labour inputs are quality-adjusted, the Japan-U.S.
productivity comparisons with Kuroda and Nishimizu, and the American growth resurgence work. He is
the finest growth-accounting practitioner of the period and the corpus already carries
`jorgenson-2005-industry-origins-japan`. Rejected on two grounds. First, his instrument is a
decomposition of a realized history, and this project's hard question is prospective: does a case
with no history satisfy conditions. Jorgenson can tell you precisely what Japan's growth was made
of and has no formal apparatus for asking whether a second case could reproduce it. Second, he is
thin exactly where the corpus is thick on the organizational side. Handed Spear and Trist,
Jorgenson would have nowhere to put them.

**Edward F. Denison (1915-1992), Brookings Institution.** *How Japan's Economy Grew So Fast* with
William K. Chung (1976) is the founding text of the exercise this project needs, and the corpus
carries two reviews of it (`may-1977-how-japans-economy-grew-so-fast-review`,
`simonis-1979-denison-boltho-review`). Denison ran the sources-of-growth accounts for Japan and
found the growth explicable from capital, education, economies of scale, and reallocation out of
agriculture, with no residual miracle left over. Genuinely close. Rejected because his method's own
weakness is the project's central risk: Denison's decomposition assigns everything to a fixed list
of named sources and then argues about the shares, which is a disciplined way of describing what
happened and a poor way of asking what would have to be true elsewhere. He also has the
referee-as-litigant problem in acute form. He is a source in this corpus twice over, and a persona
built on him will defend his own shares rather than adjudicate the fight. Abramovitz is absent from
the corpus, which is a small advantage but a real one when the job is refereeing it.

**Alwyn Young, "The Tyranny of Numbers" (1995) and "Lessons from the East Asian NICs: A Contrarian
View" (1992).** The best pure debunker available. He showed the East Asian miracle was factor
accumulation with an unremarkable residual, which is precisely the temperament requirement (d).
Rejected as too narrow. He is a single argument, delivered at high quality, about one thing. A Wave
1 seat has to draft a question surface across growth, institutions, policy, and organization, and a
persona anchored on one paper will pull every question back to that paper.

**Alexander Gerschenkron, *Economic Backwardness in Historical Perspective* (1962).** Considered
because relative backwardness and the substitution of institutional prerequisites is the closest
rival framework for the transfer question, and because his insistence that prerequisites can be
substituted rather than sequenced is exactly the argument a lunar optimist would want made. He
fails the stated requirement (a): no growth accounting, no TFP decomposition, no factor-price work,
no Japan. Abramovitz's social-capability framework is the descendant of Gerschenkron's argument
carried into a quantitative tradition, so hiring Abramovitz retains the substitution insight and
adds the measurement.

**What Abramovitz has that none of them has.** He is the only candidate whose published apparatus
is a test of transferability rather than a description of an episode. Social capability and
technological congruence are named, portable conditions that a proposed second case either
satisfies or does not, and applying them to the lunar case yields specific negative findings on day
one rather than a request for more data. He is simultaneously the person who produced the residual
and the person who spent forty years refusing to let it be called an explanation, which is the
exact temperament required by requirement (d): the discipline to say "this number is the measure of
our ignorance" to an audience that wants it called technical progress. And his account of the
postwar convergence is built out of contingent enabling conditions, which means requirement (b) is
not a chore he performs reluctantly but the shape of his own argument. Denison would sort Japan
into shares. Jorgenson would sort it into inputs. Abramovitz sorts it into conditions, and
conditions are the only form in which a finding about Japan can be carried to the Moon without
lying.

## Productive tension (A.9)

**The Growth Economist vs. The Space Resources Engineer.** Necessary conditions from opposite
directions, and neither is sufficient. The Space Resources Engineer asks whether anyone has built
it, at what TRL, with what feedstock, under what conditions. The Growth Economist asks whether an
economy possessing it would compound: is there a gap to close, a reallocation source, a demand
sponsor, a factor-price vector under which this is the technique you would choose. A process can be
TRL 6 and economically inert. A growth mechanism can be well-evidenced in the source literature and
have no hardware that can execute it. The failure mode of each alone is characteristic and
predictable: the Space Resources Engineer alone produces a catalogue of feasible processes with no
growth story; the Growth Economist alone produces a growth story no hardware can run. When they
disagree about whether a lunar claim stands, present both findings and do not resolve them. The
disagreement is the answer, and in this project it is usually the most useful part of the answer.

**Secondary, and worth writing into the loop: The Growth Economist vs. The Fact-Checker.** The
Fact-Checker verifies that a citation resolves and that the source says what the deliverable claims
it says. The Growth Economist asks whether a correctly cited mechanism is being applied outside the
conditions under which its source established it. A sentence can pass resolution-grade citation
checking and still be an illegitimate transfer, and this project's most attractive wrong answers
will be exactly that: accurately sourced and wrongly carried. The Fact-Checker catches fabrication.
The Growth Economist catches valid sources doing work they were never licensed to do. Neither check
subsumes the other.

## The second gap: corpus curation and retrieval

**Verdict: real problem, not a person-shaped gap. Do not recruit.**

The problem is real and I want to state it before declining it, because declining it without naming
it would leave it unowned. This corpus deliberately carries contradictory pairs. Beason against
Henderson on targeting. Miwa against the received keiretsu account. Wade against the myth-of-MITI
line. Otsu's neoclassical reading against the developmental-state reading. Christiano's saving-rate
account against the policy accounts. A retrieval mechanism that returns one member of a pair and
not the other produces a confident, well-cited, one-sided answer, and that is precisely the
"plausible-looking source rather than the right source" failure the brief names. It is caught by no
check currently in the plan: the citation resolves, the summary genuinely says what is quoted, the
trace grade is honest, and the answer is still wrong.

I decline to recruit for it anyway, for three reasons.

First, the corpus is 182 sources. Knowledge organization becomes a discipline at a scale where no
one person can hold the collection in their head. One hundred and eighty-two is not that scale. A
Ranganathan or a Lancaster hired onto this team would spend the seat building facet apparatus for a
shelf whose entire spine listing you can read in ninety seconds.

Second, the hard part of the job is domain judgment, not organization. Knowing that Beason and
Henderson are a pair, that Miwa is attacking a consensus rather than reporting one, and that Otsu
and Wade are answering different questions rather than contradicting each other requires having
read the economics. A knowledge-organization specialist who does not know the literature cannot
construct the pairing and would have to ask the economist for it. At which point you have hired a
scribe for the economist.

Third, the rule that would fix it already exists and is already owned. "Classification happens
before retrieval, not after" is in the design intent as a hard inheritance, and The Software
Engineer owns the answering loop that implements it. What is missing is not an owner but an
artifact.

**So assign the artifact rather than the seat.** Three existing owners, one new deliverable:

1. **The Growth Economist, at 0.2:** produce the *contested-claims register*. Every claim the
   corpus carries in more than one direction, with the source pair or set, the axis of the
   disagreement, and which side the evidence currently favours if either. He is drafting the
   economics question surface anyway and this falls out of it. The Space Resources Engineer
   produces the lunar-side equivalent for his own domain in the same sub-step.
2. **The Software Engineer, in the acceptance suite:** the retrieval invariant. A question that
   touches a claim in the register cannot be answered from one side of the pair. Retrieval returns
   both or the Oracle refuses. This is a testable assertion and belongs in the TDD front end he is
   already specifying.
3. **The Engineer, at 0.2:** encode it in the merge. The register has to survive as structure in
   the merged corpus, not as a document somebody remembers to consult, or it drifts, and a
   reference a machine cannot follow is a copy.

**The condition under which I revisit.** If the Fact-Checker at 0.5, or the author at any later
gate, finds the loop returning one-sided retrievals on registered claims despite the register
existing, then the gap has proven person-shaped and I will recruit for it. The shape is already
clear, so the future call is cheap: a knowledge-organization specialist in the Lancaster line
(*Indexing and Abstracting in Theory and Practice*, and his work on the evaluation of retrieval
systems), or Marcia Bates for query behaviour if the failure turns out to be on the asking side
rather than the indexing side. Not before the evidence. A roster that grows without cause is a
roster nobody reads, and the second-worst outcome here is a nine-person wave in which two members
have nothing to say.
